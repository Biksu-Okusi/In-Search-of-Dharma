# 0.1 Life & Formation

> ◉ **Public edition.** This note is published in a redacted public edition. Personal contact details, private third-party specifics, and passages resting on the author's private records are excluded, and each exclusion is disclosed where it is made. The private sources themselves (correspondence, family records, the mail archive) are held by the author and are not part of the public repository.

**Question:** Trace the author's biographical and intellectual formation: Perth / Western-Australia origins and the geography that orients him toward maritime SE Asia (Perth is closer to Jakarta than to Sydney); 1990s intensive Indonesian language and cultural studies at Australian and Indonesian universities; 1996 migration to Indonesia and subsequent Indonesian citizenship; the advanced degree in Indonesian anthropology; and present life in Bali as a programmer and knowledgebase-builder running the Okusi Group.

## Synthesis

The author, Gary Robert Dean (dharma name Biksu Okusi — given him by Indonesian colleagues, not self-chosen; see the final Finding) [S20][S29], was born in 1957 and raised in Perth, Western Australia — the world's most isolated major city, which sits ~300 km closer to Jakarta than to Sydney, a geography that orients its inhabitants across the Indian Ocean toward maritime Southeast Asia rather than the Australian east [S1][S11][S13]. Before the Indonesian turn, though, came three decades in Australia that the shared corpus barely records: a working-class childhood in suburban Perth and a restless adolescence (he left school and home young and drifted around the continent for years), a political apprenticeship in the Communist Party and then the anarchist movement (the arc traced in [[0.2-politics-and-anarchism]]), and a decade as a self-taught programmer building the Australian Labor Party's computerised polling and campaign systems — the technical grounding that resurfaces, much later, in the knowledgebase work [S27]. The Indian-Ocean orientation eventually became a vocation: a Bachelor of Asian Studies at Murdoch University (1996–2000) including an in-country year at Universitas Gadjah Mada in Yogyakarta, migration to Indonesia in 1996, eventual Indonesian citizenship, and a working life there — first in business and journalism, now as a programmer in Bali running the Okusi Group and building the very RAG knowledgebases this essay series draws on [S1][S11][S12][S14]. The intellectual thread is the convergence the author calls "precisely my thesis": that dharmas and mutual aid are both evolved cultural technologies by which a particular cooperative primate learns to live well together, read through human behavioural biology rather than metaphysics [S2]. Crucially, almost every biographical claim here is **self-reported** — the author's own public site, his own corpus contributions, and a private email — with only the Perth-isolation geography and the institutional ACICIS pathway corroborated by strictly third-party sources [S13][S15][S16]. The one substantive tension the research surfaced, the form of his degree, resolves to a documented *Bachelor of Asian Studies* (with "Indonesian anthropology" and "sociology" being the author's looser glosses), and is flagged rather than asserted beyond the evidence [S4][S5][S11][S17].

## Findings

### Perth origins and the Indian-Ocean orientation
The author states he was "born and raised in Perth, Western Australia, in the pre-Sputnik era," in "a mono-cultural English/British suburban environment" [S11] — "pre-Sputnik" placing his birth before October 1957, consistent with the 1970s letterpress-printing and Communist Party activity he recounts elsewhere [S1]. The geographic argument he builds on this origin — that "my home town, Perth, the most isolated city in the world, is closer to Jakarta (3000 km) than it is to Sydney (3300 km)" [S1] — is the one part of the biography corroborated by a fully independent source. Wikipedia describes Perth as "one of the most isolated major cities in the world" and records the distances from Perth as 3,000 km to Jakarta and 3,300 km to Sydney, noting that Perth is "geographically closer to both Timor-Leste … and Jakarta, Indonesia … than to Sydney" — i.e. roughly 300 km nearer Jakarta, matching the author's figures to the kilometre [S13]. Perth sits on the Indian-Ocean edge of the continent, facing the maritime Southeast-Asian region [S13]. The "so what" the author draws is biographical-as-geographic: an upbringing on Australia's western rim points naturally north toward island Asia, not east toward Sydney [S1].

### Early life, milieu, and the departures
The author was born in 1957 and raised in **Embleton**, a low-cost working-class suburb in the northern reaches of Perth — the "mono-cultural English/British suburban" world he describes, a city that "considered itself a bastion of the British Empire," where cinema audiences still stood for *God Save the Queen* [S24][S27]. His father was a metalworker and his mother a secretary (shorthand and typing); the neighbours and schoolmates came from the same industrial-labouring and tradesman stock, the women largely keeping house, and Labor and the union Left were simply "the normal" of that world [S27]. His closest schoolfriend was **Kim Salmon** — later a noted Perth musician, a founder of The Scientists and widely credited as a progenitor of grunge — whose father, a union secretary, went on to become an industrial commissioner in the Western Australian industrial courts [S27][S28]. Salmon, born the same year as the author, is independently documented as a Perth punk and garage-rock pioneer and a 2004 inductee of the WAM Hall of Fame: the one externally checkable anchor in an otherwise wholly self-reported childhood [S28].

The author asks that this childhood not be remembered only as bleak, and the correction matters to him. Set against the alienation of his published memoir is a real counterweight: parts of that childhood were "absolutely wonderful," carrying an "incredible freedom to roam" across bushland and wetlands — an unsupervised liberty he sees as all but destroyed since by "hyper-urbanisation and the atomisation of societies," and which he wishes his own children could have known [S27]. The darker register is nonetheless genuine and his own: by his *Escape at 14* memoir he was an unhappy adolescent — "depressed, alienated and bored beyond belief; a beacon for bullies for thinking differently, and by nature a little asocial" — drawn to the distant counterculture (Woodstock, Haight-Ashbury) as "solace, hope and identity" [S24]. Part of that "thinking differently," by his own later account, was already political: he considered himself a communist from the age of thirteen and paid for it socially throughout high school, well before any formal Party membership (the political arc is [[0.2-politics-and-anarchism]]) [S27]. He self-identifies as neurodivergent — an "Aspie" (autism spectrum), INFJ — folding it into the same lifelong sense of "thinking differently," and names a characteristically mixed set of role models: Robert Sapolsky, Gotama, Germaine Greer, and the rustic film character Pa Kettle [S20][S23].

The leaving came in stages. A first attempt at fourteen turned back: he packed flour, tea and his guitar and hitch-hiked east out of Perth toward the Eastern States, only to turn around at the edge of the Nullarbor near Kalgoorlie — "I started hitching. West. No other direction was possible" — an episode he casts as a formative awakening ("I could never be the same again") [S24]. The real departure followed within a year or two (the memoir dates the "escape for good" to "a little over two years later"): at fifteen he left school and, by his own account, was more or less forced out of home, then was recruited into a band that carried him out of Western Australia — a free, one-way train ticket east across the Nullarbor to Sydney [S24][S27]. When the band fell apart after some four months he was left penniless, and for roughly the next five years he drifted around Australia with a backpack and a single change of clothes — no unemployment benefit existed then, and he was, in his own words, "pretty much unemployable (still am!)" — eventually passing through every Australian state, "not by design, but that's just the way I drifted" [S27]. The most far-flung episode of those years came at eighteen: living in a Salvation Army hostel in Brisbane, he saved a little dole money for a one-way ticket to the **New Hebrides** (now Vanuatu, then an Anglo-French condominium), talked his way past immigration without a return ticket, and spent two contented weeks in a tent in a village he calls Pango-Pango — until the French administration tired of him and deported him back to Brisbane at its own expense [S27]. The restless, anti-authoritarian temperament these years laid down is the one the political arc in [[0.2-politics-and-anarchism]] picks up on his return to Perth in 1977 [S27].

### From ruminant rations to campaign systems: the Australian computing decade
A whole working life in Australia precedes the Indonesian turn, and it explains where a philosopher's late-life facility with code came from. Returning to Perth in 1977, the author threw himself first into the political activism that is the subject of [[0.2-politics-and-anarchism]]; alongside and after it he trained in an entirely different field, taking an Associate Diploma of Agricultural Technology (1983–84) and certificates in animal technology and nursing (1981–82), with a focus on ruminant husbandry [S20]. It was there that the computing began: in the early 1980s he started using the "new-fangled computers" of the day to devise least-cost feed-ration formulations for ruminants — a practical first encounter with the machine that grew into a vocation [S27]. A major Australian Labor Party benefactor (a relative by marriage) then recruited him as a programmer into an ALP front company, **East West Insight**, to build computerised electorate-management, polling and campaign systems for the party at state and federal level; by his account he did so with conspicuous success, spending the better part of a decade more or less continuously on the campaign trail [S27]. He dates his computing identity precisely: an early adopter from around 1981, a systems engineer with the ALP operation by 1985, his first FidoNet point established in 1989, and his first internet accounts obtained — "surreptitiously" — through the local universities; for a time, while studying Indonesian, he ran a side business installing internet connections in private homes, a genuinely technical job in an era when Windows was ill-equipped for it [S27]. Much of this period, he notes ruefully, is poorly documented for the simple reason that the consumer internet did not yet exist to record it [S27].

It was during the campaign decade that the Asian thread reasserted itself. Developing an interest in East Asian economy and politics, he visited Malaysia, where he met the Malaysian partner who returned with him to Australia and later joined the same ALP company as a data-entry operator for polling data [S27]. Around 1991 the pace caught up with him: burned out after years of high-pressure campaigning, he suffered what he calls a "mental breakdown," with severe and frequent panic attacks continuing for at least two years and forcing him to work from home over one of the earliest such arrangements — "telecommuting," in the language of the day [S27]. By 1994 he had withdrawn from full-time work at East West Insight to study Indonesian intensively, and by the end of 1996 he was in Indonesia for good; the institutional detail of that study — the Perth diploma, the Murdoch degree, the in-country year at Gadjah Mada — is traced in the next section [S27]. The arc from least-cost ruminant rations to a RAG knowledgebase of human ethics is a long one, but it is continuous: the same self-taught facility with data and machines runs through all of it.

### 1990s Indonesian language and cultural study (Murdoch + Gadjah Mada)
The author's intensive Indonesian study in the 1990s — "intensive indonesian language and cultural studies at australian and indonesian universities" in his own framing [S1] — is documented on his site as two concrete records: a **Diploma of Social Science (Indonesian)** at the Central Institute of Technology, Perth (1995–1996), and a **Bachelor of Asian Studies at Murdoch University, Perth (1996–2000)** that *included* "a year at Universitas Gadjah Mada, Yogyakarta" [S11][S12]. His UGM student ID is recorded as 96/111909/EK/13500, and he notes he "lived in Yogyakarta in 1998 during Indonesia's reformasi period" [S12].

The *institutional vehicle* by which a 1990s Murdoch student of Indonesian reached UGM in-country is independently corroborated and forms a notable crossover with the corpus. The **Australian Consortium for In-Country Indonesian Studies (ACICIS)** was founded in 1994 by Professor David Hill AM at Murdoch University to remove the academic and bureaucratic barriers that had previously stopped Australian students taking credited semester study at Indonesian universities; its initial programs ran at Gadjah Mada University (Yogyakarta) and Muhammadiyah University (Malang) [S15]. A secondary account adds that at Murdoch "Paul Stange and David Hill undertook the planning and negotiations," with the first cohort of roughly twenty Australian students arriving mid-1995 on the semester-at-UGM model [S16]. The crossover worth flagging: Paul Stange is the *same* scholar whose writings on Javanese Sumarah mysticism appear in the shared corpus [S9][S16] — so the Murdoch Indonesian-studies milieu the author's own record says he passed through is the same milieu that produced the corpus's Sumarah sources. The ACICIS link is *circumstantial*: no source names the author within an ACICIS cohort, but it explains how a Murdoch BAsianSt student of exactly that era reached UGM, which his own record states he did [S12][S15].

### Migration (1996) and Indonesian citizenship
The author's site states plainly: "Emigrated to Indonesia in 1996, and I am an Indonesian citizen" [S11], corroborating the SEED's "my migration to Indonesia in 1996 (btw, i am now an indonesian citizen)" [S1]. The corpus partially supports the migration date from a different angle: a 2016 self-introduction has him writing "I am originally from Australia, and migrated to Indonesia 20 years ago," which back-calculates to ≈1996 [S3]. The citizenship claim is corroborated only by the author's own published statement; naturalisation of a long-resident former Australian is legally ordinary in Indonesia, but no third-party record of *his* citizenship was sought or found [S11]. The migration was not a clean break from Australasia: a c.2020 autobiographical note has him recalling that he "lived most of my adult life in SE asia, mainly malaysia and indonesia… before then, in australasia, australia, new zealand, the pacific" [S5].

### The degree — documented as Bachelor of Asian Studies
The most substantive evidentiary tension in this note concerns the *form* of the author's degree, which is reported three inconsistent ways across self-reported sources. The SEED and the private correspondence describe an "advanced degree in Indonesian anthropology" [S1][S2]; a c.2020 corpus note has him writing "I have an advanced degree in sociology. ironic eh." [S5]; and a 2019 corpus email signature carries the post-nominal "BAsianSt" (Bachelor of Asian Studies) [S4]. The documented credential resolves cleanly: two independent public surfaces — his personal site and the Okusi Associates consultants page — agree on the *same* formal qualification, a **Bachelor of Asian Studies** (Murdoch, 1996–2000, with the year at UGM) [S11][S17], matching the "BAsianSt" signature [S4]. The most defensible reading is that "Indonesian anthropology" and "sociology" are the author's own *discipline-flavoured glosses* on an Asian-Studies degree weighted toward Indonesian society and culture (Asian Studies majors routinely blend anthropology, sociology, politics and language), not evidence of a distinct postgraduate degree — none of which is evidenced anywhere [S11][S12]. This note therefore asserts the documented fact (Bachelor of Asian Studies, with the UGM year) and treats "Indonesian anthropology" as the author's looser self-description rather than a corroborated postgraduate qualification.

### Working life in Indonesia: business, journalism, and the Okusi Group
The author's working life in Indonesia ran from study into business and journalism before the present knowledgebase work. His site and the company's own site describe him as "founder and chairman of the Okusi group of companies, providing corporate services to foreign companies investing in Indonesia," and a director of several other Indonesian companies [S11]. The firm corroborates this independently of his personal bio: Okusi Associates was "founded in 1997," has operated 25+ years, claims to have established "over 3,000 Foreign Direct Investment companies (FDI, or PMA …), more than any other firm in Indonesia," and runs offices in Jakarta, Bali (Istana Kuta Galeria, Kuta) and Batam with 60+ graduate staff [S14]. A 2019 corpus email signature independently shows "Gary Dean BAsianSt, Chairman, Okusi Associates" [S4]. Between the Murdoch years and the present KB work he also worked as a copy editor at *The Jakarta Post* (2004–2005) [S12]. His works page shows the threads running together over time — Indonesia business/economics essays from 1997–2003, the Jakarta Post period, and then the recent convergence of programming and philosophy: "Getting Serious about Bash" (12 Apr 2026), "Digital Sovereignty" (31 Mar 2026, on Indonesia's technological independence), "Defining Dharma" (2 Sep 2024) and "In Search of Dharma" (20 Jun 2026) [S12].

On the tension between this trade and the book's politics — a lifelong anarchist running a corporate-services firm for foreign-invested companies — the author's stance is pragmatic and deliberately undefended: he has to earn a living; the firm gives a living to hundreds of people, directly and indirectly; the companies it establishes are real businesses employing, in his words, "god-knows how many" Indonesians; and he is, he says, "very aware of the apparent contradiction" [S31]. The book's Preface treats the contradiction as a disclosed fingerprint on the lens rather than a resolved question, naming both directions of its effect: the day job as a standing education in what state compliance actually demands, and the class distance of admiring a refusal he has never had to pay for [S31].

The Jakarta Post years left one essay that bears directly on the later dharma work. In *Where Is My Compassion?*, written on a later anniversary of the 26 December 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami — which he covered from the newsroom as the toll climbed from "hundreds" to "hundreds of thousands" — he describes reaching a point where "I had to stop caring… my empathy was exhausted," and a deliberate, lasting self-hardening "not because I am not empathetic, but because I am *too* empathetic" [S25]. The same piece states a theme that runs straight into the dharma project: that to live in Indonesia is to live where "catastrophe, impermanence and instability are very much more in the foreground than in other places on the planet" — impermanence as a daily condition rather than a doctrine [S25].

### Indonesia's Indic substratum (why "dharma" is at hand)
The author's choice of *dharma* as his subject is partly geographic and cultural rather than purely abstract. He observes that Indonesia, "despite now being a nominally predominantly muslim country, has deep cultural roots imported from India ('Hindu'). sanskrit words and concepts are embedded within the language, and even the cosmology is never far away. dharma is just another of those words; every bit as hand-wavey here as it is elsewhere" [S1]. The corpus corroborates that he reasons about the term from inside that cultural field: a 2022 contribution discusses the everyday meaning of "dharma" "here in Java-Bali," drawing on Indonesian usages such as *Dharma Wanita* and *adat* [S8]. This grounds the connection between his biography and the project: the word is not exotic vocabulary to him but ambient cultural furniture.

### Javanese immersion via Sumarah
A textured strand of the formation is the author's long practice of **Sumarah meditation**, which deepened his immersion in central-Javanese culture. He writes that he "practised Sumarah meditation for many years, which although mainly just a meditation practice was inevitably bound to elements traditional Javanese culture, with which I did not always identify," and that he came to secular dharma "as an unrepentant Western liberal atheist" [S3]. Sumarah is a Javanese *kebatinan* (mystical) movement of "total surrender" (*sujud*), without a fixed guru or canon, documented in the corpus through Paul Stange's writings and a *Religions of the World* encyclopedia entry [S9]. The author has since confirmed directly that it was **Paul Stange** — his Asian Studies lecturer at Murdoch (Stange taught there 1980–2003) — who introduced him to Sumarah, making the Murdoch → Stange → Sumarah link *direct* rather than merely circumstantial, and naming it as his earliest encounter with something akin to a secular dharma (see [[0.7-influences-and-acknowledgments]]) [S27]. His involvement ran past practice into authorship: he built the movement's English-language website, `sumarah.net`, in 1998 while living in Java, and maintains it still — nearly thirty years on, on essentially the same code — a small artifact of the same self-taught programming that runs through this whole formation [S27]. A c.2020 note frames the cultural drift this produced: "up until perhaps 20 years ago, I strongly identified as an anarcho-communist westerner, with hippie leanings… as I became increasingly immersed in the daily life and culture of central Java in the late '90s, I sort of started to put a distance between myself and western culture" [S5].

### Present life in Bali: programmer and knowledgebase-builder
The present-day picture — "i now live in bali; most of my time now is spent programming; updating my companies systems/intranet, and working on RAG knowledgebases, among the most important is appliedanthropology/seculardharma" [S1] — is the part of the biography the corpus documents most richly. His site lists the matching technical skills: "application programming (PHP, SQL, Bash, C)" and "Linux system administration" [S11]. In the corpus he describes himself doing "programming, you know, writing tools," and says "curation is my main task at the moment" [S6][S7]. The knowledgebase project is documented in detail: he curates a large dataset (on the order of 700 videos plus texts), transcribes audio with OpenAI's Whisper, cleans the transcripts into coherent paragraphs with further AI tooling, and builds a model/agent intended to "sit alongside other LLMs or be incorporated into other knowledgebases" [S6][S7]. He set up the **Indonesian Open Technology Foundation** (YaTTI / Yayasan Teknologi Terbuka Indonesia) "to promote open source" and publishes his tools (e.g. the `dejavu2` interpreter) to its GitHub — the same YaTTi behind the `yatti-api` interface used to research this very note [S7]. The private correspondence (a local primary source) dates the secular-dharma knowledgebase to "~ seven years ago," with an underlying personal collection "forty" years old, since broadened into "applied anthropology — over 14,000 curated books, articles and transcripts," into which the sangha's ~50 hours of "a secular dharma?" video transcripts were folded [S2][S6]. He also runs **jawawa.id**, an Indonesian news archive of some 246,000 articles spanning 1994 to the present, grown out of the same *Jakarta Post* archive he once worked in — a through-line from the newsroom years to the data-and-RAG work [S26].

The residence picture, at public-edition level, is simple and publicly attested. The firm's public contact page lists client offices in three Indonesian cities — Menara Cakrawala (Jakarta), Citra Super Mall at Harbour Bay (Batam) and Istana Kuta Galeria (Kuta, Bali) [S18][S14] — and for the past several years the author has been based principally in **Bali**: the SEED's "i now live in bali" [S1]. The corpus's thinner signals — a 2022 message reasoning from "here in Java-Bali," another inviting a correspondent to "drop into Bali" [S8], the Kuta business address in the private correspondence [S2] — sit consistently inside this picture. ▲ *Household and residence specifics beyond the public office footprint are held in the author's private records and excluded from this edition.*

### The intellectual thesis the biography serves
The formation culminates in a stated thesis that connects the life to the essay series. In the private correspondence the author writes that dharmas and mutual aid "are both, in the end, evolved ways for a particular cooperative primate to live well together. different vocabularies but the same underlying machinery," naming this convergence (felt by his correspondent between Graeber and Batchelor) as "precisely my thesis" [S2]. The "connective tissue," he writes, is human behavioural biology — David Sloan Wilson's *This View of Life* (and *Prosocial*), Christopher Boehm's *Hierarchy in the Forest* on how foragers actively hold their egalitarianism in place, and Sarah Hrdy's *Mothers and Others* on the origins of cooperative instinct — read alongside Kropotkin so that "the whole 'anarchist anthropology' framing stops looking romantic and starts looking like plain natural history" [S2]. He situates secular dharma as "one of the applications running on top of" applied anthropology as substrate: "for me, secular dharma stops being floating piety the moment you ground it in human evolutionary biology" [S2]. This is the bridge from biography (Part 0) to the project's evolutionary-functional account of dharma developed across the foundational and philosophical notes.

### Intellectual temperament (from the working journals)
The dated journals of his AI assistant add texture to the temperament behind the project. He frames secular dharma bluntly as Buddhism-as-practical-psychology "minus the metaphysics," with "no woo-woo," and likes the compression "consciousness without soul: Gotama and Sapolsky arrived at the same place" — holding, with Sapolsky, a hard-determinist view of behaviour that he nonetheless refuses to let dissolve moral responsibility at the social level, the naturalistic core that [[0.5-thesis-evolved-dharma]] develops [S26]. He treats provenance as a discipline in its own right, even musing on adapting the Islamic hadith sciences — *isnad* (chains of transmission) and *mutaba'a* (convergent auditing) — to the verification of knowledge, recognisably the same instinct that drives this project's per-claim sourcing and the method of [[0.6-knowledgebase-as-method]] [S26]. In working style he demands "radical candour" over flattery, distrusts "AI slop" and marketing padding, and holds to KISS — wanting analysis "legible to an intelligent non-specialist," concrete and evidence-led, with "no hand-waving" [S26].

### Influences and the small sangha (context)
The author flags that the essays "would not have been possible without direct input from other people, entities, and non-entities," and credits a "tiny sangha" of himself, Elfie and Rupert, whose recorded conversations were uploaded and later transcribed into the corpus [S1][S6]. He dates the group's formation to 13 February 2018, at the first of six 5-day Bodhi College retreats on Secular Dharma in West Sussex, the main teacher being Stephen Batchelor, whose *After Buddhism* he names as influential in his taking the course [S1]. His study of dharma, he writes, "intensified once again in about 2014" [S1]. His earlier political arc — beginning with the Communist Party of Australia ("I started out, for a few years, with the CPA, but was eventually invited to leave") and moving to anarchist groups, "mainly the Freedom Collective in Fremantle" — is recounted in his own words and supplies the "anarchist anthropology" lineage he later folds into the thesis [S1][S2].

### Family and personal life (self-reported; public edition)
The author's personal life has been, in his own published words, "large and disorderly by any conventional measure": multiple marriages across his Australian and Indonesian life, **nine children, born between 1979 and 2017**, divided between Australia and Indonesia, and **four grandchildren** [S22]. (Per the author's standing instruction his children's names are never recorded; they are referred to by relationship only.) Two bereavements bracket the period in which his dharma study intensified: his son died in 2012, and his partner **Anita** — whom he had met in Yogyakarta in 2004 and cared for through her final illness — died of cancer in 2015; he writes that it was "not a happy time, to say the least" [S1][S22]. Anita is named in the author's own published essays; the naming here follows that public record. He now lives in Bali with his partner and their two youngest children [S22].

### The name "Biksu Okusi" (given, not taken)
The name on the book's cover — *biksu* is Indonesian for a Buddhist monk — was **given to the author, not chosen by him** [S29]. By his direct account it arose more than a decade ago among Indonesian friends and colleagues — all of them, as it happens, Muslims — who knew of his meditation practice and his dharma reading, and who observed his habit of secluding himself in his office studios in Jakarta, Batam and Bali for weeks and months at a time, conducting all Okusi business online and avoiding direct contact with the firm's clients (a practice he maintains), his working life narrowed to programming — for Okusi, for websites, for the knowledgebases — and the study of applied anthropology [S29]. He connects the deepening of that seclusion to the bereavements of 2012–2015 (§ *Family*, above) and to the Jakarta terror attacks of 2009 and 2016, both of which touched him directly: a client was killed in the 2009 Marriott/Ritz-Carlton bombings, and the January 2016 attack came as three bombs, the second and largest detonating in the Starbucks on the ground floor of Menara Cakrawala — the building housing his Jakarta office, twelve floors above — where he bought coffee every working morning. The author's own assessment is that this bomb "was probably meant for me" [S29][S30]. The name was "a little playful, but at the same time serious," and at first it annoyed him; over years of use he came to accept it as part of his Indonesian identity, "mixing my commitment to a dharma, and my 'marriage' to my company, Okusi," the firm he has run since 1997 [S29][S14]. The name also serves a deliberate protective function. Because the book handles topics that remain legally and socially sensitive in Indonesia — communism and atheism above all (see [[0.2-politics-and-anarchism]]) — publishing under the given name "deflect[s] some attention away from my legal name" [S29]. It is expressly not a disguise: the author's legal name is attached to the project's public repository and to his published works; the intent is a partial screen, not anonymity [S29]. He stresses that the name was never derogatory, and that its givers — ordinary Indonesian Muslims — are not to be conflated with the hard-line currents that make such topics sensitive [S29].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **Almost everything here is self-reported.** With a handful of exceptions, every biographical claim traces to the author's own surfaces — his public site [S11][S12], his own contributions to the corpus [S3][S5][S6][S7][S8], the private correspondence [S2], and his direct June-2026 account to the project [S27] — or to the SEED file in his own words [S1]. The company site [S14][S17] is "independent" only in the weak sense that it is a different website; it speaks for the author's own firm. This is honest provenance, not a defect: a Part-0 biographical note legitimately rests on primary self-report. But a Stage-2 essay should not present these as externally verified facts. The genuinely third-party corroborations are few, and touch the *setting* of the life rather than its private substance: (a) **Wikipedia** on Perth's isolation and the Jakarta/Sydney distances [S13]; (b) **ACICIS** institutional history on the 1990s Murdoch→UGM study pathway [S15][S16] — circumstantial, placing the *pathway* and not the author by name in that consortium; and (c) the public record of the author's named schoolfriend, the musician **Kim Salmon** [S28], confirming at least one fixed point in the otherwise unverifiable Perth childhood. The expanded June-2026 material folded into this note — the working-class Embleton childhood and its now-vanished freedoms, the teenage drift years and the New Hebrides episode, and the entire Australian computing / ALP-campaign career — rests on the same self-reported footing, recorded at the author's request rather than because it was externally checked [S27].
