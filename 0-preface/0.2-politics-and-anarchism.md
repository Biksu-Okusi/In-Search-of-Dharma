# 0.2 Politics & Anarchism

> ◉ **Public edition.** This note is published in a redacted public edition. Personal contact details, private third-party specifics, and passages resting on the author's private records are excluded, and each exclusion is disclosed where it is made. The private sources themselves (correspondence, family records, the mail archive) are held by the author and are not part of the public repository.

**Question:** Reconstruct the author's political-intellectual arc and how it feeds the series' thesis: early Communist Party of Australia membership, the move to anarchist groups (notably the Freedom Collective in Fremantle), the 1970s letterpress printing; and the throughline to "anarchist anthropology" — Graeber, Colin Ward, Alex Prichard, Kropotkin's mutual aid — read as plain natural history of a cooperative primate rather than romantic politics.

## Synthesis

The author's political arc runs, in his own words, from a few years inside the **Communist Party of Australia** ("eventually invited to leave; they were very nice about it") to **anarchist groups around Australia, mainly the Freedom Collective in Fremantle**, with a sideline as "a primitive letterpress printer in the 70s"; a fuller June-2026 account adds the working-class union milieu that made CPA membership unremarkable, his 1977 entry via the party's industrial branch and the Unemployed Workers Movement, the self-edited magazine *BOOTLEG*, and a comically overdetermined exit — a self-reported spine the shared corpus is silent on and that, per project policy, is recorded faithfully but not externally corroborated [S1][S2][S19][E1][E4]. What *is* heavily documented is the intellectual destination: the corpus holds, as primary full texts, exactly the "anarchist anthropology" constellation he names — Graeber, Kropotkin (five works), James C Scott, and Colin Ward (reached via Scott) — and these texts make the move the author makes, treating cooperation-without-hierarchy not as a political programme but as the observed default of a social animal [S3][S4][S6][S7][S9]. The hinge is Kropotkin's claim, echoed by Scott and Ward, that the moral sense is "instinctive," a natural faculty of group-living animals, later overlaid by law, religion and authority — i.e. ethics as natural history, which is precisely the author's "dharmas and mutual aid… different vocabularies but the same underlying machinery" thesis stated in a different idiom [S2][S8][S10][E11]. Read that way the politics stops being romantic and starts being descriptive: anarchism is "a way of living, or at least a set of practices," and mutual aid is "the quotidian experience of most people," not a utopia to be built [S5][S11]. The corpus is, however, curated *toward* this reading — Graeber/Kropotkin/Scott/Wilson in full, a sympathetic anarchist book review, and only Scott as internal caution — so the throughline is partly an interpretive frame the author chooses, not a neutral finding, and the note flags that honestly [S6][E8][E12].

## Findings

### The political spine — author's own words, uncorroborated by design
The arc the question reconstructs is sourced **entirely** to the author's own account: the autobiographical seed and his private email to Ramsey Margolis [S1][S2]. He writes: "i started out, for a few years, with the CPA, but was eventually invited to leave (they were very nice about it). from there gravitated to anarchist groups around Australia, mainly the Freedom Collective in Fremantle" [S2]. The letterpress detail is from the same email: "i used to get up to all sorts of mischief with my own little primitive letterpress printer in the 70s" [S2]. A separate autobiographical "keep" note in the corpus has him describing the same period from the inside — "up until perhaps 20 years ago, I strongly identified as an anarcho-communist westerner, with hippie leanings" — which is the author corroborating *himself* across surfaces, not an independent source [S12]. The "pre-Sputnik" birth he reports elsewhere (before October 1957) is consistent with a young adult doing CPA and anarchist work and running a letterpress in the 1970s [S1]. The shared corpus and the `prosocial.world` corpus return **nothing** on Gary Dean's personal politics, the Freedom Collective, Fremantle anarchism, or his letterpress activity — expected, since the corpus is topical rather than biographical, and itself the central provenance finding: for this life-story spine the only capturable source is the author's own words, and the knowledgebase supplies the intellectual backdrop, not the biography [S13].

The author's June-2026 account fills in this spine considerably, and reframes it. By his own account the conviction long predated any membership card: he considered himself a communist from the age of thirteen and suffered for it socially throughout his high-school years, so that the CPA he formally joined on returning to Perth in 1977 ratified a politics held since boyhood rather than a fresh enthusiasm [S19]. If that singled him out at school, it would not single him out in the adult world he was moving toward: membership of the CPA in the Western Australia of his youth was, he stresses, socially unremarkable: from the 1940s to the late 1980s Communists were dominant as trade-union officials and shop stewards despite the party almost never winning a parliamentary seat, trusted by workers "not to sell out even when an ALP government was in power," and — because the unions held direct representation in the Labor Party and financed the bulk of its campaigns — wielding real influence over ALP policy from within [S19]. By his account the CPA had long since broken with both Moscow and Beijing and operated much like the European "Eurocommunist" parties, on occasion playing "the adult in the room" against ultra-leftists in the ALP (especially in Victoria) [S19]. The external record is consistent with this: the party's 1971 turn toward Eurocommunism is documented, as is its disproportionate industrial weight relative to a tiny electoral footprint [E4][E1]. On returning to Perth in 1977 he joined the CPA's **industrial branch** and began years of activism, first through the **Unemployed Workers Movement** — a largely CPA-driven group, with anarchists alongside, based among the union offices at Trades Hall — organising protests and direct actions and advocating for unemployed people refused benefits [S19]. That 1977 date sits exactly on the period when the Perth Freedom Collective is independently recorded as active [E5].

His exit from the party he recounts with some relish, and as overdetermined: any of several episodes might have prompted the invitation to leave — hanging red flags from the top windows of Trades Hall; the thousands of dollars in unpaid parking fines accrued outside it (which one day brought a full police response as he left); a bomb mailed to the post-office box of the magazine he edited, **BOOTLEG**; the contents he published in it; his growing closeness to the anarchists; or the police raids [S19]. Whatever the proximate cause, he gravitated from there to the anarchist movement, "mainly the Freedom Collective in Fremantle" [S2][S19]. The point of the detail is not the comedy but what it documents: a sustained, years-long immersion in organised working-class and anti-authoritarian politics, not a brief youthful phase. There is a final irony the arc should record — the same man invited out of the Communist Party went on, in the 1980s, to spend the better part of a decade as the paid programmer building the **Australian Labor Party's** computerised polling and campaign machinery (treated in [[0.1-life-and-formation]]): anti-authoritarian conviction and a pragmatic facility for the machinery of mainstream electoral politics held, not altogether comfortably, in one life [S19].

A further account (July 2026) fills in the milieu beneath the membership, and corrects the emotional register in which the arc should be told. The union-Left world was not something he joined; it was the landscape he grew up in. His father was a metalworker, and shop stewards and union officials were an ordinary part of childhood life; his best friend's father, Owen Salmon, was secretary of the hospital workers' union and later appointed a commissioner on the Western Australian industrial arbitration court, and the two would still meet at times in Trades Hall years later, during the author's Unemployed Workers Movement activism [S20]. The Left of that time and place he describes as "ecumenical": communists, Labor people and anarchists all knew one another and worked together more often than not [S20]. His retrospective stance is explicitly unashamed — "quite the contrary… it was right for its time" — and he regards the 1970s CPA as the most progressive and liberal party of its day, its members, particularly the union leaders he was closest to, people of exceptional integrity [S20]. The same Left provenance explains the ALP decade: he was approached in 1984 to build the ALP's computerised electorate-campaign and polling systems precisely because he was *known* to the broad Left as someone who could be trusted; the work ran federally and in every state for over a decade, and its confidential nature means very little of it is public information [S20]. He notes plainly that none of this milieu-level history is documented anywhere: the only sources are the memories of people who were there — which is exactly the self-report provenance this note's method anticipates, recorded faithfully and flagged as uncorroborated [S20]. A further gloss (also July 2026) supplies the arc as he reads it: the party cracked in 1956 with the invasion of Hungary — "a year before i was even born" — and was "utterly severed" from the Soviet Union in 1968, which led to "its development as a genuinely independent working-class political entity with an influence that was far in excess of its actual size"; the ending he reads as response rather than defeat — "i have never really changed. but the world did": union influence waned through the late 1980s, the party's relevance with it, and the CPA answered by dissolving itself, "by fits and starts," into the new paradigm, the Greens [S20]. The arc's main elements match the externally checked record in [[7.6-cpa-case]] — the 1956 membership haemorrhage, the 1967–68 severance, the union weight against a tiny electoral footprint, the staged dissolution-and-migration — which makes this the rare stretch of the self-report carrying independent corroboration. ▲ One element is corroborated only in part, and Stage 2 should state it precisely: the terminus was **not** an organisational merger with the Greens. The party dissolved in 1991 into the New Left Party, with its assets to the SEARCH Foundation; what did flow to the Greens was the **migration of ex-members**, Jack Mundey among them, which is real and documented. [[7.6-cpa-case]] rates the "merged into the Greens" reading "◐ half right." The correction is to the corporate mechanism only and touches nothing in the register above: the reading of the ending as response rather than defeat stands. Any Stage-2 use of the political arc must respect this register: the CPA years are to be told with pride of provenance, not as a youthful error recanted; the counter-case reading (the party that unbuilt its own dogma, [[7.6-cpa-case]]) is the author's considered view of what he was part of, not a later absolution.

External research does not corroborate the author's individual case (and, per the privacy constraint, was not used to try) but does confirm that the *kinds* of organisation he names existed in the form and period he describes, which makes the self-report historically coherent without prying. The **Communist Party of Australia** was founded in Sydney on 30 October 1920, peaked at roughly 22,000 members in 1945, and had a real, documented culture of resignations and expulsions through the late-1960s–70s (the 1971 Eurocommunism turn produced the Watts/Ross expulsions and a wholesale walkout to form the Socialist Party of Australia in December 1971) — so "invited to leave" in that decade is entirely plausible for the organisation, whatever the author's personal circumstances [E1][E4]. The **Freedom Collective** is likewise real and regionally documented, though at one remove: ▲ the Takver history-archive page carrying the detail was reachable only **via search index** — direct fetch failed on an expired TLS certificate — so what follows is a search-index summary of an archival record, not the fetched page itself [E5]. On that basis the **Perth Freedom Collective** was active from 1977 as part of the 1970s–80s Australian anarchist revival, and in mid-1981 initiated a proposal for a federation of anarchist groups and better inter-group communication, while feeling isolated from the eastern-state groups and fearing police persecution under Western Australia's then-conservative government [E5]. It does *not* appear in the general survey of Australian anarchism (which covers Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane and Adelaide), marking it a minor, regionally-documented group of the right place and period rather than a nationally prominent one — exactly what "anarchist groups around Australia, mainly the Freedom Collective in Fremantle" would lead one to expect [E5][E6]. The author's *public* site carries the non-sensitive frame of all this: Perth-born, emigrated to Indonesia in 1996, with standing interests in "Group theory/Prosociality," human evolution "(biological, cultural, technological, political)" and "Secularised dharmas," and a published Perth-childhood memoir, *Escape at 14* (2016) — so the early-life and political-formation strand is something he has worked in public before, not solely private correspondence [E2][E3].

### "Anarchist anthropology" as the corpus understands it — practice, not doctrine
The destination of the arc — the framing the author calls "precisely my thesis" — is present in the corpus as primary texts, not summaries. David Graeber's *Direct Action: An Ethnography* lays out three senses of "anarchist": (1) adherents of the explicit nineteenth-century doctrine; (2) the broad historical sense (peasant rebels, medieval radicals who rejected government); and (3) the **ethnographic** sense — "when an anthropologist like Evans-Pritchard refers to the Nuer as living in an 'ordered anarchy' (1940), or Joanna Overing uses the word to describe the Amazonian Piaroa" — i.e. anarchism as institutions, habits and practices, "a way of living, or at least, a set of practices," not a programme [S5]. This third sense *is* the "anarchist anthropology read as natural history" move the question asks for. Graeber's anarchist bibliography in the same volume (Bookchin, Castoriadis, Malatesta, Evans-Pritchard's *The Nuer*) indexes the wider field the corpus carries [S5]. The corpus also holds Graeber and Andrej Grubačić's *Introduction to Mutual Aid* (2020), which frames Kropotkin's argument as "a particularly cogent argument against reigning political common sense" and is dedicated to Graeber's memory [S3].

The book that crystallised the connection for the author's correspondent — and which the author endorsed as "precisely my thesis" — is **Graeber's *Fragments of an Anarchist Anthropology*** (Prickly Paradigm Press, 2004); Margolis wrote that reading it struck him with "how close some of Graeber's thinking was to that of Stephen Batchelor," and the author replied that "that closeness is precisely my thesis" [S2][E7]. *Fragments* is the structural backbone of the reading: anthropology is "particularly well positioned" to catalogue the full range of human social arrangements, and anarchism is "an ethical discourse about revolutionary practice" — something *done*, not theorised — so that a stateless egalitarian society (Graeber's Madagascar Tsimihety example) counts as anarchist "even if they don't declare themselves that way, because anarchy is about practice" [E7][E8].

### Kropotkin: mutual aid and the naturalistic moral sense
The scientific keystone the author names is Kropotkin, present in the corpus as full primary text in at least two works. *Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution* (1902) argues that "sociability is as much a law of nature as mutual struggle… mutual aid is as much a law of animal life as mutual struggle, but… as a factor of evolution it most probably has a far greater importance" [S4]. Externally it is bibliographically pinned (Heinemann / McClure, Phillips & Co., 1902; essays serialised in *The Nineteenth Century*, 1890–96) and read as an explicit rebuttal of social-Darwinist "survival of the fittest" — specifically TH Huxley's "gladiatorial" view of nature — tracing mutual aid from animals through "savages," "barbarians," medieval free cities and modern labour [E11]. *Anarchist Morality* (1897) makes the ethical move the whole series turns on: the moral sense is "instinctive, based on the sympathy and unity inherent in group life," "a natural faculty in us like the sense of smell or of touch," grounded in the golden rule as a natural product of group living and offering "a scientific basis for morality in the theory of evolution" — a natural sense later "perverted… by the superstitions surrounding law, religion and authority" [S8][E12]. That is the author's "different vocabularies, same machinery" thesis [S2] stated by Kropotkin himself: ethics as the natural history of a cooperative animal, with religion and law as a later overlay rather than the source.

This is not a personal quirk but a documented lineage. The corpus's own essay "On the Origin of Socialist Darwinism" records that the *original* application of Darwin to society was cooperative and egalitarian — "Socialist Darwinism… predates Social Darwinism" — with **Kropotkin "the most prominent advocate"** [S14]. And David Sloan Wilson's *This View of Life* (in the corpus) devotes a chapter, "The Anarchist," to profiling Kropotkin and framing *Mutual Aid* as a genuine contribution to evolutionary theory, situating him against Malthus, Spencer and Galton — exactly the "rebalancing Darwin away from red-in-tooth-and-claw" story [S9]. Wilson, Boehm and Hrdy are the named "connective tissue" the author cites; Boehm's *Hierarchy in the Forest* (Harvard, 1999) supplies the field evidence that egalitarianism is *actively held in place* — foragers cooperate as groups, care for non-relatives, and "preach steadfastly… in favor of altruism" via a "reverse dominance" hierarchy — which is the empirical face of Kropotkin's claim that mutuality is the animal's default [S2][S15].

### James C Scott and Colin Ward: mutual aid as the quotidian, not the utopian
The corpus's single strongest bridge text for the "natural history, not romantic politics" reading is **James C Scott's *Two Cheers for Anarchism* (2012)**, present both as full text and as a corpus review. Scott defines the relevant anarchism, via Proudhon, as "mutuality, or *cooperation without hierarchy or state rule*," and offers "a sort of anarchist squint" [S7][S11]. His central empirical claim is the question's exact move: "forms of informal cooperation, coordination, and action that embody mutuality without hierarchy are the quotidian experience of most people… the experience of anarchistic mutuality is ubiquitous" [S7]. Critically, Scott quotes **Colin Ward** — one of the three thinkers the question names — directly: anarchistic mutual aid is "far from being a speculative vision of a future society… a description of a mode of human experience of everyday life, which operates side-by-side with… the dominant authoritarian trends of our society" (Ward, *Anarchy in Action*, Freedom Press) [S7]. The corpus reaches Ward only second-hand through Scott, and the external pass does not close that gap: ▲ [E9] is the **encyclopaedia entry on** *Anarchy in Action*, not Ward's own text, so Ward remains a second-hand citation — bibliographically pinned, with his wording relayed rather than read. At that level, Ward's *Anarchy in Action* (Allen & Unwin, 1973; 2nd ed. Freedom Press, 2008) is the most exact external statement of the note's thesis: Ward is reported as calling his own book "an extended, updating footnote to Kropotkin's *Mutual Aid*," and argues that "an anarchist society, a society which organizes itself without authority, is always in existence, like a seed beneath the snow," grounding the claim in anthropology, sociology and ordinary life (housing, education, work) rather than political struggle [E9].

The fourth thinker the question names, **Alex Prichard**, is the one the corpus is thin on — he did not surface as a distinct corpus author in any query — so the external pass fills the gap. Prichard's *Justice, Order and Anarchy: The International Political Theory of Pierre-Joseph Proudhon* (Routledge, 2013) recovers **Proudhon** — the man who coined the political use of "anarchy" as *order without rule* — as the first anarchist theorist of war, peace and international order. ▲ But [E10] is the **Routledge catalogue page** for that book — publisher metadata, not Prichard's text — so the note can pin Prichard bibliographically (as Margolis's email names him) while remaining unable to cite him at wording level; the description of his argument is the publisher's, not a reading of the book [S2][E10]. Scott himself supplies the internal counterweight to romanticism: he worries that two centuries of strong states and liberal economies "may have socialized us so that we have largely lost the habits of mutuality" — i.e. the cooperative primate's default can be eroded, which is a critical-realist check on the "anarchism is everywhere" reading [S7].

### Why anthropologists keep becoming anarchists — and why it matters here
The author notices, in Margolis's arc and "in a subset of people drawn into recent secular dharma communities," a recurring "constellation": "anthropology into anarchist politics" [S2]. The corpus documents the pattern explicitly. A corpus review of Scott asks: "What is it about anthropologists and anarchism? Noted anarchists Brian Morris and David Graeber are anthropologists in their day jobs while Peter Kropotkin and Elisée Reclus both made significant contributions to the field" — and proposes the structural explanation: "anthropology shows that people have lived in many different ways and so confirms a basic principle of anarchism: capitalism is just one of many systems and… can be replaced" [S6]. Externally this is corroborated by Graeber's own case (anthropology professor turned movement anarchist, *Fragments* being the bridge) and by the survey treatment of anthropology as "particularly well positioned" for anarchist theory because it documents that present arrangements are contingent [E7][E8]. This is the structural reason the author's biography and his thesis fit together: the discipline that shows ethics is plural and evolved tends to produce people who read politics as natural history — which is the same lens this whole essay series applies to dharma.

### The bridge to 0.5: mutual aid and dharmas as the same machinery
The reason the political arc *feeds the thesis* (rather than being mere colour) is the claim that mutual aid and dharma are two descriptions of one underlying thing. The author states it directly: "dharmas and mutual aid are both, in the end, evolved ways for a particular cooperative primates to live well together. different vocabularies but the same underlying machinery" [S2]. The `prosocial.world` corpus supplies the science that makes this a claim rather than a slogan: Oliver Scott Curry's "morality as cooperation" frames morality as "a set of biological and cultural strategies for solving problems of cooperation and conflict… a vast Periodic Table of Ethics," and summarises that "morality is always and everywhere a cooperative phenomenon" [S16][S17]. A `prosocial.world` essay adds that cultural moral norms "are best understood not as moral absolutes but as heuristics… for sustainably increasing the benefits of cooperation" [S18]. If dharmas are evolved cultural technologies for cooperation, and mutual aid is the same cooperation viewed by anarchists and biologists, then they are indeed "different vocabularies, same machinery" — and Kropotkin's naturalistic ethics, James C Scott's ubiquitous mutuality, and Curry's periodic table of cooperation become three angles on the single fact the series develops in the foundational notes. This is the explicit hinge to [[0.5-thesis-evolved-dharma]] (the named cast and lineage) and to the project's evolutionary-functional account of dharma.

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The political spine is self-reported and uncorroborated, by design.** The CPA membership, the "invited to leave" (and the fuller June-2026 elaboration of it — the union milieu, the 1977 industrial-branch entry, the Unemployed Workers Movement, the self-edited magazine *BOOTLEG* and the bomb mailed to its PO box, and the later decade building ALP campaign systems), the Freedom Collective, and the 1970s letterpress are sourced *only* to the author's own words [S1][S2][S19], reinforced by his own corpus "keep" note ("anarcho-communist westerner, with hippie leanings" [S12]) — which is the author corroborating himself, not independent verification. His *characterisation of CPA history* (Communist dominance in the unions, the Eurocommunist break) is partly borne out by the external record [E1][E4], but his personal *role* in it is not. Per the project's privacy constraint on private political and family history, none of this was web-searched against the individual; the external pass confirmed only that the organisations existed in the right form and period [E1][E4][E5]. This is honest provenance for a Part-0 biographical note, not a defect — but a Stage-2 essay must not present the personal arc as externally verified fact.
- **The corpus is curated toward the thesis (a strong secular / evolutionary / anarchist-leaning lean).** The shared corpus carries Graeber, Kropotkin (five works), James C Scott, DS Wilson and Boehm in full, plus a sympathetic "anarcho" book review — i.e. it is curated around exactly this note's constellation [S3][S4][S6][S7][S9][S15]. There is little in-corpus that is *skeptical* of mutual aid or anarchist anthropology: no strong Hobbesian/realist or conservative critique surfaced, and Scott himself supplies the only internal caution (that strong states may erode "the habits of mutuality") [S7]. The corpus even self-flags Kropotkin's selective naturalism as "as one-sided as Dawkins' selfish genes," which is the kind of balance the rest of the shelf under-supplies [S13]. External balance was therefore needed and is folded into the points below.
- **The naturalistic-ethics move is openly contested.** Kropotkin's grounding of morality in evolution is, in the secondary literature, both praised as "an early form of naturalistic ethics and a distinctive reply to social-Darwinist and Hobbesian pessimism" *and* criticised for "naturalizing moral norms" and "underestimating conflict and domination" [E12]. The note carries this two-sidedness rather than treating "mutual aid = morality" as settled: it is precisely the is/ought and selective-evidence worry that the corpus under-represents, and that the series must argue rather than assume.
- **"Anarchism is everywhere" is a normative reading, not a neutral fact.** Graeber and Ward both concede they are *choosing* to call stateless or everyday cooperation "anarchist" — Graeber: anarchist "because anarchy is about practice"; Ward: anarchist principles "discerned in" everyday life [E8][E9]. So the throughline this note traces is partly an interpretive frame laid over the ethnographic record, not a finding forced by it. That is exactly why the author's "stops looking romantic and starts looking like plain natural history" [S2] is a *claim to be argued* in the essays, not a given — the honest counterweight this note's thesis needs.
- **Prichard was a real corpus gap.** Of the four named thinkers, Graeber, Ward (via Scott) and Kropotkin are well covered in-corpus; **Alex Prichard did not surface as a distinct corpus author** [S13]. His citation rests entirely on the external pass [E10] — and that pass reached only the **publisher's catalogue page**, not his text. Ward is thinner than it looks too: [E9] is the encyclopaedia entry, not *Anarchy in Action* itself. So of the four named thinkers, only Graeber and Kropotkin are held at wording level; Ward and Prichard are pinned bibliographically and no further. This is a genuine thinness, not a synthesis: any essay leaning on Proudhon-via-Prichard must obtain the book.
- **Tooling caveat.** During the KB research pass the `yatti-api` *scholarly* (LLM-synthesis) endpoint was reliably returning HTTP 502 / "Empty response from LLM"; only the context-only retrieval endpoint worked. All KB findings therefore rest on raw retrieved chunks with doc/segment metadata, not on synthesised RAG answers — accurate as quotation, but not cross-checked by the model's own synthesis [S13].
