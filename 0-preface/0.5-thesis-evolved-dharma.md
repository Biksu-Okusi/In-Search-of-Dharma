# 0.5 The Thesis: Dharma as Evolved Cultural Technology

> ◉ **Public edition.** This note is published in a redacted public edition. Personal contact details, private third-party specifics, and passages resting on the author's private records are excluded, and each exclusion is disclosed where it is made. The private sources themselves (correspondence, family records, the mail archive) are held by the author and are not part of the public repository.

**Question:** Articulate the intellectual core of the whole series, in the author's framing: dharmas (plural) are evolved cultural technologies — ethical systems shaped by and for a particular kind of cooperative social primate; applied anthropology is the substrate and secular dharma one application running on top of it; and Graeber sits naturally beside Batchelor because mutual aid and dharma are different vocabularies for the same evolved machinery. Ground this in the influence "cast" and their key works.

## Synthesis

The intellectual core of the series is a single claim the author states in his own words: "dharmas — plural — are evolved cultural technologies: ethical systems shaped by and for a particular kind of social primate. applied anthropology is the substrate; secular dharma is one of the applications running on top of it" [S30]. The shared corpus does not contain that exact sentence, but it is curated to make the claim true — it defines a dharma functionally as a "social technology" / "operating system" that solves the coordination problems every human group faces (trust, conflict, free-riding, the commons) by making behaviour predictable and enabling cooperation at scale [S1][S2][S3], and it grounds that function in evolved tendencies toward cooperation, altruism and social bonding [S3][S6]. The author's curatorial gesture is to shelve a contemplative-ethical tradition (Stephen Batchelor's secular dharma) next to a political-anthropological one (David Graeber's mutual aid) on the grounds that both name "evolved ways for a particular cooperative primate[s] to live well together — different vocabularies but the same underlying machinery" [S30]; the most visible textual sign of that convergence is that Batchelor's own *Ten Theses* and Graeber's anarchist anthropology reach for the same phrase, "structural violence," and the same evolved "ethics of care" [S10][S7]. ▲ That phrase is **Johan Galtung's** coinage (1969), standard peace-studies vocabulary both men inherited [S44], so it evidences **shared left-analytic idiom, not proof of convergence**; the convergence has to rest on the substantive overlap, not the wording. The connective tissue between the two registers is human behavioural biology — Kropotkin's mutual aid as a *factor of evolution* [S26], D.S. Wilson's multilevel selection [S12][S15], Boehm on how foragers actively enforce egalitarianism [S19], Hrdy on cooperative breeding as the origin of prosociality [S21], Sapolsky on behaviour across nested timescales [S22] — which is exactly the "cast" the author names [S30][S31]. This is the most knowledgebase-primary note in the preface set: the framing is the author's own, but nearly every named book in the cast is physically present in the corpus as a primary text [S7][S10][S12][S19][S21][S22][S25][S26].

## Findings

### The thesis in the author's own words (local primary)

The claim is stated twice in the private Margolis correspondence and once in the author's public essay. In the email of 24 May 2026 the author writes that "for me, secular dharma stops being floating piety the moment you ground it in human evolutionary biology," that "dharmas — plural — are evolved cultural technologies: ethical systems shaped by and for a particular kind of social primate," and that "applied anthropology is the substrate; secular dharma is one of the applications running on top of it. so, for me, Graeber and Sapolsky sit quite naturally alongside Batchelor — different vocabularies, but the same underlying project" [S30]. In the email of 17 June 2026 he restates the convergence directly: "dharmas and mutual aid are both, in the end, evolved ways for a particular cooperative primate[s] to live well together. different vocabularies but the same underlying machinery, perhaps" [S30]. The public, citable form of the same idea appears in his essay *In Search of Dharma* (Part 1, garydean.id, 20 Jun 2026), which calls a dharma "the characteristic human solution" to the problem of cooperation, and states that "morality … is the social technology that lets the animal cooperate at scale" [S32]. So the thesis no longer rests only on private correspondence: the public essay carries the underlying "dharma = evolved social technology for a cooperative animal" claim on the record, while the email supplies the specific substrate/application sentence and the Graeber-beside-Batchelor wording [S30][S32].

### "Cultural technology": the corpus's own functional definition

The author's phrase is not imported jargon; it is the corpus's own register. The shared corpus repeatedly defines a dharma as an "evolving constellation of values, norms, stories, practices, and institutions" — a "bundle" telling a person or group "how to live well, here, now" — and names it a "mid-level cultural control system" and an "operating system / protocol suite for coordinated action" [S1][S2][S3]. It is explicitly a "social technology" / "coordination technology" that solves the enduring problems every human group must solve (trust, conflict, belonging, meaning, free-riding, commons management) by making behaviour predictable and enabling cooperation at scale [S2][S4]. The "technology" framing carries the corollaries the thesis needs: **plurality** ("there is no single 'The Dharma'"; cultures generate many, sometimes competing dharmas), **adaptiveness and impermanence** (dharmas "arise, mutate, hybridise, and sometimes go extinct" like languages or species, under cultural selection), and **non-privileging** (religious and secular dharmas "share the same structural logic," metaphysical dharmas holding "no privilege over secular ones") [S3][S5]. The author's public essay states the same function/mechanism split: "The function of a dharma is nearly always the same: sustain cooperation, hold the group together. But the mechanism … can be almost anything" — one evolved job, many cultural implementations [S32].

### "Shaped by and for a cooperative social primate"

The corpus states that dharmas are "cultural adaptations that enhance group cohesion, cooperation, and predictive capacity," aligning with "evolved human tendencies toward cooperation, altruism, and social bonding" [S3][S6]. That phrasing is what turns "evolved cultural technology" from metaphor into claim, and it is licensed by the gene-culture-coevolution consensus: dual-inheritance theory (Boyd, Richerson, Henrich) holds that individual-selection / inclusive-fitness models alone cannot explain the breadth of human cooperation, and that group selection on *cultural* variation plausibly drove the genes underpinning cooperation among unrelated people [S25][S33]. D.S. Wilson's multilevel selection (MLS) is "the theory of choice" for human cultural evolution in that literature [S18], which is precisely the substrate on which "dharmas evolve as cultural adaptations" stands.

### The substrate / application relation

The corpus instantiates the substrate/application relation without ever using that exact phrase. The applied-anthropology persona (**DrAA**) answers the *general* question — "what is a dharma?" — across all cultures, subcultures and organisations, and treats secular dharma as one subset within that wider field [S1][S5]; the author makes the same move in the 24 May email, describing applied anthropology as "over 14,000 curated books, articles and transcripts spanning anthropology in its broadest sense … secular dharma is one subset within this" [S30]. The applied payoff is stated directly in the corpus: understanding a community's local dharmas lets practitioners design interventions that "fit the moral ecology" — public health, conflict mediation, technology rollouts [S3][S4]. The verbatim "substrate / application" sentence, however, is the **author's own**, sourced to the SEED, not to any corpus document; the corpus is the scaffolding that makes the claim defensible, not its statement [S30].

### The cast — each thinker tied to the evolved-cooperation thesis

**David Graeber** (anarchist anthropology / mutual aid) is present in the corpus in primary form: *Fragments of an Anarchist Anthropology* (2004), the Grubačić co-essay "Anarchism, or the Revolutionary Movement of the Twenty-First Century," and his introduction (with Grubačić) to Kropotkin's *Mutual Aid* [S7][S8][S9]. The key claim is that anarchism's basic principles — "self-organization, voluntary association, mutual aid," egalitarian decision-making, rejection of structural violence — refer to "forms of human behavior they assumed to have been around about as long as humanity," an attitude "as old as humanity" rather than an invented doctrine [S7][S8]. That is mutual aid framed as innate/evolved human behaviour — the bridge from politics to biology.

**Stephen Batchelor** (secular dharma) is present including his own "Ten Theses of Secular Dharma," the closing chapter of *After Buddhism* (Yale UP, 2015), curated into the author's keep-notes [S10][S11]. Two claims matter. First, dharma-as-cultural-technology in Batchelor's own words: "The dharma serves the needs of people at specific times and places. Each form the dharma assumes is a transient human creation, contingent upon the historical, cultural, social and economic conditions that generated it" [S10][S11]. Second, Batchelor himself grounds the ethics in evolution and reaches for Graeber's vocabulary: his theses commit the practitioner to "an ethics of care, founded on empathy, compassion and love for all creatures who have evolved on this earth" and to "understand and diminish the structural violence of societies and institutions" — "structural violence" being **not Graeber's own term but Johan Galtung's** ("Violence, Peace, and Peace Research," *Journal of Peace Research*, 1969), the standard peace-studies coinage Graeber inherited along with the rest of the left-analytic vocabulary [S44][S10][S7]. Secular dharma is defined as a "this-worldly" practical and ethical philosophy with "no use for metaphysical beliefs," whose emerging practitioners align with "egalitarianism, inclusiveness, and democratic self-rule" — the same egalitarian/anarchist constellation Graeber describes [S11].

**Peter Kropotkin** (*Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution*, 1902) is the nineteenth-century root of the whole strand, present in the corpus as full primary text — `mutual_aid.md`, the Project Gutenberg edition, also carried in [[0.2-politics-and-anarchism]] as its S4 — and framed by D.S. Wilson [S45][S12]. Kropotkin argued — against social-Darwinist competition — that mutual aid is *more natural and usual* than competition among animals and humans and is itself favoured by natural selection [S45][S26]. Wilson's *This View of Life* devotes a section, "The Anarchist," to Kropotkin: the competition emphasis "was misplaced and an artifact of British capitalism," most species live in groups whose members provide mutual aid, and "mutual aid [is] the main survival tool of humans and other animals" [S12]. Wilson lines Kropotkin up with Darwin and Huxley against the "born selfish" camp of Williams and Dawkins [S13]. This is the literal hinge of "mutual aid as a factor of evolution," not romance. ▲ Provenance note: of the pointers previously carrying that argument here, **none was Kropotkin himself** — [S26] is the encyclopaedia entry *about* the book, [S9] is Graeber and Grubačić's *introduction* to it, [S12] is Wilson's chapter on him. Only [S45] is his own text, and Stage 2 should quote from there.

**David Sloan Wilson** (*This View of Life*, 2019; *Prosocial* as practical offshoot) supplies the selective logic, present in full [S12][S27]. His MLS thesis, in the Hillel-style quip he favours: "Selfishness beats altruism within groups. Altruistic groups beat selfish groups. Everything else is commentary" — cooperation evolves at the between-group level [S12][S14]. This confirms the SEED's "Prosocial, its practical offshoot": **ProSocial World** is the applied programme (Wilson with Paul Atkins), combining Elinor Ostrom's Core Design Principles for governing the commons with Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (Hayes), generalised via evolutionary theory to make groups more cooperative — the worked, applied end of the thesis and a *secular, explicitly evolutionary sibling application* to secular dharma [S16][S17][S34].

**Christopher Boehm** (*Hierarchy in the Forest*, Harvard UP, 1999) is present per-chapter and supplies the cooperation-enforcement machinery [S19][S20]. His "reverse dominance hierarchy": humans retain an innate drive to dominance, but mobile foragers form a "moral community" that collectively suppresses upstarts via ridicule, criticism, ostracism and, at the limit, execution — egalitarianism is *imposed by the community and actively maintained* [S19][S20]. This matches the SEED's gloss that "foragers actively hold their egalitarianism in place" [S30]. Boehm coded ~50 of 150 "Late-Pleistocene-appropriate" forager societies — ▲ that LPA coding belongs to his later *Moral Origins* (2012), **not** to *Hierarchy in the Forest* (1999), which frames this paragraph — and reverse dominance with internalised prosocial morality is the pattern reported across that coded sample; ▲ the flat quantifier "**all** show" is not in the cited entry and must not be carried into Stage 2. The corpus synthesis ties the pattern to an evolved normative order ("fire and cooking coevolved with … ethical behavior") [S20].

**Sarah Hrdy** (*Mothers and Others*, Harvard UP, 2009) supplies the origin story for the cooperative instincts the dharmas later regulate [S20][S21]. Her cooperative-breeding / alloparenting thesis: humans descend from cooperatively breeding Pleistocene ancestors, and the capacity to cooperate, empathise and wonder what others are thinking arose under the selective pressure of shared childrearing — alloparenting is **ubiquitous** among foragers, with alloparents carrying much of the direct childcare, and "our uniquely prosocial shared intentionality can be traced back to … cooperative breeding" [S20][S21][S35]. ▲ The often-quoted "mothers do only about half of direct childcare" figure is **not** a pan-forager statistic: it derives from particular ethnographic cases (Efé, Aka), and no entry cited here supplies the number. Stage 2 should either name those societies or keep to the ubiquity claim, which is sourced. Hrdy is a deliberate counterweight to the "bellicose school," arguing that "prioritizing sharing over competition … is the only viable way to sustain the rapid evolution of human intelligence" [S21]. This matches the SEED's "where our cooperative instincts come from" [S30].

**Robert Sapolsky** (*Behave*, Penguin Press, 2017) is the connective-tissue text, present in full plus a summary transcript [S22][S23]. *Behave* explains behaviour — cooperation, aggression, in-group/out-group morality — across nested timescales (neurons → hormones → development → genes → culture → "evolutionary pressures played out over the prior millions of years"), refusing single-discipline tunnel vision: "brains and cultures coevolve" and moral intuitions are biological and historically contingent [S22][S23][S36]. Sapolsky endorses neo-group/multilevel selection as frequent and consequential in humans (citing Bowles's "altruism's midwife"), linking him directly to Wilson's MLS frame [S24].

**Supporting cast** named in the SEED and present in the corpus: Joseph Henrich, *The Secret of Our Success* (culture-gene coevolution, alloparenting) [S25][S33]; Richard Wrangham, *The Goodness Paradox* (self-domestication — humans reduced reactive but retained proactive aggression, the failure-mode counterweight) [S43][S37]; Rutger Bregman, *Humankind* (the optimistic register of the same evolved-cooperation case) [S38]; James C. Scott, *Against the Grain* (early states as coercive/fragile, the anarchist-anthropology bridge to deep history) [S39]; Lisa Feldman Barrett, *How Emotions Are Made* (constructed emotion — affect is built from physiology, concepts and culture, so a dharma's affective conditioning does real work) [S40]; and Frans de Waal–style accounts of empathy, fairness and reciprocity in the prosocial corpus [S41].

### The synthesis the corpus licenses

Mutual aid (Kropotkin/Graeber), egalitarian self-rule (Boehm), cooperative care (Hrdy) and the biology of social behaviour (Sapolsky) all describe one evolved system: a cooperative primate that must continually *manufacture and police* cooperation, with Wilson's MLS giving the selective logic and the corpus's dharma essays giving the cultural-technology vocabulary [S2][S12][S19][S21][S22]. "Secular dharma" (Batchelor) and "mutual aid" (Graeber) are thus two registers — contemplative-ethical versus political-anthropological — for the same machinery, which is precisely the author's stated thesis [S30]. The shared vocabulary is the most visible sign of this, but ▲ **not proof of it**: Batchelor's own theses and Graeber's own text do converge on "structural violence" and on an evolved "ethics of care" [S10][S7], yet "structural violence" is Galtung's 1969 term and common currency across the left-analytic literature both men read [S44]. Inherited idiom cannot carry the convergence; the substantive claims can.

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The lean *is* the subject here, not a distortion.** The shared corpus carries the well-known secular / evolutionary / Western-academic tilt (curated around Boehm, Sapolsky, Graeber, Batchelor, Bregman, Wilson). For most notes that tilt is a caveat; for this note it is the thing being described — the author's deliberate curatorial thesis. So the usual "the corpus tilts secular" flag is self-referential, and the honest flag is the *inverse*: the corpus almost entirely omits thinkers who would resist reducing dharma to evolved cooperation.
- **The non-reductive counter-voice the corpus does not supply.** In classical Hindu thought *dharma* is rooted in **ṛta**, the cosmic order/truth the moral and ritual order participate in; in Mīmāṃsā and Dharmaśāstra, dharma is what the Veda *enjoins* — duty disclosed by revealed scripture (*śruti*), not a contingent human invention selected for group fitness. In Theravāda Buddhism the **Dhamma** is presented as a discovered law of reality (*dhammatā*) the Buddha *re-discovered* rather than constructed [S42]. A traditionalist reading therefore treats the evolved-cultural-technology account as, at best, a description of dharma's *social effects* that misses its claimed *truth-tracking / normative-binding* character — exactly the reduction Batchelor's secular dharma performs and orthodox readers reject. This balance is encyclopaedic-level here, included to *flag* that the corpus omits it; a Stage-2 essay would need primary traditional sources (Dharmaśāstra, Mīmāṃsā, a Theravāda doctrinal statement) to do it justice [S42].
- **The "self-aware" honesty of the author's own essay.** Notably, the author does not over-claim: *In Search of Dharma* deliberately leaves open whether godless dharmas can genuinely *bind* and whether all dharmas warrant equal ethical standing — the author flagging, in public, the very tension this note records [S32].
- **A heavy self-referential / AI-generated layer in the corpus.** Several of the "definition" documents that supply the cultural-technology vocabulary (`Dharma_What-is-it_o3.md`, `Dharma-according-to-Grok-4.md`, `why-is-the-concept-of-dharma-important_*`, `what-is-dharma.md`) are themselves LLM-authored corpus essays, some produced by the sibling *DefiningDharma* pipeline. They are corroborative of the *framing* — they are effectively the house articulation — but are **not** independent scholarship and should not be read as evidence. The citable backbone is the primary-text layer: Graeber, Boehm, Wilson, Sapolsky, Batchelor's theses, `zoonpolitikon`, Henrich, Wrangham [S1][S2][S3][S4][S5][S6].
- **The substrate/application sentence and the Graeber-beside-Batchelor "thesis" are self-reported.** They are sourced to the SEED — the author's bio and the private Margolis email — not to any corpus document or third-party source. This is honest provenance, not a defect: the framing is *meant* to be the author's, and the corpus thinkers supply support for it, not the claim itself [S30].
- **The evolved-cooperation story is internally contested even within the corpus, and should not be presented as frictionless.** D.S. Wilson's optimism is checked inside the prosocial corpus by Folbre (MLS underrates intra-group conflict; "coerced cooperation"; cooperation "not always voluntary, democratic or egalitarian") and by Mercier & Sperber (religion as a by-product, not, *pace* Wilson, an adaptation) [S28][S29]. Wrangham's self-domestication thesis is the same caution from the biology side: the cooperative primate is also the coalition-killing primate [S43][S37]. These belong in any treatment of dharmic failure modes / tribalism.
