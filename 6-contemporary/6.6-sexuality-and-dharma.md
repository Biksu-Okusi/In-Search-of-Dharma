# 6.6 Sexuality & Dharma

**Question:** How do dharmas regulate, use and give meaning to sex — between individuals
(the pair-bond, marriage, celibacy, pleasure) and within communities (incest rules, marriage
alliance, purity and modesty, the policing of desire, sex as social glue)? Where has sexual
regulation held people without crushing them, where has the sexual arena become the site of a
dharma's worst abuses, and what do the two tests of [`the-better-ones.md`](../the-better-ones.md)
— *holds without crushing*, *revisable by those bound* — say about each?

## Synthesis
Sex is the first thing every dharma regulates and the last thing it fully controls. The
pair-bond, the innate aversion to sex with those one grew up beside, and the use of sex to
bond and to make peace are all older than any code: they are visible in our primate cousins
and in every human marriage system, from the polygynous Turkana to the "marriage-phobic" Na
[S1][S2][S3][S4]. What a dharma adds is to make mating a community matter — whom one may
marry, how many, whose child counts, where the couple lives — and, by labelling distant kin
"brother" and "sister", it turns an innate aversion into a taboo that can be stretched to build
alliances between groups [S5][S6][S7][S37]. On that base the traditions divide along the axis
Weber mapped: orgy or chastity, the renouncer's war on "the peculiar irrationality of the
sexual act" or the householder's sacralising of marriage and offspring, with the ascetic strand
"generally terminating in hostility toward sexuality" [S8]. Yet hostile and affirming strands
coexist inside the same traditions: *kāma* stands among the three *puruṣārthas* in the very
culture whose monastic code makes intercourse the first ground of expulsion; the Samin made sex
sacred and defiant *and* pledged one wife for life [S10][S15][S23][S25][S39][S42]. Purity and
modesty norms are best read as pre-emptive policing of self-control fused with mate-guarding,
carrying a durable double standard against women [S13][S17][S18]; the sexual arena is therefore
where the "holds without crushing" test most often fails, and where the procedural test is
decisive: the teacher-abuse scandals of modern Buddhism are, structurally, authority that the
student had vowed never to revoke [S29][A]. The secular turn — from public sin to private
consent, from reproductive to "plastic" sexuality — is real and recent [S27][S50][S52], and its
one remaining rule, consent, is now contested from within [S55][A].

## Findings

### 1. The substrate: sex before any dharma

- **The pair-bond is a species trait, not a cultural choice.** Christakis's survey of marriage
  systems finds that "even the strongest ecological or cultural forces rarely, if ever,
  overcome one core feature of human relationships": the pair-bond between sexual partners,
  "seen in cultures with all marriage types, and that people experience as love. The drive to
  love your partner is universal" [S1:175]. An "*enduring* and *sentimental* sexual association,
  whether monogamous, polygynous, polyandrous, or homosexual, is the core feature that
  differentiates the sexual practice of our species from most other animals"; social monogamy is
  common in birds (about 90% of species) and rare among mammals, and the leading account ties
  it to selection for paternal care [S1:178]. The bonding circuitry is borrowed: the mother–infant
  attachment system "was apparently modified among certain species (including our own) so that
  females came to feel about their partners as they did about their babies, taking advantage of
  aspects of sex to establish or maintain a bond" [S1:195]. ▲ This is a claim about bonding, not
  the "oxytocin as parochial drug" claim that Part 5 of the book withdrew; the same caution about
  the intranasal literature applies (see [[5.1-neurological-basis]]) [A].
- **Marriage systems track ecology; the bond does not.** The relatively egalitarian Hadza are
  monogamous "in part because women are less dependent on men for food"; among the pastoral
  Turkana, cattle create inequality and polygyny, and marriage "functions to create labor units"
  [S1:175]. Across the anthropological record "approximately 85 per cent of human societies have
  permitted men to have more than one wife" [S33]. Polygyny became *more* common with agriculture
  and the state, and monogamy's return as a norm — "first in the West (beginning two thousand
  years ago)" — was cultural, not evolutionary [S1:137]. Normative monogamy, Henrich, Boyd and
  Richerson argue, "reduces crime rates, including rape, murder, assault, robbery and fraud" by
  shrinking the pool of unmarried men, and "increases savings, child investment and economic
  productivity" by shifting male effort from mate-seeking to paternal investment [S33].
- **The limit case: a society without marriage.** The Na of Yunnan practise "a system of furtive
  or conspicuous night visits during which a man goes to a woman's home"; partners "are
  independent and live separately with no exclusive sexual or economic claims" [S38][S1:171].
  Cai Hua's reading, which Christakis endorses, is that humans carry two desires that cannot both
  be institutionalised — to possess one's partner and to have multiple partners — so a society
  must choose "possession without the pleasure of diversity or the pleasure of diversity without
  possession"; among the Na it is fidelity and exclusivity that are the "forbidden" and therefore
  exciting desires, and couples still elope to form them [S1:171]. Even the kibbutz, which set
  out to dissolve the couple and the nuclear family, found "gender distinctions and pair-bonding
  between spouses … not easy to efface", and the family re-emerged after the 1950s [S1:81].
- **Sex as social glue: the bonobo evidence.** Sapolsky's lectures set the twentieth-century
  orthodoxy — that "sexual behavior is about reproduction", confined to ovulation — against
  bonobos, whose sexual behaviour serves "a very similar role" to grooming, "all about social
  cohesion, as well as decreasing individual tensions, reconciliation" [S2:240,243]. Wrangham
  reports that female bonobos "use homosexual interactions to diffuse tensions among
  themselves", the genito-genital rubbing known in the Congo as *hoka-hoka*, and reads the
  species' same-sex behaviour as a by-product of self-domestication that "has now been co-opted
  and evolved into a useful behavior" [S3:120,227]. At Wamba, when two bonobo groups met at a
  sugarcane bait, the males stayed aloof but "the females crossed to the other group and
  copulated both with other females and sometimes with the males" — the opposite of the
  chimpanzee border display [S4]. De Waal's early popular statement of the same finding is
  subtitled "The behavior of a close relative challenges assumptions about male supremacy in
  human evolution" [S49]. ▲ Bonobos are a sister species, not an ancestor; what they show is that
  non-reproductive, bonding sex is within the hominid repertoire, not that humans inherited the
  bonobo pattern [A].
- **Innate incest aversion (the Westermarck effect).** Shepher's kibbutz study reported "no cases
  of marriage between any two members of the same peer group", and "the avoidance was completely
  voluntary" [S34]; Wolf's Taiwanese data on minor marriages found that "women who marry a
  childhood associate bear fewer children, divorce more frequently, and are more often
  adulterous" [S35]. ▲ Shor and Simchai's re-study of the kibbutzim found that people raised
  communally were often attracted to their peers and that sexual *aversion* was rare, so the
  kibbutz leg of the evidence is contested [S36]. The sheep, monkey and human data Sapolsky
  reviews point the same way as Westermarck: early experience shapes not *how* to mate but
  *with whom* [S2:227].

### 2. The first thing a dharma does with sex: make it the community's business

- **Marriage recruits third parties.** "Marriage, often with its accompanying rituals and gift
  exchanges, involves the community in a couple's pair-bond. That is, community members are
  third parties who monitor (gossip about) and potentially sanction those who violate marriage
  norms." The norms govern "(1) whom one can marry (e.g., incest taboos), (2) how many partners
  one can marry (polygamy?), (3) what the inheritance rights are and who a 'legitimate' heir is,
  (4) where the new couple will live" [S5:178].
- **From aversion to taboo.** Henrich's Fijian vignette shows the mechanism: villagers were more
  alarmed by *classificatory* siblings talking alone than by real siblings, because "innate
  incest aversion was prophylactic" for the latter while "for classificatory siblings, the only
  effective prophylactic was the continuous monitoring and potential wrath of the community".
  "Incest taboos are social norms that evolved culturally to regulate sex and pair-bonding
  between non-close relatives by harnessing innate intuitions … By harnessing innate incest
  aversion and labeling distant relatives as 'brothers' and 'sisters,' cultural evolution seized
  a powerful lever to control human behavior" [S5:187]. This is the evolutionary restatement of
  Lévi-Strauss's thesis that the incest prohibition is the rule that founds exchange: it pushes
  groups toward exogamy and produces a "circulation of women" that links them into a society
  [S37].
- **Alliance as the flow of life.** Clastres found local exogamy near-universal among the
  Tropical Forest tribes and read it as "an opening … to the outside, to the other communities":
  groups "engaged in the exchange of women (where residence is patrilocal) or of sons-in-law
  (where it is matrilocal)" cannot also assert "extreme independence", which "implies a mutual
  hostility that can quickly develop into war" [S6:59,60]. The eastern-Indonesian material shows
  the rule at its most elaborate: in Thie (Rote) one clan is forbidden marriage only with itself
  while another is barred from ten others, leaving "at least 15 other clans with whom marriage is
  possible" [S7:240]; among the Ema, "no marriage can occur between the eastern core houses,
  between the western core houses, or between the eastern and the western core houses without
  going through the chiefly core houses" — a four-partner cycle of "the circulation of women"
  [S7:267]. Weber's sociology treats exogamy as the minimal content of kin membership, bundled
  with totemic food taboos and blood revenge [S8:652].
- **Endogamy as the other lever.** Where exogamy builds alliance, endogamy builds hierarchy.
  Dumont concluded that in Hindu India "endogamy is, in modern times, only an average and general
  result at one level or another of the principle of hierarchy" [S9:178]; O'Malley's Malabar case
  — a Nambudiri Brahmin's adultery with a toddy-tapper woman, the man blinded, her close
  relations killed or sold — shows the sanction at its extreme [S9:390]. Manu makes the stake
  explicit: when men violate others' wives, "the king should disfigure their bodies with
  punishments that inspire terror and then execute them; for such violations give rise to the
  mixing of social classes among the people, creating deviation from the Law that tears out the
  very root and leads to the destruction of everything" (8.352–353) [S10:335]; a Brahmin who has
  sex with a Caṇḍāla woman "falls from his caste" (11.176) [S10:415]. Ambedkar drew the inverse
  conclusion from the same mechanism: "The real remedy for breaking Caste is intermarriage.
  Nothing else will serve as the solvent of Caste" [S31:60].
- **Honour.** In honour cultures "a woman who has sexual relations outside marriage, even against
  her will, defiles her family, which is shamed and shunned. Other families will not marry
  members of the defiled family" — Rai and Fiske's "Unity" motive, experienced as disgust
  [S12:17]. The Buddhist texts on nuns rest on the same assumption: Collins notes that in the
  Indian setting "sexual transgressions are committed by men not directly against a woman but
  against those who either 'own' her or are in some other way legally responsible for her", and
  Findly that women's sexuality "can be controlled, primarily through the institution of
  marriage" [S13:157]. The lay precept against sexual misconduct is drafted on exactly this map:
  misconduct is relations "with women who have their mother, father, both mother and father,
  brother, sister, relatives, or clan as guardian … or who has a husband, or whose violation is
  punishable by law, or even one who has been garlanded as a token of betrothal" (AN 10.176; the
  same list at MN 41) [S41]. The wrong is defined by whose protection is breached, not by the
  woman's consent [A].

### 3. The renouncer: sex as the central ascetic problem

- **Weber's map.** "The relationship of religion to sexuality is extraordinarily intimate";
  sexual love is "among the most fundamental and universal components of the actual course of
  interpersonal behavior" [S8:964]. At one pole stands the orgy — "sexual intoxication is a
  typical component of the orgy, the religious behavior of the laity at a primitive level",
  retained "quite directly and by calculation" in Śākta religion, and the root of the notion
  "that sexual surrender has a religious meritoriousness" [S8:965]. At the other stands
  asceticism: "Rational ascetic alertness, self-control, and methodical planning of life are
  threatened the most by the peculiar irrationality of the sexual act, which is ultimately and
  uniquely unsusceptible to rational organization." All prophetic and priestly systematisations
  "concern themselves with sexuality from such motives … generally terminating in hostility
  toward sexuality" [S8:967]. Religion first seeks to eliminate the orgy, then "all free sexual
  relationships in the interest of the religious regulation and legitimation of marriage";
  Islam proscribed the extra-marital forms "with a success scarcely duplicated elsewhere", and
  even the world-accommodating Confucian ethic "viewed irregular sexual expression as an
  inferior irrationality" that disturbed the gentleman's equilibrium [S8:967].
- **The Indian renouncer.** The world-renouncing strands of yoga hold that "anything which has
  to do with satisfaction of the desires in the body will increase the bonds which tie the subtle
  forms of our existence to the material plane", "the sexual desire being the most powerful"
  [S14]. The Buddhist monastic code makes this concrete: the first *pārājika*, the offence that
  expels a monk, is sexual intercourse — "If a monk … has sexual intercourse, even with a female
  animal, he is expelled and excluded from the community" — and its origin story is Sudinna's
  intercourse with his former wife at his family's urging to produce an heir [S42]. The term for
  the ideal religious life itself, *brahmacariya*, is the word for celibacy [S13:165]. The Jain
  layman's fourth vow, *brahma-vrata*, is the householder's version — "marriage and moderation":
  he should "avoid the wives of others" and be "content with his own wife", and Jaini notes that
  Jains see this restraint "as simply one more facet in the universal implementation of ahiṃsā"
  [S15].
- **The Christian renouncer.** Peter Brown's *The Body and Society* traces permanent sexual
  renunciation — continence, celibacy, lifelong virginity — through Christian circles from the
  first to the fifth centuries, from Tertullian and Origen to the Desert Fathers, Jerome,
  Ambrose and Augustine [S47]. Weber's summary: "The religious preaching of Jesus, with its
  demand of absolute and indissoluble monogamy, went beyond all other religions in the
  limitations imposed upon permissible and legitimate sexuality"; in the earliest church
  "adultery and whoredom were almost regarded as the only absolute mortal sins"; the
  once-married *univira* was "the hallmark of the Christian community"; and the misogyny of the
  monastery meant that "a particular woman might be regarded as sacred, yet the entire female sex
  would still be considered vessels of sin" [S8:968]. Clerical celibacy became law for the Latin
  church at the Second Lateran Council of 1139, whose canon 7 declared the marriages of bishops,
  priests, deacons, subdeacons and monks not to be marriages at all [S48]. Luther, in Weber's
  reading, kept the ascetic valuation even while abolishing the monastery, treating sex within
  marriage as "a lesser evil enjoined for the avoidance of whoredom" [S8:969].
- **The householder's counter-doctrine: the duty to beget.** "Legally regulated marriage itself
  was regarded by both prophetic and priestly ethics, not as an erotic value, but … simply as an
  economic institution for the production and rearing of children as a labor force and
  subsequently as carriers of the cult of the dead" [S8:969]. Mencius states the Confucian form:
  "There are three things which are unfilial, and to have no posterity is the greatest of them"
  (4A26) [S46]. Judaism, Confucianism and Vedic ethics "culminated in the direct religious
  obligation to beget children"; Talmudic Judaism and Islam enjoined marriage partly on the view
  "that sexual drives are absolutely irresistible for the average man, for whom it is better
  that a legally regulated channel of expression be made available" [S8:969]. Celibacy and
  fertility are thus not two religions but two settings of the same dial, and a dharma may run
  both at once by assigning them to different orders — monk and householder [A].
- **Celibacy as the costliest signal.** Sosis and Bressler's commune data show religious
  communes imposing "more than twice as many requirements on their members as secular communes"
  and lasting far longer (median 25 years against 5) [S16:13]; the costly requirement that
  binds hardest, celibacy, also prevents a group from reproducing its membership — the Shaker
  case already recorded in [[5.2-evolutionary-psychology]] [A].

### 4. Sex as a good inside the rule: pleasure, bond, sacrament, defiance

- ***Kāma* as one of the three.** Dharma, *artha* and *kāma* form the *trivarga*; *mokṣa* was
  added later, and the "scholarly consensus is that the original trivarga became expanded into
  four with the addition of mokṣa" [S39]. ▲ Olivelle warns that *puruṣārtha* is "often
  mistranslated as 'aims of man'"; the three are domains of beneficial human activity rather than
  goals, so "aims of life" should not be presented as uncontested [S39]. The *Kāmasūtra* ranks
  them only in conflict — "When these three aims—religion, power, and pleasure—compete, each is
  more important than the one that follows" (1.2.14) — and immediately excepts the king and the
  courtesan, for whom "power, in the form of wealth, is the most important goal" (1.2.15) [S40].
  Pleasure is thus a legitimate domain with its own textbook, ranked third but not condemned
  [A].
- **The transgressive path.** Alongside the renouncer, "within Hindu and Buddhist thinking there
  is a Tantric strand, sometimes called the left-handed path … Tantrism takes the position that
  to realise the fullness of our potential we need to transmute rather than subordinate the
  energies and desires which spring naturally within us" [S14]. Samuel describes "a
  'transgressive' tradition of rituals involving cremation grounds, polluting substances
  associated with sex and death", in which initiation "involved the consumption of the 'clan
  essence', the mixed sexual secretions of male guru and female consort", later internalised as
  yogic practice "which have a marked sexual component"; White's *Kiss of the Yoginī* reads the
  early cult as "the exchange of powerful, transformative sexual fluids" between practitioners
  and the *yoginīs*, and his answer to whether this has anything to do with the "Tantric sex"
  marketed in the West is "there is none" [S45]. China's oldest guides to intercourse as
  cultivation, *He yin yang* and *Tianxia zhi dao tan*, come from Mawangdui tomb 3, sealed in
  168 BCE [S44].
- **Sex as sacred among the *wong sikep*.** Samin Surosentiko called his teaching "the science of
  the prophet Adam" and explained that *nabi* meant "woman" and *adam* "man"; "such
  identifications point to the central importance of sex and the magical power inherent in it
  for the Saminists. They called their wives 'sikep', and themselves 'wong sikep', meaning those
  who embrace" [S23:25]. Benda and Castles read the third line of the Samin formula as "Woman-man,
  i.e. the sexual act or relationship, shall exist till the end of time"; "when called upon to
  perform the village duty of nightwatching (*ronda malam*), the Saminists used to point to their
  sexual organs and say that they had a duty to their wives at night", calling "upon the magical
  power known to them in sex, to strengthen them in their doubtless worrying defiance of
  authority" [S23:27]. The same observers recorded that "the Saminists accorded their women far
  more respect than was true of their *abangan* neighbours, an attitude quite in accordance not
  only with the movement's stress on matters marital and sexual but also with its egalitarian
  notions in general" [S23:29] — and the ethic Jasper was given included "do not commit
  adultery" [S23:27]. Dangir's 1928 initiation formula defines husband and wife by reciprocal
  sexual-marital duty (see [[4.1-samin-of-java]]) [S24], and the customary rite still carries a
  lifetime-monogamy pledge, *bojo siji kangge saklawase*, one spouse for ever [S25:21]. Here sex
  is at once sacred, political and strictly bounded: the clearest case in the corpus of a dharma
  that affirms sex without licensing it [A].
- **Java's sacred erotic.** The *Serat Centhini*, compiled from 1814 at the Surakarta court under
  the crown prince who became Pakubuwana V (Day dates the commission to 1815, under
  Ronggasutrasna, Yasadipura II and Sastradipura), carries explicit sexual episodes inside a
  Sufi-Javanese mystical encyclopaedia, read by Kristianto as an "erotic spirituality" [S63]. At
  Gunung Kemukus in Sragen the pilgrimage to the grave of Pangeran Samodro requires, by the
  pilgrims' own account, that they "perform sexual intercourse (copulate) for seven consecutive
  times every Friday Pon, with a person who is not their spouse" to obtain fortune, and "in its
  development, the pilgrimage turns into sexual activity with prostitutes"; after SBS Dateline's
  "Sex Mountain" (18 November 2014) the governor of Central Java told pilgrims "Please do make
  visits to the tomb, but don't engage in free sex there", the karaoke bars were raided, and the
  site was rebuilt as "New Kemukus" (2020–2022) [S64]. Here sex is neither bond nor pleasure but
  a transaction with the sacred, and the state's answer was to keep the shrine and remove the
  sex — the purity register overwriting the older orgiastic one, in Weber's terms [A].
- **Bali: the rule relaxed in practice.** Covarrubias noted that Balinese high priests "do not
  observe sexual abstinence, although it is recommended in the scriptures" [S20:637], and that
  "the idea of homosexuality is inconsequential to the Balinese" [S20:336] — a reminder that a
  code's sexual rules and a community's sexual life can run at different settings [A].
- **Islamic marriage as contract with rights on both sides.** Hallaq shows that the classical
  marriage contract let women stipulate "the right to work outside the marital home; to divorce
  her husband; to forbid him from taking a second wife; or, on penalty of divorce, to prohibit
  him from moving the marital home" [S22:152]; the husband's unilateral divorce "paid a price",
  since it "amounted to a one-way transfer of property from the husband to the wife" [S22:79];
  and Tunisia in 1956 criminalised polygamy outright "on the grounds that it is impossible for
  any man to be just … to two wives" [S22:157]. Indonesian Muslim women's organisations argue
  the same ground from inside the tradition: 'Aisyiyah's Nurhidayani that polygamy is "almost
  always impossible … to be fair" while "women have equal rights in *mu'asharah* (the mutual
  relation of husband and wife), to enjoy their relationship"; Rifka Annisa that of 1,062
  requests to register polygamous marriages in 2002 "only about 50 were granted", and that men
  enter them "by forging their identity documents … forging the first wife's letter of consent"
  [S21:229,230].
- **The Abrahamic affirmation inside the rule.** The hostility Weber mapped is not the whole of
  the Abrahamic record. A hadith in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* counts intercourse with one's wife as alms —
  "in man's sexual intercourse (with his wife) there is a sadaqa" — and answers the Companions'
  surprise that satisfying desire could be rewarded: "if he were to devote it to something
  forbidden, would it not be a sin on his part? Similarly, if he were to devote it to something
  lawful, he should have a reward" [S32]. Al-Ghazālī's book on the etiquette of marriage makes
  the wife's pleasure the husband's duty: "Once the husband has attained his fulfillment, let him
  tarry until his wife also attains hers … for to satisfy her is his duty" [S59]. Jewish law reads
  Exodus 21:10 — a husband "must not withhold from this one her food, her clothing, or her
  conjugal rights" — as the obligation of *onah*, which the Mishnah schedules by occupation: "Men
  of leisure … every day, laborers … twice a week, donkey drivers once a week, camel drivers once
  every thirty days, and sailors once every six months" [S60]. The Song of Songs, "an emphatically
  secular book: the name of God is never once mentioned", sits in the canon on Rabbi Akiva's word
  that "all the writings are holy but the Song of Songs is the holy of holies"; as the Blochs put
  it, "sex is no sin in the Old Testament" [S61]. Even *Humanae Vitae*, the document that fixed
  the Catholic ban on contraception, does so by insisting on "the inseparable connection …
  between the unitive significance and the procreative significance which are both inherent to
  the marriage act" — naming union as a good while refusing to let it be pursued apart from
  fertility [S62]. The affirming and the ascetic strands are not two religions but two voices
  inside each; the question for the floor is which voice the bound may invoke against the other
  [A].

### 5. Policing desire: purity, modesty and the double standard

- **What puritanism is for.** Fitouchi, André and Baumard argue that puritanical norms —
  against lust, intoxicants, immodesty, idleness — are pre-emptive policing of *self-control*
  in the service of cooperation, and that these concerns "co-occur and cohere in the most
  culturally successful moral traditions, which cover almost 80% of the world's population"
  [S17:7]. Modesty rules are "preventive" or "situational" self-control strategies that lower
  the demand on willpower "by limiting exposure to sexual cues" [S17:29,51]; the moralisation of
  pornography tracks the belief that it is addictive [S17:42]. They accept that mate-guarding
  "surely underly many sexual restrictions" — "veiling, virginity tests, female claustration,
  and menstrual taboos" — and that it "is consistent with the frequent double standard favoring
  men in the moralization of sexuality", while arguing that mate-guarding alone cannot explain
  the condemnation of gluttony or idleness [S17:19]. The Dharmasūtra of Vasiṣṭha supplies the
  menstrual taboo in its classical form: "people feel disgust towards her and her condition,
  thinking 'Let her not come near!'" [S11:384].
- **Purity is not one thing.** Gray and colleagues' review of thirty years of purity research
  finds nine distinct understandings under one word, "a contra-chimera", from pathogen avoidance
  to metaphysical concerns [S18:3,60]; "sexual chastity has featured heavily within the
  development of the purity concept", with Haidt's early vignettes of siblings kissing and
  Rozin's of incest and age-gap sex [S18:45]. Purity violations differ from harms in what they
  signal: "a fundamental break from basic social norms", a defect of character rather than a
  lapse [S18:78]. The "Julie and Mark" sibling-incest vignette and the "moral dumbfounding" it
  produces — "the stubborn and puzzled maintenance of a moral judgment without supporting
  reasons" — remain the standard demonstration that sexual judgments arrive before argument
  [S19]; Royzman and colleagues contest how dumbfounded the subjects really were [S19].
- **The double standard in the codes.** Manu's adultery law defines the offence by the man's
  approach to another man's wife — secret conversation, dallying, "touching the ornaments or
  clothes, and sitting together on a bed" (8.354–358) [S10:335] — and confines the adulterous
  wife "in a single room" (11.177) [S10:415]; the Jain lay vow "speak[s] directly to men, who are
  clearly considered much more prone to sexual license than are women" [S15]. Goldman's
  nineteenth-century polemic names the secular form: the working man "marries only to have a
  wife and house-keeper", and the woman is "chained to the house by a thousand duties" with no
  equivalent licence to recreation [S28]. The Buddhist nuns' order was placed under the monks on
  the model of "male relatives protecting their women" [S13:165]; Batchelor reads the eight
  "heavy rules" as "inserted into the canon by patriarchal hard-liners at a fairly early date"
  while the same passage "endorses an equal role for men and women adherents" [S30:16] (see
  [[6.4-gender-and-dharma]]).
- **Same-sex desire.** The Vinaya bars the *paṇḍaka* from ordination — "A paṇḍaka shouldn't be
  given the full ordination. If it has been given, he should be expelled" (Mahāvagga 1.61) —
  and Zwilling's survey sets out Buddhaghosa's five types [S43]; Manu prescribes penances for a
  twice-born who "has sexual intercourse with a man" (11.174–175) [S10:415]. Against this,
  Covarrubias's Bali and the Muslim minority in Indonesia who argue "that same-sex love is a gift
  of God, not a crime or act of violence against others" — "who in this case can determine
  whether something is natural or not?" [S20:336][S21:232]. The archipelago's older institutions
  ran on different maps: the Bugis counted five genders — *oroané*, *makkunrai*, *calalai*,
  *calabai* and the *bissu*, "an order of shamans who embody female and male elements" — until the
  DI/TII years, when ritual objects were burned or sunk and *bissu* were forced to live as men
  [S65]; and Ponorogo's *warok* kept boy companions, *gemblak*, in the tradition Wilson titles
  "spirituality, sexuality, and power" [S66]. Wrangham's evidence that
  domestication "may have regularly led to the evolution of increased homosexual behavior" in
  sheep, non-human primates and humans, and that "women and men in single-sex institutions such
  as prisons, schools, monasteries, and ships often temporarily shift their sexual activity
  toward their own sex" [S3:227,232], undercuts any code that treats same-sex desire as outside
  nature; the Netherlands opened civil marriage to same-sex couples on 1 April 2001, the first
  state to do so [S56]. ▲ Whether a dharma *should* make room is a value question the floor in
  [`the-better-ones.md`](../the-better-ones.md) answers procedurally: a rule the excluded cannot
  contest fails the keystone test [A].
- **Sexual slander as a weapon of the state.** The Suharto regime's founding story — "that the
  seven officers were tortured and mutilated by crowds of ecstatic PKI supporters, while women
  from Gerwani (the Indonesian Women's Movement) danced naked" — "was an absurd fabrication by
  psychological warfare experts"; the castration story "turned out to be a fabrication" [S26:67,
  112]. Anticommunism then became "the state religion, complete with sacred sites, rituals, and
  dates" [S26:8]. Wieringa traces the slander to the army paper *Berita Yudha* of 11 October 1965
  — eyes "gouged out", genitals "cut off" — and to "army-derived reports" of "women dancing
  naked", and calls it "the spark without which the bloodbath would not have happened"; the
  forensic autopsies, published by Anderson in 1987, found that "the genitals of the generals were
  intact, all eyes were in place, and there were no traces of cuts with razors" [S67][S68]. A
  sexual-purity panic was the pretext for the killing of hundreds of thousands:
  the purity register is not a private matter but a political instrument [A] (see
  [[7.3-dharma-and-power]], [[7.1-failure-modes]]).

### 6. When the dharma's authority meets sex: abuse

- **The structural account from inside Buddhism.** In 1993 twenty-two Western Buddhist
  teachers met the Dalai Lama in Dharamsala "to discuss how to address issues of ethical
  misconduct among Buddhist teachers … In most cases, this had to do with alleged sexual abuse";
  one teacher named was Sogyal Rinpoche, "who in August 2017 was forced to resign as the head of
  the Rigpa community because of these and other allegations" [S29:6]. The Dalai Lama's point
  was that "it is thus the student who invests the teacher with authority. For the teacher then
  to use that authority to take advantage of the student for his own personal ends constitutes a
  profound betrayal"; in the Vajrayāna, "since you have vowed not to criticise the guru and have
  been instructed to regard whatever he says or does as the enlightened activity of a buddha,
  there is nothing, in principle, that he could not ask of you" [S29:6]. Batchelor's diagnosis:
  "The problem is systemic. It lies in the synergy of tantric doctrines and feudal structures
  that allow a teacher to legitimate abusive behaviour to himself and his students" [S29:7]. The
  meeting's open letter said what the floor says: "No matter what level of spiritual attainment a
  teacher has, or claims to have reached, no person can stand above the norms of ethical
  conduct" — and "neither the Dalai Lama nor any of the other Tibetans present at the meeting
  signed it" [S29:7,8]. When the eight Rigpa students went public in 2017, senior lamas "appeared
  to be more concerned about the students breaking their oath of allegiance (*samaya*) to their
  teacher than about the suffering of those who said they had been mistreated"; one "denounced
  them as agents of demonic forces" [S29:8]. The students' own words: "We no longer want to
  indulge in the stupidity of seeing the guru as perfect at any cost" [S29:9].
- **The documented cases.** *San Francisco Zen Center, 1983.* "In April of this year, Zen Center's
  Abbot, Richard Baker-roshi, began an indefinite leave of absence. The precipitating event … was
  his relationship with a married resident woman student"; the community's own journal added
  that "he had been involved in similar situations before" and that the crisis raised wider
  questions of "size, finances, decision making". He resigned on 20 December 1983 "with deep
  regret and shame" [S69]. *Rigpa, 2017–2018.* A letter of 14 July 2017 from eight students set
  out the abuse; Sogyal retired "with immediate effect" on 11 August 2017; the independent
  report by Karen Baxter of Lewis Silkin (22 August 2018) found "on the balance of probabilities"
  that "some students … (who were part of the 'inner circle' …) have been subjected to serious
  physical, sexual and emotional abuse by him; and … there were senior individuals within Rigpa
  who were aware of at least some of these issues and failed to address them"; the Charity
  Commission's inquiry followed in 2020 [S70]. *Shambhala, 2018–2019.* Andrea Winn's Buddhist
  Project Sunshine report (February 2018) was followed by the Sakyong's letter of 10 July 2018 —
  "I feel that I must, at this time, step back from my administrative and teaching
  responsibilities" — and by the Wickwire Holm investigation (released 3 February 2019), which
  found that "the Sakyong's behavior in the 1990s and up to 2005 included frequent sexual contact
  with women who were his students and, thus, characterized by a power imbalance" [S71]. *The
  Catholic Church.* The John Jay study for the US bishops found allegations against 4,392 priests
  — 4.0% of the 109,694 priests active between 1950 and 2002 — from 10,667 individuals [S72];
  Australia's Royal Commission reported that of 4,029 survivors who described abuse in religious
  institutions, 2,489 (61.8%) were abused in Catholic institutions, and that of 9,025 priests who
  ministered between 1950 and 2010, 507 were alleged perpetrators — seven per cent, weighted by
  duration of ministry [S73]. The shape is the same in each: sexual access taken under an
  authority the victim had been taught not to question, inside an institution whose first reflex
  was to protect itself [A].
- **Reading the cases with the two tests.** Each is a failure of *holds without crushing* in the
  most literal sense, but the deeper failure is procedural: the vow of *samaya*, the "heavy
  rules", the seal of the confessional and the deference owed to a *roshi* or a *kyai* are all
  devices by which the bound renounce the power to revise. Sexual abuse by religious authority is
  therefore the sharpest available evidence for the keystone test in
  [`the-better-ones.md`](../the-better-ones.md): a dharma whose sexual rules bind the student but
  not the teacher, and which the student cannot contest without "schism", has made itself
  unrevisable exactly where revision is most needed [A]. The Javanese warning that seekers
  should "find competent guidance" and that the spiritual journey's "dangers … run very deep" is
  the same knowledge in a different key (see [[3.5.2-practice-and-transmission]]) [A].

### 7. The secular age: from public sin to private consent

- **The long revolution.** Dabhoiwala dates the collapse of public punishment for sexual
  immorality in England to 1600–1800: the idea that extramarital sex should be publicly punished
  "began to weaken in the late seventeenth century", and "sexual mores that were unthinkable in
  1600 were, by 1800, quite thinkable" [S52]. Larsen's literary history counts three Western
  sexual revolutions — around 1200 (lifelong monogamy under Christian feudalism, taught through
  the sagas), 1750 (the shift from parental to individual choice of partner) and 1968 — and
  projects a fourth driven by artificial intelligence [S53]. Taylor's account of the 1920s
  records "a hesitant lifting of the age-old denigration of sensuality" and "a hesitant
  affirmation of women's desire … and of their right to seek pleasure as well", with the 1960s
  adding "women in the work-force, the contraceptive revolution, and others" [S27]. The pill
  itself: Enovid was conditionally approved for contraception in May 1960 and finally on 23 June
  1960 [S51].
- **Plastic sexuality and the pure relationship.** Giddens names the result: "Plastic sexuality
  is decentred sexuality, freed from the needs of reproduction", and the "pure relationship" is
  one "entered into for its own sake, for what can be derived by each person from a sustained
  association with another; and which is continued only in so far as it is thought by both
  parties to deliver enough satisfactions for each individual to stay within it" [S50]. Marriage
  as sacrament or alliance gives way to relationship as contract terminable at will — the Na's
  "visit" arrived at by a different road [A].
- **Consent as the single rule, and its critics.** The secular dharma's sexual ethic reduces, in
  practice, to one prohibition — no sex without consent — and treats everything consensual as
  outside morality's jurisdiction; this is the harm-only morality that Fitouchi and colleagues
  contrast with the puritanical majority [S17:7][A]. Perry's case against the sexual revolution
  argues that "sexual liberation has ultimately freed men to prey upon women more than it has
  freed women to access sexual pleasure", and that a return to widespread monogamy "would
  benefit most individuals (female and male) as well as society writ large" [S55]. Whatever its
  merits, the critique shows the consent rule being contested *from inside* the secular order,
  which is what the revisability test asks for [A]. Twenge and colleagues' finding that "American
  adults had sex about nine fewer times per year in the early 2010s compared to the late 1990s"
  suggests that liberation and frequency are not the same thing [S54]. ▲ Measurement caution: the
  Mead–Freeman dispute over Samoan adolescence — Freeman arguing Mead was misled about a
  permissive sexual culture, Shankman that Freeman "cherry-picked much of his data" — is the
  standing warning that reports of other people's sexual freedom are the easiest ethnography to
  get wrong [S58].
- **The state re-moralises: Indonesia, 2026.** The secular turn is not one-way. Indonesia's new
  criminal code, Law 1/2023, in force from 2 January 2026, punishes intercourse "with a person
  who is not one's husband or wife" as *perzinaan* with up to a year's imprisonment (art. 411)
  and living together "as husband and wife outside marriage" with up to six months (art. 412);
  both are complaint offences, actionable only by a spouse or, for the unmarried, a parent or
  child, and the complaint may be withdrawn before trial [S74]. The 1974 Marriage Law already
  makes validity depend on "the law of each party's religion and belief" [S75]. The design is a
  hybrid: a national code that hands the policing of sex back to the family — the honour
  culture's guardians of section 2, now with a prosecutor behind them [A].
- **Secular experiments in community.** The nineteenth-century communes tried both settings of
  the dial: the celibate Shakers, and Oneida's "complex marriage" (1848–1880), where exclusive
  attachment was the forbidden desire [S57]; Sosis's data show that the religious ones lasted
  and the secular ones did not [S16:13,15]. ▲ The Oneida terminology (complex marriage, male
  continence, stirpiculture) rests on secondary sources; Foster or Klaw should be consulted
  before Stage 2 leans on it [A].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The corpus lean.** The shared corpus answers this question from three directions, all
  secular: evolutionary (Christakis, Henrich, Sapolsky, Wrangham, Boehm, Fitouchi), Weberian
  sociology of religion (the OCR'd *Economy and Society*), and anarchist polemic (Goldman). It
  carries almost no *theology* of sex from inside a tradition — no Augustine, no *fiqh* manual,
  no halakhic treatment of *onah*, no Vatican document — and no sexual ethnography of the
  Trobriand or Samoan kind except Mead's own book, which is unregistered. The affirming religious
  voices (the hadith on marital pleasure, al-Ghazālī, *onah*, the Song of Songs, *Humanae
  Vitae*), the abuse-case documentation (*Wind Bell*, the Lewis Silkin and Wickwire Holm reports,
  John Jay, the Royal Commission) and the Indonesian material beyond Roosa and Hefner–Bagir
  (Kemukus, the *Centhini*, Wieringa, the *bissu*, KUHP 2023) are therefore all external, checked
  on 2026-10-18 against the documents themselves where they could be fetched. Not fetched, and so
  cited at second hand: Anderson's 1987 autopsy article (Cornell refused the request), Wilson's
  1999 *warok* article (ANU refused), Zwilling's chapter, Lévi-Strauss's primary text, the
  pagination of the Ghazālī scan, and the Oneida terminology. The *bissu* persecution is dated
  loosely ("the DI/TII years") because the one source to hand puts "Operasi Toba" in 1966, after
  Kahar Muzakkar's death; Lathief (2004) should settle it.
- **Batchelor is an interested party.** The abuse section leans on an essay by a declared
  apostate from the Vajrayāna who is also the book's principal endorser; his account of the 1993
  meeting and of the lamas' responses is first-hand but not neutral, and no Tibetan rejoinder is
  in the corpus. The Dalai Lama's own words are quoted through him.
- **Contested evidence.** The Westermarck kibbutz data are disputed (Shor and Simchai) [S36];
  the "85% polygynous" figure is a count of societies in the ethnographic record, not of people
  [S33]; Christakis's bonding-circuitry claim sits beside the oxytocin literature the book
  already treats with suspicion [S1:195]; the Fitouchi model is a target article with published
  dissent, and the register entry notes the response files [S17]. Olivelle's objection to "aims
  of life" is a minority philological position against a long translation habit [S39].
- **OCR sources.** Weber, Manu (Olivelle 2005), Dumont, Taylor, Covarrubias, Hallaq, Wrangham,
  Jaini and Fox are `text-ocr` files; every quotation above from them was read in context and
  cleaned of visible OCR noise, but should be checked against print before Stage 2 quotes it.
  The Dumont Malabar case in particular is OCR-heavy and is cited as O'Malley's report, not as a
  verified wording.
- **The note's own yardstick.** As 6.4 says of "cage", the judgments here — that the double
  standard is a failure, that a bar on the *paṇḍaka* needs justifying, that consent is the
  minimum — carry a modern liberal standard. The project's defence is procedural rather than
  substantive: the question is not whether a tradition's sexual rule matches ours but whether the
  people it binds can contest it [A]. A traditionalist reviewer will say the renouncer's hostility
  to sex is the tradition's *point*, not its pathology; Weber's map at least shows that the
  hostility is one option among several that the same traditions have taken.
- **What remains unresolved.** Whether sex-as-bonding in humans is homologous with the bonobo
  pattern or convergent; whether normative monogamy's crime-reducing effect (Henrich et al.)
  survives in societies with high singlehood but low polygyny; whether the consent rule can carry
  the weight a whole sexual ethic once carried, or whether something like Perry's restraints
  re-enter by the back door; and what a *secular* sexual dharma would say to the renouncer, whose
  option it has quietly abolished (Higgins's question about excising "the male monastic norm").
