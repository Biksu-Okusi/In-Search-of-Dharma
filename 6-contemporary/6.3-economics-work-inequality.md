# 6.3 Economics, Work & Inequality

**Question:** How do dharmic frameworks engage material life — work, wealth, debt, and inequality? How have dharmas justified, constrained, or critiqued economic arrangements?

## Synthesis
Across traditions, dharmas treat economic life as **morally embedded** — work, wealth, and
debt are never a neutral sphere governed by its own laws but central matters of the path
itself [S4][S7]. Within that embedding, dharmas have done four different things with
material life. They have **justified** economic orders — Hindu *varna* assigns each class
its occupational duty, and Calvinist election turned disciplined work into a sign of grace
that, secularized, survives as modern meritocracy [S3][S10]. They have **constrained**
transactions — Right Livelihood, the cross-religious prohibition of usury, the Islamic
*zakat*, and the biblical Jubilee all fence the market with moral limits [S5][S9]. They
have **refused** the economy from below — the voluntary-simplicity strand, whose canonical
secular statement is Thoreau's demonstration, by arithmetic rather than exhortation, that
"by working about six weeks in a year" one can meet all the expenses of living, and whose
ascetic communal analogues this project documents among the Badui and the wong sikep [S12].
And they have **critiqued** accumulation — most powerfully in David Graeber's demonstration
that the very language of morality (sin, guilt, redemption) is borrowed from debt, and that
debt is the supreme instrument for making the subordinated "feel it's their fault"
[S1][S8]; and most radically in Malatesta's materialist inversion, which makes morality
itself a "shadow" cast by economic arrangements rather than a frame around them [S16]. The
distinctively modern condition is the **disembedding** of the economy (Polanyi): the claim
that the market is an autonomous, morally neutral sphere, against which contemporary
economic dharmas — secular and religious alike — argue for re-embedding material life in
human ends [S4][S5]. The deepest tension is that dharmas both bless and bind wealth, and
their binding constraints are routinely circumvented [S6].

*Second pass (2026-08-27).* The corpus now holds the primaries this note first cited at
second hand — Polanyi, Schumacher, Graeber's *Debt*, Weber, Manu — and two corrections
follow. Polanyi's own words are that "man's economy, as a rule, is submerged in his social
relationships," and that leaving labour and land to the market "would be tantamount to
annihilating them" [S4]. And the claim that Hinduism *prohibits* interest is wrong on the
primary text: Manu does not forbid lending at interest but **regulates** it — rates graded by
class, a cap at twice the principal, and "the path of usury" defined as any rate *above* the
legal limit — while barring only the two highest classes from the trade [S20]. The Indic
constraint on the market is a regulatory dharma, not an ascetic one, and the usury bans of
the Abrahamic traditions are, on Weber's account, themselves the hardening of a rule that
"at first applied only to brothers in faith" [S25]. Ambedkar supplies the internal Indian
verdict on the legitimating function: caste is "not merely a division of labour" but "a
division of labourers… graded one above the other" [S22].

## Findings

### The economy as morally embedded (the pre-modern default)
Karl Polanyi's *The Great Transformation* (1944) supplies the organizing frame: "The
outstanding discovery of recent historical and anthropological research is that man's
economy, as a rule, is submerged in his social relationships. He does not act so as to
safeguard his individual interest in the possession of material goods; he acts so as to
safeguard his social standing, his social claims, his social assets" [S4]. Land, labour and
money "are obviously not commodities; the postulate that anything that is bought and sold
must have been produced for sale is emphatically untrue in regard to them" — labour "is only
another name for a human activity which goes with life itself," land "only another name for
nature, which is not produced by man" [S4]. Weber's sociology of religion records the same
embedding from the other side: the speculative "avarice" of the moneyed parvenu "is
everywhere regarded as abominable from the religious point of view. It was so regarded in
the Hindu legal books, as well as in ancient Christianity and in Islam," and in Judaism the
reaction produced "the characteristic institution of a jubilee year in which debts were
cancelled and slaves liberated" [S25]. The
Hindu *puruṣārthas* make this embedding explicit by listing wealth *inside* the ethical
scheme: the four legitimate aims of life are *dharma* (righteousness), *artha* (prosperity),
*kāma* (pleasure), and *mokṣa* (liberation) — so the pursuit of material security is a
sanctioned goal, but "only when pursued within ethical boundaries," and "in cases of
conflict, dharma is considered more important than artha" [S7]. Material life is thus
located *within* the path, never outside it. This is the baseline that the modern
disembedding of the economy (below) overturns.

### Dharma as justifier of economic order
The most consequential — and most contested — function is legitimation.

- **Hindu *varṇa* / *varṇāśrama-dharma*.** Each *varṇa* carries its own occupational
  dharma, and "how healthy a society is depends on how well the [varnas] perform their
  functions." Defenders hold the original system was "based on cooperation, mutual service,
  and commensurate rights and responsibilities," but "what began as a flexible
  classification based on qualities and actions became a rigid, hereditary hierarchy," with
  texts like the *Manusmṛti* binding *varṇa* to birth [S10]. The primary text is in the
  corpus and says so itself: "For the protection of this whole creation, that One of dazzling
  brilliance assigned separate activities for those born from the mouth, arms, thighs, and
  feet" — reciting and sacrifice to the Brahmin, protection to the Kṣatriya, "trade,
  moneylending, and agriculture" to the Vaiśya, and to the Śūdra "a single activity… the
  ungrudging service of those very social classes" (Manu 1.87–91) [S20]. Here dharma supplies
  divine sanction for an economic division of labour — the legitimating function in its
  starkest form (cf. [[7.3-dharma-and-power]]). Dumont's structural reading makes the
  economic subordinate by design: in Hindu society "status is determined by principles
  independent of the distribution of authority," and "the idiom in which higher or lower
  status is expressed is the idiom of purity" — so the king, "the main employer," ranks below
  the priest, and wealth and power are encompassed by a religious ranking they cannot buy
  [S21]. The internal Indian verdict is Ambedkar's: the caste system "is not merely a
  division of labour. It is also a division of labourers… a hierarchy in which the divisions
  of labourers are graded one above the other"; the division "is not spontaneous, it is not
  based on natural aptitudes," but assigns tasks "in advance — selected not on the basis of
  trained original capacities, but on that of the social status of the parents" [S22]. And
  the legitimation has not expired with the market: Mosse finds caste "enclosed" by policy
  in the realm of religion and politics while modernity is aligned to "the caste-erasing
  market," yet in his Tamil village survey a majority of Dalits still named caste as a
  barrier (*tatai*) to their families' economic advancement — the Dalit entrepreneur's hope
  that "capitalism can destroy the caste system from the inside" set against the lived
  finding that it has not yet [S23].
- **Calvinism → the Protestant ethic → meritocracy.** Michael Sandel's reading of Weber
  traces the same structure in the West: Calvinism "combined strenuous work with
  asceticism," and disciplined work-but-low-consumption "yields the accumulation of wealth
  that fuels capitalism" — so that "even when the original religious motivations fall away,
  the Protestant ethic of work and asceticism provides the cultural basis for capitalist
  accumulation" [S3]. Predestination, demanding that the elect "prove their election
  through work in a calling," "confers divine sanction on the division of labour" [S3].
  Crucially, worldly success was meant as a *sign* of election, not its source — but, Sandel
  argues, it "proved difficult to resist the slide" from sign to source, producing "an
  early version of meritocratic" thinking [S3]. Modern **meritocracy** is the secularized
  residue: success read as desert and virtue, poverty as failure — a secular dharma that
  re-sacralizes inequality after God has dropped out. Reading it as a [[6.1-secular-translation]]
  in reverse — a translation that *kept* the legitimating function while shedding the grace
  and humility that once tempered it — is this project's framing [A]. Weber's own
  observation of the mechanism is now in the corpus. In "The Protestant Sects and the Spirit
  of Capitalism" he reports from the United States that admission to a reputable sect was
  granted only to one "whose 'conduct' made him appear to be morally qualified beyond
  doubt," so that "sect membership meant a certificate of moral qualification" — and
  therefore of creditworthiness: the sect "arranged his affairs, gave guarantees to the
  creditors," and business followed the baptised [S24]. Religious standing was already a
  form of economic capital before it was secularised into merit.

### Dharma as constraint on transactions
A second function fences the market with moral limits.

- **Right Livelihood and Buddhist economics.** *Sammā-ājīva* (right livelihood), a factor
  of the eightfold path, is the hinge on which EF Schumacher's "Buddhist Economics" (1966;
  in *Small Is Beautiful*, 1973) turns, and the essay is now in the corpus. Its counter-
  economics starts from work: "The Buddhist point of view takes the function of work to be at
  least threefold: to give man a chance to utilise and develop his faculties; to enable him
  to overcome his ego-centredness by joining with other people in a common task; and to bring
  forth the goods and services needed for a becoming existence." To organise work so that it
  is "meaningless, boring, stultifying, or nerve-racking for the worker would be little short
  of criminal" [S5]. The pin factory of *The Wealth of Nations* is its chosen antitype. On
  wealth the position is the Middle Way, not asceticism: "It is not wealth that stands in the
  way of liberation but the attachment to wealth; not the enjoyment of pleasurable things but
  the craving for them. The keynote of Buddhist economics, therefore, is simplicity and
  non-violence" — "amazingly small means leading to extraordinarily satisfactory results,"
  against an economics that assumes "a man who consumes more is 'better off' than a man who
  consumes less" [S5]. The slogan is "economics as if people mattered" [S5].
- **The prohibition of usury, and redistribution.** The condemnation of interest is
  strikingly cross-cultural: "major religions including Hinduism, Buddhism, Judaism, Islam,
  and Christianity prohibit interest," with Islam offering "the most explicit, severe, and
  comprehensive prohibitions" against *ribā* [S6]. Constraint is paired with redistribution
  — Islamic *zakat* functions as a wealth tax "to assist disadvantaged individuals" — and
  with periodic reset: the biblical **Jubilee** (Leviticus 25) mandated debt release and
  land readjustment, though "there is no evidence that such wholesale redistribution… was
  ever actually carried out" [S9]. ▲ **The cross-religious claim over-reaches on the Indic
  side.** Manu, read in Olivelle's translation, does not prohibit interest; it *regulates*
  it. A lender "may charge 1.25 per cent per month," or "2 per cent, keeping in mind the Law
  followed by good men; for by charging 2 per cent he does not become guilty of a sin
  involving money"; rates of "2, 3, 4, or 5 per cent interest per month, according to the
  descending order of the classes" are lawful; total interest "shall never exceed twice the
  principal"; and only "an interest rate set above the legal limit is exorbitant and cannot
  be enforced; that is called the path of usury" (8.140–152) [S20]. Lending on interest is
  listed among "the ten means of livelihood," with the restriction that "a Brahmin or a
  Kṣatriya must never lend money on interest" (10.116–117) [S20] — a status bar on the two
  highest classes, not a moral bar on the practice. The Indic constraint is therefore a
  *regulated* market with class-graded prices, closer in form to a usury statute than to the
  Islamic *ribā* ban [A]. Weber adds a genealogy that unsettles the Abrahamic side too: "In
  Islam and in ancient Christianity, the prohibition against usury at first applied only to
  brothers in faith, but subsequently became unconditional"; the Christian ban rests partly
  on a verse of Luke that "was then misread and mistranslated in a fashion that resulted in
  the prohibition of usury," the original basis being "the primitive custom of economic
  assistance to one's fellows" [S25]. The constraint began as in-group mutual aid and was
  universalised by exegesis. And the constraints leak: every tradition developed
  "legal fictions" — the Jewish *Heter Iska*, the Islamic *Murābaḥa* — to narrow the
  definition of usury and route around the ban [S6]. Dharma's grip on the market is real but
  evadable (cf. [[7.1-failure-modes]]).

### The refusal of livelihood: voluntary simplicity as economic dharma
A third response neither blesses nor fences the market but **withdraws demand from it**:
reduce wants until the economy loses its grip. The canonical secular statement is Thoreau.
"Life Without Principle" (1863) makes livelihood itself the central and neglected moral
question — "It is remarkable that there is little or nothing to be remembered written on
the subject of getting a living: how to make getting a living not merely honest and
honorable, but altogether inviting and glorious; for if getting a living is not so, then
living is not" [S11]. His verdict on wage-earning is total: "The ways by which you may get
money almost without exception lead downward… Those services which the community will most
readily pay for it is most disagreeable to render. You are paid for being something less
than a man"; the labourer's aim should be "not to get his living, to get 'a good job,' but
to perform well a certain work" [S11]. Speculative wealth fares worse — the gold rush shows
men "ready to live by luck, and so get the means of commanding the labor of others less
lucky, without contributing any value to society! And that is called enterprise!" — and the
conclusion is ascetic in the strict sense: "A man had better starve at once than lose his
innocence in the process of getting his bread" [S11] — echoed in *Walden*'s "trade curses
everything it handles" [S12]. Political emancipation without economic emancipation is void: the
American who has "freed himself from a political tyrant" remains "the slave of an economical
and moral tyrant" [S11].

What makes *Walden* (1854) unusual among dharmas of work is that its "Economy" chapter
argues by **ledger, not sermon**. Thoreau itemises the house ($28.12½ in materials), the
eight months' food ($8.74), a first-year outgo of $61.99¾ against $36.78 earned, and
concludes: "by working about six weeks in a year, I could meet all the expenses of living.
The whole of my winters, as well as most of my summers, I had free and clear for study"
[S12]. The accounting rests on a redefinition of price — "the cost of a thing is the amount
of what I will call life which is required to be exchanged for it, immediately or in the
long run" — under which most acquisition is a bad bargain and "the mass of men lead lives
of quiet desperation" [S12]. The prescription is "Simplicity, simplicity, simplicity!", and
the diagnosis inverts the instrument: "We do not ride on the railroad; it rides upon us"
[S12]. In this project's terms Thoreau is a one-man secular analogue of the ascetic
economic dharmas documented at [[4.6-badui-of-west-java]] (the *pikukuh*'s refusal to
alter the world) and [[4.1-samin-of-java]] (simplicity and self-sufficiency as ethical
commitments): poverty-by-choice as a dharmic position rather than a deprivation — with the
important asymmetry that the Badui and wong sikep versions are communal, hereditary and
collectively enforced, while Thoreau's is individual, elective and two years long [A].

The refusal has carried a standing scholarly objection for a century and a half, and the
note must carry it too. James Russell Lowell's 1865 review fixed its terms: "He squatted on
another man's land; he borrows an axe; his boards, his nails, his bricks, his mortar, his
books, his lamp, his fish-hooks, his plough, his hoe, all turn state's evidence against him
as an accomplice in the sin of that artificial civilization" [S13]. The documented facts
behind the jibe: the woodlot belonged to Emerson (Thoreau got the site through a
work-exchange arrangement); the experiment ran two years, two months and two days (4 July
1845 – 6 September 1847); he walked into Concord several times a week and was supported in
kind by family and friends; and he had no dependants [S14]. Two qualifications keep the
critique honest. Thoreau hides none of it — "I borrowed an axe" opens the building
narrative, with the gloss that "It is difficult to begin without borrowing," and he calls
himself "not being the owner, but merely a squatter" [S12] — so the charge is not
concealment. The critique's real force is **generalisability**: a sufficiency arithmetic
demonstrated from a subsidised position (young, single, rent-free by patronage) does not
show that the six-week year scales to a household with dependants and no Emerson. That is
the same structural weakness the post-scarcity claim ran into a century later (below) [A].

### Dharma as critique of accumulation and debt
The corpus's deepest material is David Graeber's anthropology of debt. Graeber's central
claim is that morality and debt share a vocabulary: "in a lot of world religions the word
for sin is the same as that for debt" — the Lord's Prayer originally read "forgive us our
debts," Plato's *Republic* defines justice as "paying one's debts," and in the Vedic texts
"life is a debt you pay to the Gods, sacrifice is the interest" [S1]. Tellingly, "in every
case, they first frame morality as debt, and then say, well, actually, no, not really" —
every tradition feels "shackled to that logic" yet strains to escape it [S1]. Debt, for
Graeber, is "the perversion of a promise… through mathematics and violence," so that "we've
come to see such acts of violence as the very essence of morality" [S1]; and it is "the most
effective way to take a relation of violent subordination and make the victims feel that
it's their fault" — colonial regimes "would charge people for the cost of their own
conquest, via taxes" [S1]. The book's constructive point inverts the moral intuition: "in
the ancient world, it was not repaying debts that was sacred, but one's ability to forgive
or… cancel debts" — Mesopotamian "clean slate" amnesties and the biblical Jubilee model a
freedom understood as "the collective power to readjust the commitments we have to one
another" [S2]. The published book (*Debt: The First 5,000 Years*, 2011), now in the corpus,
carries the Vedic material in full: in the early texts "debt seems to stand in for a broader
sense of inner suffering," and "it was only with the Brahmanas that commentators started
trying to weave all this together into a more comprehensive philosophy. The conclusion: that
human existence is itself a form of debt. A man, being born, is a debt; by his own self he
is born to Death, and only when he sacrifices does he redeem himself from Death" — a debt
owed also to the sages (repaid by study), the ancestors (by children) and "to men" (by
hospitality) [S8]. The Lord's Prayer is read the same way — "forgive us our debts, as we
also forgive our debtors," with the "similarly dire" implication that "most Christians
reciting the prayer are aware that they do not generally forgive their debtors" [S8]. And
the interview's provocation about wage labour is in the book in sharper form: wage labour is
"the renting of our freedom in the same way that slavery can be conceived as its sale" [S8].
(The book's framing question — why debts must be repaid even when repayment "results in the
suffering and death of innocents" — was not locatable in the corpus chunks and stays on the
printed edition [S8].)

### The materialist inversion: morality as the economy's shadow
The anarchist tradition radicalises the critique into a full inversion of the embedding
frame. Where Polanyi has the pre-modern economy embedded in morals, Malatesta's "The
Economic Question" (1884) embeds morals in the economy: the economic question is
"fundamental in Sociology, and… other matters — political, religious, etc., — are merely
its reflections, perhaps even the shadows it casts," condensed into the formula "Economic
inequality is the source of all moral, intellectual, political, etc. inequalities" [S16].
Religion itself is re-described as an economic arrangement — the Church runs "a system of
levies very much like the State's," and the clergy are "the class of those who have ducked
out of their labor obligations in order to devote themselves to God" [S16]. The companion
piece "Capitalists and Thieves" (1912) collapses the moral distinction property law rests
on: "The capitalist is a thief who has succeeded… the thief is an aspiring capitalist,"
while theft by a man "denied the opportunity to work by society… rather than starve" is "a
rebellion against social injustice and may well become the most sacred of rights and the
most imperious of duties" [S17]. Yet Malatesta's own reporting undercuts a pure reduction:
his account of the 1889 London dock strike dwells on strikers racing, unpaid and mid-strike,
to unload a ship of ice rumoured to be "meant for the hospitals," and he faults the early
anarchists for weighing the strike only as an "economic weapon" while failing "to give it
its due as an index of moral rebellion" [S18] — a moral surplus his own formula says should
not exist. The inversion matters to this note as a limit case: if morality is only the
economy's shadow, then every justifying, constraining and refusing dharma above is
epiphenomenal — a claim the note reports as the corpus's strongest anti-embedding position,
not as its own [A].

### Post-scarcity: what abundance changes about a dharma of work
Every dharma of work above assumes scarcity — the *varṇa* division of labour, the Calvinist
calling, even Thoreau's ledger are answers to the question "who must toil, and how much?"
Murray Bookchin's post-scarcity thesis (1971) claims the question itself has expired: a
technological turning point since the 1950s "negates all values and social programs of
previous history, by making possible an era at once materially abundant and virtually free
from toil," dissolving "the repressive attitudes and hierarchical institutions previously
necessitated by scarcity and the need to work" [S15]. If true, this changes what an
economic dharma may demand: work-discipline loses its material justification, and an ethics
of liberated, self-chosen activity replaces an ethics of toil. The corpus, usefully,
carries the thesis inside its own strongest rebuttal — Jeremy Brecher's 1973 review-dialogue.
Brecher's empirical verdict is that Bookchin's technological projections are "the science
fiction they in fact are," and his sociological verdict is sharper: the youth who appeared
to live post-scarcity did so "on the basis of parental subsidy, educational advantage,
personal connections, and other forms of privilege" — their "privileged position already
allows them to simulate 'post-scarcity'" [S15]. Bookchin's reply defends the projections as
*understated* and accuses Brecher of "selective quoting," but concedes nothing on the
subsidy point [S15]. The assessment this note adopts: as an empirical claim about 1970,
post-scarcity was dated optimism — the recession that ended the counterculture ended the
demonstration, as Brecher observed in real time [S15]; but as an analytic point it is
durable — what a dharma can ask about work is indexed to the scarcity regime it assumes,
and the recurring failure mode is the same one Lowell found at Walden Pond: claimed
abundance that is in fact subsidy, a freedom from toil quietly underwritten by someone
else's labour [A]. The oldest secular proposal to fund a floor *without* subsidy is also in
the corpus: Paine's *Agrarian Justice* (1797) argues that "the condition of every person born
into the world, after a state of civilization commences, ought not to be worse than if he had
been born before that period," finds that for "millions" it is, and proposes a national fund
paying "fifteen pounds" to every person at twenty-one and an annuity in old age, as
compensation for the loss of the natural inheritance in land [S26]. The modern descendants of
that fund — universal basic income and its pilots — are treated at [[8.2-ai-and-dharma]],
not here.

### The modern rupture: disembedding and its discontents
What unites contemporary economic dharmas is a response to Polanyi's diagnosis. The 19th-
century project of a self-regulating market sought to **disembed** the economy from society,
treating land, labour, and money as "fictitious commodities" — things "not created for the
market"; a market society that fully commodifies them, Polanyi warned, "would annihilate
society," provoking a "double movement" of protective counter-responses (labour law,
welfare) [S4]. Today's economic dharmas — Schumacher's Buddhist economics, the usury and
Jubilee traditions, Graeber's debt-abolitionism — converge on a single demand: **re-embed**
material life in human and moral ends [S2][S5]. Graeber sharpens the stakes with a
provocation: wage labour, where "we no longer sell ourselves to employers, we rent
ourselves," would look to anyone from the ancient world like a softened form of the
debt-slavery they most feared [S1]. The disembedded economy, on this reading, reproduces an
ancient bondage in a respectable new form — which is exactly what the meritocratic dharma,
by reading the resulting inequality as just deserts, is built to obscure [S3].

The re-embedding demand is itself vulnerable to a failure mode distinct from the evasion
documented above (legal fictions) and the non-enactment of the Jubilee: **recuperation** —
the absorption and neutralisation of a moral critique by the arrangement it criticised.
Brian Morris's worked example is ecology, once "the subversive science": within a
generation, corporations were "'greening' their public image," "sustainable development"
had come to mean "sustaining 'development', that is capitalist growth," and responsibility
for the crisis was displaced from the economic system onto "the actions of individual
'consumers'" [S19]. An economic dharma can thus fail not by being circumvented or ignored
but by being *adopted* — its vocabulary retained, its constraint deleted (cf.
[[7.1-failure-modes]]; and [[8.1-planetary-dharma]] for the stakes) [S19].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias
The corpus is **rich but lopsided to the left** on this question. Its economic material is
curated around David Graeber — an anarchist anthropologist and Occupy activist — and Michael
Sandel, a communitarian critic of meritocracy, supplemented by Buddhist-economics sources.
The result is a strong tilt toward the *critique* of capitalism and inequality and a
thinness on dharmas that *bless* wealth on their own terms. On the first pass the Calvinist
material reached the corpus only through Sandel's critical lens (*The Tyranny of Merit*), and
the comparative usury/*zakat*/Jubilee balance and the *puruṣārtha*/*varṇa* framing had to be
imported externally [S6][S9][S7][S10]. A non-privileging treatment must therefore foreground
these systems' *internal* self-understanding — *varṇa* as a cooperative ideal of mutual
service, Calvinist work as the glorification of God — before applying the corpus's ready
critique [S3][S10].

**Second pass (2026-08-27): what the expansion fixed, and what it did not.** The 2026-08
shelves put the primaries behind this note's second-hand citations into the corpus — Polanyi
[S4], Schumacher [S5], Graeber's *Debt* [S8], Weber's *Economy and Society* and the sects
essay [S24][S25], Manu in Olivelle's edition [S20], Dumont, Ambedkar and Mosse on caste
[S21][S22][S23], Paine [S26] — and one of them *corrects* the note: the "cross-religious
prohibition of interest" claim from the RSIS survey [S6] does not survive Manu 8.140–152,
which regulates rates rather than banning the practice; Stage 2 must not repeat "Hinduism
prohibits interest." Weber's genealogy of the usury ban (in-group mutual aid, universalised
partly by a mistranslation) is a second correction of tone: the Abrahamic constraint is less
a timeless moral absolute than a hardened customary rule [S25]. The lean itself is not
corrected. Weber is a neutral witness, not a defender; Dumont describes the hierarchy's
logic without endorsing it, and the caste shelf is otherwise Ambedkarite; the corpus still
contains no economist's defence of interest, wage labour or growth on their own terms, no
prosperity-gospel or market-Hindu voice, and no *artha*-affirming text beyond Manu's own
list of lawful livelihoods. The `5-ubi-automation/` shelf (Frey & Osborne, Autor, the Kenya,
Finland and Stockton trials) exists for [[8.2-ai-and-dharma]] and was left there. ▲ OCR
caution: Polanyi, both Weber volumes, Dumont and Manu are scanned texts; every phrase quoted
above was checked against the chunk, but page-bleed and garbling are visible around them,
so re-check against print before quoting in Part 6.

The 2026-07-27 corpus addition (Thoreau, Malatesta, Bookchin, Morris) **deepens rather than
corrects** this lean: all four new in-corpus voices are anarchist or anarchist-adjacent
critics of the economic order, and the corpus still contains no economist's defence of
interest, wage labour, or growth on their own terms. Three honesty notes on the new
material. The Thoreau strand is balanced *in-note* by the external Lowell critique and the
documented subsidy facts [S13][S14], which had to be imported from outside the corpus — the
corpus supplies the refusal but not its standing objection. The Bookchin post-scarcity
thesis is cited here through a hostile review-dialogue (Brecher's summary plus Bookchin's
reply), not through *Post-Scarcity Anarchism* itself; the thesis-statement quoted is
Brecher's paraphrase, and Bookchin's protest that the review misquotes him is recorded
[S15] — a future pass should check the thesis against the Bookchin Reader now in-corpus.
And the Malatesta inversion is reported as a limit case, not endorsed: taken literally it
would dissolve this note's own subject-matter, and Malatesta's dock-strike reporting is
cited against it [S16][S17][S18]. All quotations from the new material were verified
verbatim against the source files on disk, not taken from RAG excerpts.

Two further cautions. **First**, Graeber's interview material is polemical and activist; his
strongest claims — that wage labour is a species of debt-slavery, that debt is "the essence"
of violence-morality — are provocations grounded in real scholarship but contested by
economists, and are reported here as a powerful thesis, not settled consensus [S1]. The
corpus also contains AI-generated essays restating Graeber & Wengrow's "indigenous critique"
(`graebers-indigenous-critique_*.md`); these were used for orientation only, with the
genuine Graeber interviews and the published *Debt* carrying the evidentiary weight [S8].
**Second**, the gap between dharmic economic *ideal* and *practice* is wide and largely
unmeasured: usury bans were routinely circumvented [S6], the Jubilee may never have been enacted
[S9], and whether any constraint-tradition actually governed an economy — versus merely
voicing aspiration — is an open empirical question handed to
[[7.4-measurement-falsifiability]] and [[7.1-failure-modes]].
