# 6.2 Digital-Age Dharma

**Question:** What new dharmic frameworks are emerging to address digital-age challenges like information overload, social media, and AI? How do they differ from traditional forms?

## Synthesis
A cluster of new frameworks is emerging — "humane technology" (Tristan Harris / Center for
Humane Technology), the attention-resistance philosophies (James Williams, Cal Newport,
Jenny Odell), and science-grounded collective programs (David Sloan Wilson's ProSocial) —
and what unites them is that they treat **attention** as the contested ground of the good
life [S2][S5][S6][S7][S8]. They differ from traditional dharmas chiefly in the *locus of the
adversary*: classical contemplative paths trained the individual mind against its own
craving and delusion in a world of information *scarcity*, whereas digital-age frameworks
confront an *external, industrialized* adversary — an "extractive attention economy" of
firms using AI to hijack evolved reward systems (supernormal stimuli) in a world of
*abundance* [S6][S1][S10]. This forces a move classical contemplative dharma never needed:
redesign the *environment*, not only the self — a structural and political register
[S5][S2]. Shoshana Zuboff supplies the adversary's economic name: *surveillance capitalism*,
which "claims human experience as free raw material for hidden commercial practices of
extraction, prediction, and sales" and subordinates production to "a new global architecture
of behavioral modification" [S17]. One correction sharpens this picture: the adversary is not *new*, only newly
industrialized at the level of the individual nervous system. Guy Debord argued in 1967
that lived experience was already being displaced by mediated representation — "a social
relation between people that is mediated by images" — and in 1988 judged the spectacle
more total, not less, two decades on [S12][S13]; the Situationists had even fielded
countermeasure practices (dérive, détournement) in the 1950s, and watched them be
absorbed by what they opposed [S14][S16]. Yet the new frameworks also *recover* the old
insight that attention is never ethically neutral (Buddhist *sati* was always morally
embedded) [S9]. The cautionary
thread from [[6.1-secular-translation]] returns sharpened: secular mindfulness, the
celebrated translation, can itself become the attention economy's pacifier, so a real
digital-age dharma must be more than a coping technique [S11].

A second-pass check (2026-08-27) adds the discipline the first pass lacked: the *harm*
side of the adversary is contested by the best population evidence. Twenge's surveys of
half a million US adolescents tie post-2010 rises in depressive symptoms to new-media
screen time [S19], but Orben and Przybylski's specification-curve analysis of 355,358
adolescents finds the association "negative but small, explaining at most 0.4% of the
variation in well-being" — on a par with eating potatoes — and "too small to warrant policy
change" [S20]; Odgers's review of Haidt concludes "there is no evidence that using these
platforms is rewiring children's brains or driving an epidemic of mental illness" [S22];
and a 2025 study of 30 English schools found restrictive phone policies cut in-school use
without any difference in wellbeing [S23]. Doctorow adds that surveillance capitalism's
mind-control claims are mostly its own advertising, and that the durable harm is monopoly
over the informational commons [S18]. The digital-age dharmas therefore rest on a diagnosis
whose *mechanism* (supernormal stimulus, engineered engagement) is better supported than
its *epidemiology*; a dharma built on an inflated harm story is itself a form of the
spectacle [A].

## Findings

### Reading humane tech and attention-resistance as emergent dharmas
None of the figures here call their work a "dharma." Reading humane technology, digital
minimalism, and the "attention ecology" *as* emergent secular dharmas is the project's
interpretive lens, licensed by the working definition that dharmas are plural, adaptive
ethical paths ([[1.3-boundary-definition]]): each offers a way of orienting attention and
a life toward what one values, carried by practices and an ethic. That framing is named
here as interpretation, not as the authors' self-description (see Tensions) [A].

### The new adversary: the extractive attention economy
The defining shift is from an inner to an industrial adversary. James Williams — a Google
advertising strategist turned Oxford philosopher — argues in *Stand Out of Our Light*
(2018) that as information becomes abundant the truly scarce resource is **attention**,
and that the internet and social media are "not knowledge systems or communicative
networks, but ways of attracting attention and redirecting people away from their wills";
human freedom is therefore "threatened by systems of intelligent persuasion" [S6]. Tristan
Harris, an ex-Google design ethicist, founded the Center for Humane Technology (2018) out
of the "Time Well Spent" movement to fight what he calls the "human downgrading" worked by
an "extractive" attention economy in which firms profit by "playing tricks on people's
minds" [S5]. At the limit, attention itself is enclosed and commodified — "the new
frontier of the capitalist process of enclosure" — which is why some theorists reframe the
goal as moving "from an attention economy to an ecology of attention" [S10].

The corpus's distinctive contribution is the *evolutionary mechanism* beneath this. Mark
van Vugt (interviewed for ProSocial) describes social media as "an exaggerated cue, a
supernormal stimulus": humans crave social interaction, but where our ancestors had to
"expend energy and time" to maintain relationships, "social media interactions come very
cheap," and "just as drugs hijack our reward system so do tweets, likes, and shares." The
danger is addiction, and "we have no natural defense mechanisms against the social media
invasion of our lives," so we are thrown back on uneven self-control [S1]. This is the
[[5.2-evolutionary-psychology]] mismatch applied directly to the feed.

Two primaries now in the corpus name the industrial side of the mechanism. Zuboff's
*Surveillance Capitalism* (2019) is the economic account: Google's 2002 "discovery of
behavioral surplus" — data beyond what service improvement needs, refined into "prediction
products" and traded in "behavioral futures markets" — set off "competitive dynamics" that
"drive surveillance capitalists to acquire ever-more-predictive sources of behavioral
surplus: our voices, personalities, and emotions"; the result is "a new instrumentarian
power that asserts dominance over society" [S17]. Max Fisher's reporting supplies the
affective engine: the psychologist William Brady, a former online vegan brawler, found that
"you can get people's attention by posting more inflammatory stuff," that all users are
"simultaneously both conducting and serving as the subjects of a never-ending psychology
experiment," and that the key emotion the feed rewards is *moral outrage* — "not just anger
against a transgressor" but "a desire to see the entire community line up against them"
[S16]. Harris's own 2023 statement of the thesis is on record: "In this first contact with
social media, humanity lost," because the paradigm ("giving people a voice… connecting
people with their friends") hid the business model, and "when the business model is
engagement… the race for social media translates to these large language models as
companies competing to have an intimate spot in your life" [S5]. The moral-outrage finding
is the note's tightest link to [[5.2-evolutionary-psychology]] and [[5.3-moral-psychology-development]]:
the feed does not invent a new appetite; it pays out on the oldest one, third-party
punishment [A].

The mechanism story has a sceptic inside the same anti-monopoly camp. Cory Doctorow
grants the harvesting but denies the mind control: surveillance capitalists "promise their
customers (the advertisers)" that machine learning will "bypass the rational faculties of
the public and direct their behavior," but "there's little evidence that this is happening"
— what they mostly do is segment, deceive and dominate; influence campaigns have effects
that are "small and temporary while monopolistic dominance over informational systems has
massive, enduring effects," so "monopoly is the cause, and surveillance capitalism and its
negative outcomes are the effects" [S18]. For this note the disagreement matters: if
Doctorow is right, the adversary is structural rather than neuro-psychological, and the
individual-practice dharmas (Newport, Odell) are aimed at the wrong target [A].

The collateral damage is informational and social. With the gatekeeping function of
traditional media eroded, "anyone can distribute any kind of news without a guarantee of
quality," harmful and polarizing content spreads fastest, and — because "the more people
act on their convictions, the stronger and less changeable that conviction becomes" —
sharing entrenches belief; interventions such as "accuracy prompts" can cut willingness to
share misinformation by as much as 20% [S3]. There is even a neural worry: screen-mediated
interaction may operate "less efficiently" for the mirror-neuron system underlying theory
of mind and role-taking, with possible costs to social cohesion (the source cites Sherry
Turkle's *Alone Together* and the literature on democratic polarization) [S4].

### An older diagnosis: Debord's spectacle (1967/1988)
The 2026-07 corpus fold forces a correction to the framing above: the "external,
industrialized adversary" was diagnosed two decades before the web and five before the
smartphone. Guy Debord's *The Society of the Spectacle* (1967) opens with the claim that
in modern conditions of production "life is presented as an immense accumulation of
*spectacles*. Everything that was directly lived has receded into a representation"
(thesis 1), and insists the spectacle "is not a collection of images; it is a social
relation between people that is mediated by images" (thesis 4) [S12]. Two of his theses
read as a pre-digital specification of attention capture: the spectacle "monopolizes the
majority of the time spent outside the production process" (thesis 6), and the "passive
acceptance it demands is already effectively imposed by its monopoly of appearances, its
manner of appearing without allowing any reply" (thesis 12) [S12]. Citton's "ecology of
attention" [S10] engages Debord's spectacle directly (it takes up the thesis-12 motto
"What appears is good; what is good appears"), so the note's own attention-ecology
source stands in this lineage rather than beginning with the smartphone [S10][S12].

Debord's *Comments on the Society of the Spectacle* (1988) is the more useful text here,
because it is the diagnosis re-run against twenty years of evidence. His verdict was
that the spectacle had become "integrated": where the concentrated (totalitarian) form
left most of society untouched and the diffuse (consumer) form a little, "today, no
part" escapes (§IV); its five features are "incessant technological renewal; fusion of
State and economy; generalized secrecy," unanswerable falsehood, and "a perpetual
present" (§V) [S13]. Several 1988 observations anticipate, from television-era evidence,
the mechanics the note's contemporary sources describe: an image-flow whose controller
"chooses where the flow will lead, and the rhythm of what should be shown, as a
perpetual, arbitrary surprise," leaving no "time for reflection" (§X — a startling
pre-description of the algorithmic feed); a news cycle in which "when the spectacle
stops talking about something for three days, it is as if it did not exist" (§VII); and
the disappearance of the agora — "no place where people can discuss the realities which
concern them" (§VII) [S13]. These are a polemicist's theses, not measured findings —
Debord offers no mechanism and no data, and the *Comments* shade into conspiracism in
their later sections — but as a dated prediction they establish that the "extractive"
adversary [S5][S6] is an intensification of something already named in 1967, not a
creature of the smartphone [S13][A].

### Situationist countermeasures — and their recorded fate
The Situationists also prefigure the *response* side of the note. Their proposed
practices were environmental before the fact: the **dérive**, "a technique of rapid
passage through varied ambiances" in which participants "drop their relations, their
work and leisure activities, and all their other usual motives for movement and action"
and follow the terrain's attractions [S15] — structurally an ancestor of Newport's
declutter (deliberate suspension of default attention contracts) and of Odell's
re-grounding of attention in place [S7][S8][A]; **détournement**, the partisan re-use of
the dominant culture's own material against it ("the literary and artistic heritage of
humanity should be used for partisan propaganda purposes") [S16], the acknowledged
ancestor of culture jamming; and the **constructed situation**, which attacks the
spectacle's "very principle... nonintervention" by converting passive spectators into
"livers" of situations they build themselves [S14]. Debord's 1957 *Report* even states
the attention-economy thesis in leisure-industry form: the ruling class fights a "battle
of leisure" through "a vast industrial sector of leisure activities" that stupefies, and
"the abundance of televised imbecilities" helps explain political passivity [S14]. The
locus is already the *environment*, not the practitioner's craving — which is why these
practices, unlike classical contemplative training, were conceived as collective and
political from the start [S14][A].

The record of whether they *worked* is unusually honest, because Debord audited his own
movement. The 1957 *Report* already describes the failure mode with precision: the
ruling ideology ensures "subversive discoveries are trivialized and sterilized, after
which they can be safely spectacularized," and the bourgeoisie "resists actual creations
when they first appear, then eventually exploits them" — his case study being
surrealism, co-opted "into ordinary aesthetic commerce" [S14]. The 1967 book generalizes
it: "dissatisfaction itself becomes a commodity as soon as the economy of abundance
develops the capacity to process that particular raw material" (thesis 59) [S12]. And
the 1988 *Comments* concede the outcome: after 1968 "the spectacle has thus continued to
reinforce itself" (§II); "wherever the spectacle rules, the only organized forces are
those that want the spectacle" (§VIII); even critique of the spectacle survives only as
"spectacular critique of the spectacle" — Debord approvingly quotes *Le Monde*'s
observation that books deploring the phenomenon "must sacrifice themselves to the
spectacle if they're to become known" (§III) [S13]. By his own account, then, the one
sustained twentieth-century attempt at environment-scale attention resistance ended in
recuperation and defeat — Situationist style became art-world and advertising style
while the diagnosis it carried was neutralized [S13][S14][A].

### The responses — a spectrum from individual practice to structural redesign
The emerging frameworks array along a spectrum that mirrors [[6.1-secular-translation]]'s
extraction-to-reconstruction axis:

- **Individual practice.** Cal Newport's *Digital Minimalism* (2019), now read in full,
  offers "a philosophy of technology use, something that covers from the ground up which
  digital tools we allow into our life, for what reasons, and under what constraints" —
  because "willpower, tips, and vague resolutions are not sufficient by themselves to tame
  the ability of new technologies to invade your cognitive landscape" [S7]. Its practice
  is the thirty-day declutter, after which one reintroduces technologies "starting from a
  blank slate," each justified by "what value it serves in your life," so that the reset
  replaces "compulsive habits that may have accumulated haphazardly" with behaviours
  "optimized… to support your values instead of subverting them" [S7]. Newport is candid
  that "a nontrivial number of people ended up aborting this process before the full thirty
  days were done" [S7], and he defines addiction in the psychologists' sense (rewarding
  effects that "provide a compelling incentive to repeatedly pursue the behavior despite
  detrimental consequences") rather than the lurid one [S7]. Structurally this is an installation practice in the
  sense of [[5.4-ritual-habituation]] — a ritualized decluttering that re-trains a
  disposition.
- **Re-grounding attention.** Jenny Odell's *How to Do Nothing: Resisting the Attention
  Economy* (2019) argues not for withdrawal but for redirecting attention to "active modes
  of listening and contemplation," resisting "a colonization of the self by capitalist
  ideas of productivity," and staying "in the midst of life"; attention is re-anchored in
  place, bioregion, and community [S8] — a thread that runs forward to
  [[8.1-planetary-dharma]].
- **Structural / collective.** Harris's *humane technology* moves the locus from self to
  *system*: align technology "with humanity's best interests" by redesigning the
  environment and its incentives [S5]. Wilson's ProSocial casts itself as "Morality in the
  Machine Age," insisting that collective intelligence stay distributed among all members
  rather than "restricted to machines or human elites," on the premise that "moral systems
  are the glue" holding groups together and that the Internet Age "can either enhance or
  disrupt moral systems… depending upon how the benefits are distributed" [S2]. Mental-
  immunity training and accuracy-prompt design are public-scale versions of the same
  structural impulse [S3].

### The harm story, tested (second pass)
The first pass took the adversary's damage on the critics' word. The corpus now carries
both sides of the adolescent-wellbeing dispute that is the closest thing to a test. On
the alarm side, Twenge and colleagues report that in two national surveys of US
adolescents (N = 506,820), "depressive symptoms, suicide-related outcomes, and suicide
rates increased between 2010 and 2015, especially among females," that heavier new-media
users were more likely to report mental-health issues, and that economic factors "were not
linked" when matched by year [S19]; her 2018 study of 40,337 US children finds that "after
1 h/day of use, more hours of daily screen time were associated with lower psychological
well-being," larger among adolescents than younger children [S21]. On the other side,
Orben and Przybylski re-ran those correlations under specification-curve analysis across
three datasets (n = 355,358) precisely because large datasets are "explored with an
analytical flexibility that marks small effects as statistically significant": the
association is "negative but small, explaining at most 0.4% of the variation in
well-being," smaller than the association with being bullied and comparable to eating
potatoes, and "too small to warrant policy change" [S20]. Orben's review of more than
eighty reviews opens on the field's own admission: "There is, as yet, no scientific
consensus on the impact of screen-based lifestyles on the mental health of young people"
[S24]. Odgers's *Nature* review of Haidt's *Anxious Generation* holds two things at once:
"there is no evidence that using these platforms is rewiring children's brains or driving
an epidemic of mental illness," and "considerable reforms to these platforms are
required, given how much time young people spend on them"; bans and age restrictions
"are unlikely to be effective in practice — or worse, could backfire" [S22]. The one
policy experiment in the corpus agrees: across 30 English schools and 1,227 pupils,
restrictive phone policies reduced in-school phone and social-media time but showed "no
evidence" of better mental wellbeing or of lower overall use [S23].

The lesson for a digital-age dharma is precise [A]. The mechanism claims of [S1], [S16]
and [S17] survive this literature — supernormal stimulus and engagement-optimised outrage
are about *how* attention is captured, not about population-level depression — but the
frameworks' rhetoric of "human downgrading" and rewired brains does not, and a dharma
that installs itself by catastrophising its adversary has borrowed the spectacle's own
technique. Odgers's pairing (no epidemic; reforms still required) is the register these
frameworks should adopt, and it points, as Doctorow does [S18], to the structural end of
the spectrum.

### How these differ from traditional forms
- **Locus of the adversary:** inner craving and delusion → external industrial extraction.
  Traditional contemplative dharmas disciplined attention against the *untrained mind*;
  digital-age dharmas defend attention against *engineered* capture [S6][S1].
- **Not new — newly personalized:** the external adversary predates digitization. Debord
  dated the spectacle's birth to the late 1920s ("in 1967 it had barely forty years
  behind it," §II) and judged it fully "integrated" by 1988 [S13]. What the digital forms
  add is *individual address*: broadcast spectacle was one-way and undifferentiated,
  whereas the feed is personalized, interactive, and optimized in real time against each
  user's measured responses [S5][S6][A] — and the new frameworks add what Debord lacked,
  a tested evolutionary mechanism (supernormal stimuli, reward-system hijack) in place of
  his unfalsifiable totalizing polemic [S1][A].
- **Information regime:** scarcity → abundance. Wisdom was once rare and teacher-gated; now
  the task is filtering an engineered deluge rather than acquiring scarce insight [S6].
- **From self-cultivation to environment-design:** traditional dharma worked only on the
  practitioner; digital-age dharma must *also* act on the attention environment — design,
  regulation, platform governance — a political dimension classical contemplative paths
  lacked [S5][S2].
- **Continuity beneath the rupture:** the new frameworks *recover* the traditional truth
  that attention is ethically loaded. Buddhist *sati* was "never a neutral awareness" but a
  "morally informed and conceptually structured attentiveness" — *yoniso manasikara*,
  "appropriate attention," not "bare attention" — embedded in the eightfold path [S9].
  Williams's account of attention as the capacity to "do what you want to do" (higher-order
  volition) is a secular restatement of right intention, linking to
  [[3.2-metacognitive-function]] [S6].

### The reflexive trap: when the cure becomes the disease
The sharpest finding bridges back to [[6.1-secular-translation]]. The most celebrated
secular-translation success — mindfulness — has itself been absorbed *by* the attention
economy. Corporate and app mindfulness is deployed to make "overworked, underpaid,
stressed-out employees" more productive and resilient rather than to question the system,
shifting focus "away from starting a union or going on strike"; as critics note, "one
becomes mindful to become more productive and produce technologies of mass distraction,
which is quite an irony" [S11]. Purser's own texts are now in the corpus. The 2013 essay
that coined "McMindfulness" notes that consultants sell programmes as "Buddhist-inspired"
while assuring corporate sponsors that their brand "has relinquished all ties and
affiliations to its Buddhist origins," and that "uncoupling mindfulness from its ethical
and religious Buddhist context is understandable as an expedient move" [S25]; the 2019
*Guardian* essay makes the political charge: "anything that offers success in our unjust
society without trying to change it is not revolutionary — it just helps people cope,"
mindfulness "says the causes of suffering are disproportionately inside us, not in the
political and economic frameworks that shape how we live," and "with the retreat to the
private sphere, mindfulness becomes a religion of the self" [S26]. The mechanism is exactly the one [S9] warns of: when
mindfulness is reduced to decontextualized "bare attention" severed from ethics, it
becomes "an ethically neutral tool easily co-opted." This confirms Purser's
[[6.1-secular-translation]] thesis in a new key — a digital-age dharma that is only a
coping technique can be turned against the practitioner, becoming the system's pacifier
rather than its remedy [S11][S9].

The trap now has a documented precedent. What critics call the McMindfulness capture is
structurally the *recuperation* the Situationists named in 1957 and suffered by 1988:
subversive practice "trivialized and sterilized, after which [it] can be safely
spectacularized" [S14], dissatisfaction processed into commodity [S12], critique of the
spectacle surviving only as "spectacular critique of the spectacle" [S13]. That an
earlier attention-resistance movement — one that, unlike app mindfulness, was explicitly
political and collective — was still absorbed by its adversary is the strongest
available caution against optimism about the current wave: recuperation is not a
contingent mishap of secular mindfulness but the attention economy's standing response
to its critics [S13][S14][S11][A].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias
This note had the project's largest **coverage gap**: the shared appliedanthropology
corpus was effectively silent on the digital-age question — the survey surfaced an
AI-generated joke-essay ("the probability someone is watching is proportional to the
stupidity of your action"), not analysis. The topic sits outside the corpus's
evolutionary-anthropology and secular-Buddhism core, so 6.2 was **predominantly
externally sourced** (Harris, Williams, Newport, Odell, the *sati* scholarship); the
corpus contributed framing only.

The 2026-07-27 corpus fold **partly closes that gap, from one direction only**. It adds
Debord and the Situationist texts [S12]–[S16] — genuine primary sources that historicize
the note's adversary and supply the recuperation precedent — but the addition deepens
the corpus's constitutive anti-authoritarian, anti-capitalist lean. The gap that mattered
in July was evidentiary rather than ideological: what arrived then was more critique of
media capitalism, with no sceptical empirical literature on "the attention crisis." The
2026-08 fold closed that gap from the other side (see the second-pass paragraph below). Four handling cautions. **First**, Debord is a polemicist and the 1950s texts
are manifestos; every claim above is attributed as his position, cited as primary text,
and none is laundered into established fact — his totalizing method offers no mechanism
and no falsifiable prediction, and the 1988 *Comments* run to conspiracism
([[7.4-measurement-falsifiability]] applies to him as much as to Newport). **Second**,
the corpus's 1988 *Comments* file names no translator (its phrasing — "the integrated
spectacular" — matches none of the standard published translations, e.g. Malcolm
Imrie's for Verso, 1990); citations from it are therefore by section number (§) with
paraphrase preferred. The 1967 text is Ken Knabb's translation (translator's note in
file) and is cited by thesis number. **Third**, the Debord-to-Citton lineage is
documented (Citton engages the thesis-12 motto), but the dérive/declutter and
détournement/culture-jamming parallels are this project's structural comparisons, marked
[A] — Newport shows no Situationist debt, and Odell's engagement with Situationist
themes, while reported in reviews, was not verified against her text. **Fourth**,
Debord's 1988 self-audit is itself testimony from a defeated partisan: that he judged
recuperation total is evidence about his verdict, not proof that resistance is futile.

**Second pass (2026-08-27).** The `4-digital-age` shelf now holds the adversary's own
theorist (Zuboff [S17]), its journalist (Fisher [S16]), its anti-monopoly sceptic
(Doctorow [S18]), Newport's book in full [S7], Purser's two essays [S25][S26], Harris's
2023 talk [S5], and — the real gain — the adolescent-wellbeing dispute on both sides
(Twenge [S19][S21] vs Orben & Przybylski [S20], Orben [S24], Odgers [S22], Goodyear et al
[S23]). The note's adversary is no longer characterised only by its critics, and its harm
claims are now bracketed by a null-leaning population literature; the Synthesis has been
revised accordingly. What is still missing: Williams [S6], Odell [S8] and Citton [S10]
remain external; there is no platform-side or industry-economics defence of engagement
design (Doctorow is a critic of a different stripe, not a defender); and the wellbeing
evidence is adolescent and Anglo-American, so it tests the "epidemic" claim, not the
adult attention-capture claim the frameworks mostly make. Zuboff and Newport are
born-digital text; Zuboff's file carries a tesseract provenance header for its front
matter only. Odgers's file is a scraped *Nature* page — the review text is at seg 9,
the rest navigation.

Where the corpus did help — prosocial.world — it is advocacy for its own product. Its
genuine evidentiary value is the *mismatch* read (van Vugt, [S1]); the *moral-systems /
multilevel-selection* frame comes from Wilson's book [S2], an external source rather than
a corpus one. But prosocial.world's self-description as "Morality in the
Machine Age" and the implication that ProSocial *solves* the problem are promotional and
unevaluated, and are treated here as hypotheses rather than demonstrated efficacy.

Three further limits. **First**, "new dharmic frameworks" is an interpretive stretch: none
of these authors frames their work as a dharma, so reading humane tech / digital minimalism
/ attention ecology *as* emergent secular dharmas is the project's lens, defensible under
[[1.3-boundary-definition]] but to be named as interpretation, not finding. **Second**, the
evidence base is thin and preliminary: the claim that screens degrade theory of mind [S4],
or that minimalism durably improves well-being, is suggestive rather than established, and
the efficacy of these "dharmas" is essentially unmeasured ([[7.4-measurement-falsifiability]]).
**Third**, the individual-practice responses risk the very McMindfulness co-optation they
diagnose [S11], which pushes the real remedy toward the structural register — contested
terrain where attention-as-enclosure is a question of power, not technique
([[7.3-dharma-and-power]]). The deeper AI questions (recommender power, agency, labour
displacement) appear here only as the *engine* of the attention economy and are deferred to
[[8.2-ai-and-dharma]].
