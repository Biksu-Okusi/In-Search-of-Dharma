# 6.5 Secularisation & the "Nones"

**Question:** How large and how fast-growing is the "secular age" the essays invoke — the
religiously unaffiliated ("nones") and the "spiritual but not religious" — and is it accurate to
call it the fastest-growing religious category? *(Demographic-grounding note for the Part 7 essay;
external-sourced from Pew because the shared corpus carried the secularisation thesis but not
current measurement — a gap the 2026-08 corpus expansion has since closed; see Tensions.)*

This note grounds the opening demographic claim of the Part 7 essay, alongside
[[6.2-digital-age-dharma]].

## Synthesis
The "secular age" the essays lean on is real and large, but the popular shorthand that
the "nones" are "the fastest-growing spiritual category on Earth" needs two corrections.
**First, in the West the growth was real but is no longer current.** In the United States the
religiously unaffiliated rose from 16% of adults in 2007 to 23% in 2014 to 29% in 2023–24,
faster than any other religious category over those two decades — but Pew's own headline on
its 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study is that this rise has *levelled off*, plateauing since
about 2021 as the parallel Christian decline stabilised [S1][S4]. So a present-tense growth
claim ("more every year") is no longer safe; a past-tense Western claim ("grew faster than
any other category across the two decades to ~2020") is [S1][S4]. **Second, "nones" and
"spiritual but not religious" are different measured things.** The unaffiliated are defined by
their answer to a religious-identity question (atheist, agnostic, or "nothing in particular");
SBNR is a separate self-description, and Pew measures it as 22% of US adults [S1][S2].
Globally the unaffiliated numbered about 1.9 billion in 2020 (24.2% of humanity), up from
1.63 billion (23.3%) in 2010 — a rise of 270 million driven mainly by religious *switching*,
not birth rates, since the unaffiliated are on average old with low fertility [S3]. That
demographic headwind is exactly why "fastest-growing on Earth" cannot be stated flatly as a
durable trend [S3]. The category is large enough to carry the essays' point — that hundreds
of millions now build a life outside inherited religion — without the overstatement. **Third,
and now sourceable from inside the corpus: the "nones" are mostly not secularists.** In Britain,
where "no religion" passed half the population in 2015 and stands at 60% of 18–24-year-olds,
Woodhead finds that self-described secularists are only 13% of the nones (5% of the population)
and are not growing; most nones "are not straightforwardly secular" [S8][S9]. Across six
countries, even confirmed atheists and agnostics endorse objective moral values, human dignity
and the "deep value" of nature at rates similar to their general populations, and only a
minority of them call the universe meaningless [S10]. So the "secular age" the essays invoke
is an age of *disaffiliation*, not of unbelief — which is exactly the population a built,
non-theistic dharma is for [A].

## Findings

### The US "nones": 29%, and the plateau
Pew's 2023–24 Religious Landscape Study — fielded in English and Spanish from 17 July 2023 to
4 March 2024 among 36,908 respondents, released 26 February 2025 — finds 29% of US adults
religiously unaffiliated: 5% atheist, 6% agnostic, and 19% "nothing in particular" [S1]. The
trend line is 16% (2007) → 23% (2014) → 29% (2023–24) [S1]. The study's central framing is
stabilisation: the Christian share, after years of decline, "has been relatively stable since
2019," and the unaffiliated share, "after rising rapidly for decades, has leveled off — at
least temporarily" [S1][S4]. Pew put the question directly in a companion piece title: "Has
the rise of religious 'nones' come to an end in the U.S.?" [S4]. This is the single most
important correction for the essay: the growth is a completed two-decade surge, not a
live year-on-year climb. PRRI's independent 2023 survey (n > 5,600) gives the same shape from
a different house: 26% unaffiliated in 2023, up from 21% in 2013, with "nothing in particular"
flat (16% → 17%) while atheists (2% → 4%) and agnostics (2% → 5%) doubled — the growth in the
last decade was at the convinced end, not the indifferent end [S7]. In absolute terms Pew's
global report puts the US at roughly 101 million nones in 2020, "up 97% from a decade earlier,"
the world's second-largest unaffiliated population after China [S3].

### "Spiritual but not religious": a distinct 22%
The SBNR label comes from a separate Pew study, "Spirituality Among Americans" (survey of
11,201 US adults, 31 July–6 August 2023; published 7 December 2023) [S2]. It sorts US adults
four ways: 48% both spiritual and religious, 22% spiritual but not religious, 10% religious
but not spiritual, and 21% neither — with 70% describing themselves as spiritual in some way
[S2]. SBNR is thus a US self-description of 22% (~58 million adults), not a global headcount,
and it is not the same measure as the 29% unaffiliated: some SBNR adults are religiously
affiliated, and some "nones" are not spiritual at all [S1][S2]. The essay's standfirst figure
of "hundreds of millions" is defensible as written — conservatively so, since the adjacent
global-unaffiliated category is 1.9 billion — provided the body does not read "hundreds of
millions" as a precise SBNR count [S2][S3].

### The global picture: 1.9 billion, but growing by switching
Pew's "How the Global Religious Landscape Changed From 2010 to 2020" (released 9 June 2025,
drawing on 2,700+ censuses and surveys across 201 countries) reports the religiously
unaffiliated at 24.2% of world population (1.9 billion) in 2020, up from 23.3% (1.63 billion)
in 2010 — a gain of 0.9 percentage points and 270 million people, a 17% increase [S3]. The
report is explicit that this growth ran *against* a demographic headwind: the unaffiliated
"population is relatively old, on average, with relatively low fertility rates," so their
expansion is explained mainly by religious switching, "especially people shedding their
religious identity after having been raised as Christians," rather than by natural increase
[S3]. That mechanism is decisive for the "fastest-growing" claim: a category sustained by
switching from an ageing, low-fertility base is not guaranteed to keep growing as a share of
humanity once the pool of switchers thins. The report's own switching ledger shows where the
gain comes from: Christians lost 3.1 for every 1.0 who joined, and "most former Christians no
longer identify with any religion" [S3]. Ten countries now have unaffiliated majorities — the
Netherlands (54%), Uruguay (52%) and New Zealand (51%) joined China, North Korea, the Czech
Republic, Hong Kong, Vietnam, Macao and Japan between 2010 and 2020 — and Japan's 57%
unaffiliated against the US's 30% is the reminder that "none" is a different social fact in
each place [S3].

### Who the nones are: disaffiliated, not secular

The measurement studies count identities; the corpus now also holds the studies that probe
what the identity contains, and they converge on one correction. Woodhead's British surveys
(YouGov, 2013 and 2015) found "no religion" rising from 46% to half the adult population in
two years, pushing "Christian" into second place; among 18–24-year-olds 60% report no religion
against 27% Christian, so that "for younger people in Britain today being religious is very
much the exception rather than the norm" [S8]. But the category is not a secularist bloc:
those who call themselves secularists "amount to just 13 % of nones, and 5 % of the population,"
are disproportionately male, and "do not appear to be growing anything like as fast as the
unaffiliated" [S9]. Woodhead reads this as a third pluralism beyond Berger's two — "a growing
proportion of British people now refuse to categorise themselves as either religious or
secular, and display notably variegated beliefs which are impossible to fit into neat
religious or secular schemas" [S9]. The Understanding Unbelief programme (Brazil, China,
Denmark, Japan, UK, US) finds the same from the other end: majorities of atheists and agnostics
report "no religion," yet 28% of Danish unbelievers identify as Christians and 8% of Japanese
unbelievers as Buddhists; the "purposeless unbeliever" is a minority in all six countries; and
"with only a few exceptions, atheists and agnostics endorse the realities of objective moral
values, human dignity and attendant rights, and the 'deep value' of nature, at similar rates to
the general populations in their countries" [S10]. Mercadante's interview work adds the
texture the surveys cannot: "Nones include secularists, atheists, agnostics, humanists, the
never affiliated, the disaffiliated, and even the loosely affiliated"; the label "is
identifying someone by a lack, rather than a quality"; disaffiliation "is often not a conscious
choice or decision. It is not a movement or an organized revolution against religion" — and nones and SBNRs
have spiritual struggles of their own, not immunity from them [S11]. Zuckerman's review of the
same population closes the loop on the essays' worry that a life outside religion is a life
without a floor: atheism and secularity are, on the social-science record, "positively
correlated with societal well-being" [S12].

For the essays this is a sharper result than the headcount. What has grown is not unbelief
but the refusal of an inherited affiliation — a population that keeps most of the moral
furniture and drops the institution. That is the market a secular dharma addresses, and it is
also why the essays' word "secular" has to mean *unaffiliated* rather than *unbelieving* if it
is to describe the people it points at [A].

### The Taylor–Asad correction: "secular" is a construction, not a residue

The Part 7 essay's framing device — the "three things a dharma used to get for free," now
lost — is a functional accounting, but told as history it belongs to a genre with a name and
a famous refutation. Charles Taylor calls such accounts **subtraction stories**: "stories of
modernity in general, and secularity in particular, which explain them by human beings having
lost, or sloughed off, or liberated themselves from certain earlier, confining horizons, or
illusions, or limitations of knowledge" [S5]. Against them, *A Secular Age* argues that
"Western modernity, including its secularity, is the fruit of new inventions, newly
constructed self-understandings and related practices, and can't be explained in terms of
perennial features of human life" [S5]. Secularity on this account is not the void religion
left; it is an achieved, positive condition, whose signature invention is *exclusive
humanism* (a widely available way of living oriented entirely within this world) and whose
signature structure is the **nova effect** — an ever-multiplying plurality of belief and
unbelief options, every one of them held, now, as *an option*: contested, fragile,
reflectively chosen rather than inhabited as obvious fact [S5]. Taylor's own one-line
version: "the crucial transforming move in the process is the coming of exclusive humanism,"
after which "the new plural, non-naïve predicament allows for multiplying the options beyond
the original gamut" [S5]. Talal Asad presses the same
conclusion from anthropology: "the secular" is itself an epistemic formation with its own
genealogy, constructed alongside (not left behind by) the modern category of "religion" —
not neutral ground but a made thing — the book opens by asking "the connection between 'the
secular' as an epistemic category and 'secularism' as a political doctrine," and whether
either "can be objects of anthropological inquiry" [S6].

Two consequences for the essays follow. First, the loss-language ("the gods have fallen
silent," foundations "lost") is the believer's-eye view of what is, from the other side, a
construction site — which *strengthens* the project's dharmas-are-made thesis rather than
weakening it: exclusive humanism is precisely a built dharma, and the nova is a proliferation
of paths, not a desert [S5][A]. Second, and sharper: Taylor's fragilisation point
universalises the essay's "three lost gifts." In the secular age *no* position inherits
grounding, installation, and a cooperating world — belief is as optional, contested and
reflectively held as unbelief — so the tests Part 7 sets are not the secular dharma's special
burden but the entrance exam every dharma now sits [S5][A]. The Part 7 essay must state this
engagement rather than merely borrow Taylor's title while running the argument he refuted [A].

### Verdict on "fastest-growing spiritual category on Earth"
The claim is true only with a time-and-place qualifier. Across roughly 2007–2020, in the US
and much of the Western world, the unaffiliated were the fastest-growing religious category by
switching [S1][S3]. As a present-tense, global, durable claim it is unsafe: US growth has
plateaued [S1][S4], and global growth rests on switching against low fertility [S3]. The
Part 7 essay's revised wording — "grew faster than any other in the Western world across the
two decades to about 2020 … before its rise levelled off" — is the accurate form [S1][S3][S4].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias
On its first pass (July 2026) this note was **entirely external-sourced**, and that was itself
the corpus-bias finding: the shared YaTTi corpus (curated around Boehm, Sapolsky, Graeber,
Batchelor, Bregman) carried the *interpretation* of secularisation as a condition — Taylor's
"secular age," the disenchantment thesis — but not the current measurements. **Second pass
(2026-08-27):** the gap is closed. The three Pew reports, PRRI 2024, Woodhead's two British
papers, the Understanding Unbelief report, Mercadante and Zuckerman are now in the corpus as
born-digital text, and Taylor and Asad are present in full (both tesseract scans — quotations
above were checked against the chunks, but the OCR is a pointer, not a proof text; Taylor's
running heads are letter-spaced). Two figures could not be re-checked in-corpus and stay on the
web editions: the 2007→2014 US trend line (16% → 23%) and the 2010 global baseline (23.3%,
1.63 billion) — the ingested executive summaries carry the 2023–24 and 2020 endpoints only.
The residual lean is now the opposite of the first pass's: the expansion is measurement-heavy
and sociology-of-religion heavy, with no *defender* of the classical secularisation thesis
(Bruce, Norris & Inglehart) in the primary; the note's "plateau" reading therefore rests on the
survey houses' own framing.

Three caveats remain. **First**, the future trajectory is genuinely uncertain and the sources
pull in opposite directions: Pew's earlier *Modeling the Future of Religion in America* (2022)
projects continued unaffiliated growth *if* current switching persists [S13] — ▲ note that the
2023–24 Religious Landscape Study makes **no** projection at all and reports a plateau, so the
forward scenario must never be attributed to it [S1][S4] — while the global report stresses the
low-fertility headwind
that could cap the unaffiliated share once switching slows [S3] — so the essays should avoid
any confident forward projection. **Second**, these are overwhelmingly US and Western figures;
the global 24.2% is heavily weighted by China's large unaffiliated population, and "unaffiliated"
means very different things across cultures (a Chinese "none" and an American "nothing in
particular" are not the same social fact) [S3]. **Third**, all figures are self-report on
survey instruments whose category boundaries (spiritual / religious / affiliated) Pew itself
revises between waves, so cross-year comparisons carry method noise [S1][S2]. None of this
threatens the essays' qualitative point — that a very large, historically novel population now
seeks meaning outside inherited religion — but it forecloses the tidy "fastest-growing on Earth"
slogan. **Fourth**, the Woodhead/Lee finding cuts the other way from the demographic caveats:
if only a small minority of nones are secularists, then the essays' "secular age" is a
misnomer for the population it describes unless "secular" is read as *unaffiliated*. Stage 2
should make that reading explicit rather than let "secular" drift toward "unbelieving" [A].
