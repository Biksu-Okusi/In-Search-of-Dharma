# 6.4 Gender & Dharma

**Question:** How do dharmic systems encode, enforce, or contest gender roles? Where have dharmas been used to subordinate, and where to liberate, along gender lines?

## Synthesis
Gender is among the very first things a dharma encodes, and the historical record is
lopsided toward subordination: most codified dharmas have sacralized patriarchal
arrangements under cosmic or scriptural warrant — Hindu *strīdharma*, the Buddhist
*garudhammas*, the Confucian "three obediences," and the household codes of the Abrahamic
traditions all converge on female subordination [S4][S7][S9][S12]. Yet nearly every tradition
also carries a liberating counter-current: egalitarian counter-texts (the Buddha's own
envisaged fourfold equality; Galatians 3:28), women mystics and poets who used devotion to
walk out of patriarchy (Mīrābāī, Akka Mahādēvi, the Therīgāthā nuns), and modern feminist
reconstructions (Gross, Wadud) [S1][S5][S7][S8][S10][S11]. So **dharma per se neither
subordinates nor liberates: it is the contested terrain on which gender is fought, and the
same scripture routinely supplies both the chains and the keys.** Nor does the pattern
require a scripture: Emma Goldman's anarchist gender ethic mounts the full liberatory
move from a secular framework with no revelation to reinterpret — and her own primary
texts document her own movement re-imposing the gender order on her, which suggests the
contradiction belongs to movements as such, not to religious warrant [S16][S17][S19].
Nor is the encoding
everywhere binary: Indic law and the Buddhist Vinaya both mark third-gender categories,
sanctioning and subordinating in the same gesture [S13][S14]. Underneath, the biology
shows real but non-dichotomous, heavily overlapping sex differences entangled with culture
[S3] — so the steep gender hierarchies dharmas enforce are cultural constructions claiming
natural or cosmic necessity, and feminist reconstruction is a [[6.1-secular-translation]]
move (reclaim the egalitarian core, discard the patriarchal accretion) whose success turns
on whether the patriarchy is accretion or structure.

## Findings

### Gender as a primary object of dharmic encoding (biology vs. culture)
A gendered division of labour is ancient and near-universal in foraging societies — Ken
Wilber, synthesising the feminist anthropology of Lenski, Chafetz, and Nielsen, reports
that "an astonishing 97 percent of foraging societies" assigned hunting to men and
gathering/child-rearing to women, with strong taboos against crossing roles — yet "this
division did not translate into significant differences in status" [S2]. Steep gender
hierarchy is therefore not a simple read-out of the biological substrate; it *intensified*
with later techno-economic forms (notably plough agrarianism) [S2]. The biology itself is qualified: Robert Sapolsky argues against "the myth of sex" —
males and females are "members of the same species with patterned, structured differences
in particular biological and physiological processes, but not in others"; meta-analysis
shows the brain is a continuum of facets, not a male/female dichotomy (the kidney we never
sex, the brain we always do); "both extremes" — no sex differences or only sex differences
— "are incorrect," and personalities "overlap quite a lot" [S3].
Dharmas convert this modest, overlapping dimorphism into a steep moral hierarchy with
sacred warrant — which is the core operation this note traces.

### The subordinating mainstream (a cross-tradition convergence)
- **Hindu *strīdharma*.** The *Manusmṛti* is explicit: "By a girl, by a young woman, or
  even by an aged one, nothing must be done independently, even in her own house" — woman
  passes from father to husband to son, never autonomous. Womanhood is constructed as
  wifehood (Sanskrit *strī* = both woman and wife), and women's supposedly "uncontrollable"
  sexuality is to be tamed by self-imposed restriction [S4].
- **Buddhist *garudhammas*.** Eight "heavy rules" make *every* nun subordinate to *every*
  monk — a *bhikkhunī* "ordained even for a hundred years" must bow to "a monk ordained but
  that day" [S7]. The orthodox narrative has the Buddha reluctant to ordain women, yielding
  only under Ānanda's pressure, comparing their presence to a "blight" and "disease" [S1].
- **Confucian "three obediences and four virtues."** A woman is to "obey your father before
  marriage," "obey your husband when married," and "obey your sons in widowhood," fulfilling
  the role of "a submissive daughter, wife, and mother" (Ban Zhao, *Lessons for Women*, 1st
  c. CE) [S9].
- **Abrahamic household codes.** The *Haustafeln* of Colossians 3:18–4:1, Ephesians
  5:21–6:9 and 1 Peter 2:13–3:7 — with 1 Timothy 2:11–12 barring women from teaching
  authority — enjoin wifely submission within a divinely ordered household, fencing
  women into subordinate positions [S12] (the egalitarian counter-text is examined
  below).

Across all four, dharma supplies divine or cosmic sanction for controlling women's
autonomy, sexuality, and reproduction (cf. [[7.3-dharma-and-power]]).

### Beyond the binary: third-gender encodings
The encoding is not everywhere two-valued, and the exceptions prove the constructedness.
Classical Indic texts recognise a third category (*tṛtīyā prakṛti*, "third nature"), and
the hijra communities of South Asia persist as a named, ritually specified niche —
publicly visible, religiously licensed to bless births and weddings, and simultaneously
marginalised; India's Supreme Court gave the category modern legal standing as a "third
gender" in *NALSA v. Union of India* (2014) [S13]. The Buddhist Vinaya marks a third
category precisely by exclusion: the *paṇḍaka* ("lacking maleness," in Buddhaghosa's
five-type gloss) is barred from ordination and expelled if inadvertently ordained — an
encoding that recognises non-binary persons only to fence them out of the renunciant
path [S14]. The pattern is this note's thesis in miniature: dharmic systems do not
merely reflect a binary, they *construct* the gender categories themselves, and can
sanction a third one and subordinate it in the same gesture. There is a structural rhyme
with [[5.6-neurodivergence]]: as with atypical minds, cultures channel atypical gender
into sanctioned specialist niches — named, licensed, and kept at the margin.

### The liberating counter-current (within each tradition)
The same traditions carry their own opposition:

- **Buddhist.** Batchelor reconstructs a Buddha who "clearly envisaged a community in which
  all members — irrespective of their status as men or women, monastics or laity — are
  entirely equal," vowing not to leave the world until he had "men and women mendicants and
  men and women adherents" accomplished in the dharma [S1]. The **Therīgāthā** — 73 poems
  by or about enlightened elder nuns, the oldest collection of women's poetry in India,
  composed by "queens, courtesans, mothers, widows" — attests women's full capacity for
  awakening [S7]. Rita Gross's *Buddhism After Patriarchy* (1993) argues that "the core
  teachings of Buddhism promote gender equity rather than male dominance, despite the often
  sexist practices found in Buddhist institutions," and the lost Theravāda *bhikkhunī*
  lineage has been revived via Taiwan [S8].
- **Hindu.** Bhakti women-saints used devotion to transgress: Akka Mahādēvi "walked out of
  her marriage and wandered naked, with her body covered only by her hair"; Mīrābāī "left
  her husband and family" for pilgrimage to Krishna. "Intimacy with God offered an
  unprecedented pretext for transgressing gender norms" — often the only avenue of
  self-expression open to women [S5].
- **Christian.** Galatians 3:28 — "there is neither male nor female, for you are all one in
  Christ Jesus" — is read by egalitarians as "the fundamental Pauline theological basis for
  the inclusion of women," though complementarians contest that reading as "divorced from
  its context" [S10]: the liberatory text and the subordinating one sit in the same canon.
- **Islamic.** Amina Wadud's *Qur'an and Woman* (1999) and *Inside the Gender Jihad* (2006)
  reread scripture "from a woman's perspective," arguing "the Qur'an carries a principle of
  equality between man and woman" and that "inequality… is contrary to Islam"; her "gender
  jihad" reframes struggle as the pursuit of gender justice [S11].

### Three nuances against romanticising either pole
1. **Feminine-divine ≠ women's status.** The worship of *Śakti* venerates the goddess's
   power, but women in the same societies remain "generally disadvantaged": they are
   accepted when they embody goddess-virtues (forgiveness, compassion, tolerance) and
   "considered destructive" when assertive [S6]. Goddess-veneration does not track women's
   social position.
2. **The counter-currents were exceptional and costly.** Women mystics typically had to
   *renounce womanhood* — leave marriage, body, household — to claim spiritual authority
   [S5]; theirs was personal escape, not structural reform, and they remained a minority
   against the patriarchal norm.
3. **Reconstruction is a translation wager.** Gross and Wadud reclaim an egalitarian "core"
   and discard patriarchal "accretions" — the same essential-vs-cultural move as
   [[4.3-indigenous-oral-dharmas]] and the same operation as [[6.1-secular-translation]].
   Its validity depends on the contested claim that the patriarchy is accretion, not
   structure — which is exactly what the *garudhammas*-authenticity debate (the rules are
   "absent from Sanskrit and Chinese" versions and may be a later interpolation [S7][S1])
   and the Galatians-context debate [S10] are really about.

### A secular counter-dharma: Goldman's anarchist gender ethic (primary texts)
The liberating counter-currents above all argue *within* a religious tradition. The
corpus now carries a fully secular test case: Emma Goldman (1869–1940), who built a
public gender ethic from an anarchist framework with no scripture to reinterpret, and
whose primary texts document first-hand both the liberatory move and the movement that
resisted it. Everything below is cited as her position — she is a polemicist writing to
persuade, not a scholar reporting findings.

1. **The subordination is economic and sexual, not merely legal.** Marriage, she argues,
   "is primarily an economic arrangement, an insurance pact" in which the wife "pays for
   it with her name, her privacy, her self-respect, her very life" [S16]; already in 1897
   she had called the married woman's condition "prostitution of the worst kind, with the
   only exception that the one is legal, the other illegal," because the institution
   "always gives the man the right and power over his wife" [S17]. Where *strīdharma*
   sacralises the same dependency under cosmic warrant [S4], Goldman's target is a
   secular-plus-religious sacralisation — State, Church, property and public opinion
   jointly rendering the arrangement "sacred" [S17]. Her demand is correspondingly total:
   "I demand freedom for both sexes, freedom of action, freedom in love and freedom in
   motherhood" [S17].
2. **A critique of the emancipation movement itself.** Goldman stood at odds with the
   mainstream feminism of her period: suffrage and workplace entry, she argued, leave the
   underlying structure standing. Her Wollstonecraft lecture tells the contemporary
   suffrage movement it will find in Wollstonecraft's life "the inadequacy of mere
   external gain as a means of freeing their sex" [S18], and *Marriage and Love* is
   sardonic about an equality delivered as "the equal right with men to be exploited…
   Surely the emancipation is complete" [S16]. This adds an analytical layer the
   religious cases lack: not only can a dharma subordinate — an emancipation movement can
   stop at the political surface and leave the economic and sexual subordination in
   place. (Her opponents' case is absent from the corpus; see Tensions.)
3. **She refuses the deferral move.** Against comrades who postponed the woman question
   to after the revolution, she inverts the priority: "If we want to accomplish Anarchy,
   we must first have free women" [S17] — women's freedom as precondition, not dividend,
   of the ideal society.
4. **And she documents her own movement reproducing the subordination.** In 1889 a young
   comrade rebuked her at a strike dance: "it did not behoove an agitator to dance,"
   certainly not "with such reckless abandon"; her recorded reply — "I want freedom, the
   right to self-expression, everybody's right to beautiful, radiant things" — she framed
   as defiance "even in spite of the condemnation of my own closest comrades" [S19].
   German anarchists in Los Angeles warned that her lecture on the sex question "would
   militate against their work" [S19]. Kropotkin himself told her *Free Society* "would
   do more if it would not waste so much space discussing sex" — in his view "woman's
   equality with man had nothing to do with sex; it was a matter of brains" — and she
   records contesting it until he conceded the point [S19]. This is the note's core
   pattern in secular form: a liberatory doctrine whose own carriers re-imposed the
   gender order, exactly as the *garudhammas* sit inside a tradition whose founder
   "envisaged" equality [S1][S7] — evidence the contradiction is tradition-independent.

**Period sexology and the "masculine woman."** Goldman's 1923 letter to Magnus Hirschfeld
(published in his *Jahrbuch für sexuelle Zwischenstufen*) must be read in the period's
own terms. She thanks Hirschfeld for his "brave struggle… for the rights of people who,
by their very nature, can not find sex expression in what is commonly called 'the normal
way'" and states plainly that she does not "think such people inferior, less moral, or
less capable of fine feelings and actions" — an explicit anarchist defence of homosexual
rights, continuous with her earlier public defence of Oscar Wilde [S20]. Yet the letter's
purpose is to refute Karl von Levetzow's posthumous classification of Louise Michel as an
"Urning," and Goldman's diagnosis is feminist: von Levetzow's premise — that any woman
lacking conventional femininity must be sexually inverted — deploys arguments "used…
by all sorts of men against woman whenever she attempted to rise out of the position of
the harem inmate" [S20]. The letter cuts both ways: it defends women's right to
gender-nonconformity without sexual reclassification, while countering the sexologists
partly in their own essentialist vocabulary — re-certifying Michel as "a complete woman"
(mother-instinct, femininity, her love of cats "anything but masculine") [S20]. Even the
era's emancipatory gender science, and its emancipatory critics, negotiated inside the
categories they contested.

Two structural echoes complete the case. Goldman grounds her ethic not in scripture but
in the era's science: her argument against jealousy rests on Morgan's and Reclus's
since-superseded reconstruction of "primitive" sexual freedom, with monogamy arising
from "the domestication and ownership of women" and its attendant "sex monopoly" [S21] —
a secular dharma's warrant can date exactly as scripture does (the same operation, with
opposite sign, as the essentialist cluster flagged in Tensions). And her *Heroic Women
of the Russian Revolution* is deliberate counter-archiving — writing Taratuta, Fanya
Baron, Spiridonova and others into a revolutionary record that would otherwise have
recorded men [S22] — the secular twin of the Therīgāthā's function [S7], and her stock
of proof, in the Hirschfeld letter, that "one can be very much of a woman and at the
same time a great rebel and fighter" [S20].

### Secular dharmas and gender (reflexive)
Do secular dharmas escape the encoding? The secular-Buddhist reconstruction explicitly
foregrounds gender justice — Batchelor argues for "a complete restoration of equality
between mendicants and adherents of both sexes" [S1], and his MBEL treats prejudices such
as homophobia and racism as targets of practice alongside greed, fear and hatred
([[6.1-secular-translation]]). But secular translation can smuggle patriarchy back in —
and the Goldman material above shows the risk is as old as secular liberation itself:
already by 1900 an avowedly emancipatory movement was policing its most prominent
woman's dancing and lecturing [S19]. Three contemporary patterns are flagged here as the
note's own observation, not yet sourced: the heavily
feminised wellness/mindfulness market, the masculine skew of the modern Stoicism revival,
and reactionary quasi-dharmic movements repackaging gender-traditionalism as a secular
"order." Most tellingly, the corpus
itself shows how a *scientific* secular dharma can re-naturalise hierarchy — its
gender-biology layer is dominated by essentialist material that reads modest, overlapping
differences as fixed destiny (see Tensions). Shedding the supernatural anchor does not
automatically shed the gender hierarchy — confirming the [[6.1-secular-translation]]
crypto-religion risk and pre-loading [[7.1-failure-modes]].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias
The corpus is **two-faced** on this question. On the reconstruction side it is genuinely
strong: Batchelor's *After-Buddhism* is an excellent gender-egalitarian source that even
flags the *garudhammas* as a patriarchal interpolation "inserted into the canon by
patriarchal hard-liners" [S1]. But on the biology side it leans hard toward **essentialism**:
the retrieval surfaced a cluster of AI-generated essays built around dismissive one-liners
(Amy Alkon, EW Howe, Heinlein) that naturalise sex differences and sneer at equality
claims, plus a hereditarian voice asserting personality is "at least 60 percent biologically
determined" and citing innate toy-preferences. The genuinely careful science — Sapolsky's
"both extremes are incorrect," the brain-as-continuum [S3] — is present but was *not* the
loudest signal; the corpus tilts toward biological essentialism, which I balanced with
Sapolsky's qualified view and by importing the comparative-religion gender scholarship
(Hindu, Confucian, Islamic, Christian) wholesale [S4]–[S11]. The corpus contributes the
Buddhist case well and the biology framing badly; almost everything else is external.

Two further cautions. **First**, the evaluative frame "subordinate vs. liberate" is itself a
modern, broadly liberal standard (cf. the WEIRD critique in [[5.3-moral-psychology-development]]
and [[3.4-relativism-universalism]]); many practitioners — including many women — describe
complementarian roles as dignified and chosen, not oppression. The ethnographic record
gives that self-understanding real weight — Saba Mahmood's Cairo mosque-movement women
cultivate piety and submission *as* agency, against the liberal assumption that agency
means resistance, and R Marie Griffith's evangelical Aglow women find "unexpected forms
of power and liberation" inside doctrinal submission [S15] — and a non-privileging
treatment must register it while still naming the documented, systematic asymmetries of
autonomy and authority. **Second**, the archive is itself
patriarchal: dharmic texts were overwhelmingly authored by male elites, so the subordinating
mainstream is over-documented and women's agency under-recorded — which is precisely why the
Therīgāthā and the Bhakti poems matter so much [S5][S7]. What remains unresolved is how much
of the gendered division of labour is evolved versus constructed,
and whether the egalitarian counter-texts are the "true core" or a minority strand
retrospectively privileged by modern values — the reconstruction's central, undecided wager
([[7.4-measurement-falsifiability]]).

**The 2026-07-27 corpus addition** (the anarchist fold-in, ~40 Goldman primary texts
among ~146 files) deepens the corpus's *existing* anti-authoritarian, secular lean
rather than correcting this note's known imbalances: it supplies a first-rate secular
liberatory case but nothing new on the religious traditions, on complementarian
self-understanding, or on the biology. Three cautions were applied in using it. First,
Goldman is cited throughout as primary text: her divorce statistics, her Morgan/Reclus
anthropology and her characterisations of marriage are polemical positions, not
established findings, and are attributed as hers. Second, her quarrel with the suffrage
movement enters the note through her side only — mainstream first-wave feminism is
unrepresented in the corpus, so the emancipation-critique layer is single-voiced and
should not be read as a settled verdict on suffragism. Third, the Hirschfeld letter is
read in the period's own terms (Urning, sexual "intermediates"); its double edge — an
explicit 1923 defence of homosexual rights alongside a refutation that re-certifies
Michel's femininity partly in the sexologists' own essentialist vocabulary — is
characterised, not resolved into contemporary categories.
