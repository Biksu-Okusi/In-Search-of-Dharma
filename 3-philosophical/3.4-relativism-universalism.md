# 3.4 Relativism vs. Universalism

**Question:** If dharmas are genuinely plural, are some better than others — and by what
standard? How do dharmic frameworks avoid collapsing into relativism while resisting a single
imposed universalism?

## Synthesis

Plurality does **not** entail relativism. The corpus's "no single *The* Dharma" is *pluralism
with standards*, not "anything goes": it already judges dharmas as adaptive or maladaptive and
tests them by whether they reduce suffering and enable wise cooperation [A]. The middle path
between "anything goes" relativism and "one dharma to rule them all" monism is a **thin universal
floor + thick plural variation**: a few near-universal constraints (limits on intra-group
violence, the wrongness of recreational cruelty, basic capabilities/rights) above which genuine,
valuable diversity flourishes [S2][S3]. Isaiah Berlin gives this its rigorous form — *value
pluralism* is explicitly "an alternative to **both** moral relativism **and** moral absolutism":
plural values are *objective* (grounded in a shared human nature) yet incommensurable [S5]. The
Nussbaum–Sen **capabilities approach** supplies the floor with teeth: a universal threshold of
human capabilities, plurally realised across cultures [S6]. So **yes, some dharmas are better** —
those that meet the floor, enable flourishing under their actual conditions, stay self-correcting,
and respect other dharmas' autonomy. The standard is neither arbitrary (it is anchored in human
nature, suffering, and cooperation — [[3.3-grounding-problem]]) nor a single imposed thick
universal (it underdetermines the plural forms). The hard, unfinished part is *where exactly the
floor sits* [S6][S7]; the project's settled formulation of the standard — a dharma is better the
more fully it *holds without crushing*, and better to the extent it remains *revisable by the
people bound by it* — is the companion essay `the-better-ones.md`, which this note feeds and does
not re-argue [A]. The corpus now also carries the two strongest voices *against* the floor from the
human sciences — Dumont's hierarchy-as-value and Geertz's dismissal of "fake universals" — and the
reformer who answers Dumont from inside the tradition, Ambedkar [S22][S24][S25]. The corpus's Nietzsche primary texts now force a sharper version of the
question: his is history's most serious attempt to rank plural moralities with *no* floor at all —
by the kind of life each produces — and the note carries it as a standing challenge with a stated
price (a smuggled aristocratic ideal, a grim reception history), not as a solution [S14][S15][S17].

## Findings

### Pluralism is not relativism (the core distinction)
The Stanford taxonomy separates three things often blurred as "relativism": **descriptive**
(cultures do in fact disagree — empirically true but trivial), **metaethical** (no
culture-independent moral truth; disagreements can't be rationally resolved), and **normative**
(therefore we ought not judge other cultures) [S7]. The dharmic-pluralist stance is *none* of
these. It is **value pluralism**. Berlin draws the line himself: "We have different tastes. There is no
more to be said." — "That is relativism. But Herder's view, and Vico's, is not that: it is what I
should describe as pluralism"; "There is a world of objective values… Ends, moral principles, are
many. But not infinitely many: they must be within the human horizon. If they are not, then they
are outside the human sphere" [S5]. The values are many, often incompatible and — each society's
"gifts, values, modes of creation" being "incommensurable with one another" — not rankable on one
scale; but they are not unreal, and "we are free to criticise the values of other cultures, to
condemn them" [S5]. The corpus matches this: dharmas
"evolve, hybridize, and sometimes go extinct," subject to cultural selection in which some
increase group viability and others are maladaptive [A] — i.e. they can be *appraised*.

### Descriptive vs normative universality (defusing the argument from disagreement)
The relativist's strongest move is the argument from disagreement: cultures differ, so there is
no universal. But this conflates the descriptive with the normative. The real question "is not
whether everyone *accepts* X, but whether X is *binding* on us all." On that reading, "recreational
cruelty is wrong" is "an incontestable" moral universal: the dissenter "is obligated to avoid
recreational cruelty whether he knows it or not" [S2]. Disagreement about a norm does not refute
its bindingness — so descriptive plurality leaves normative universality intact. (Compare the two
senses of "moral universal": a universally *binding* ought vs an empirically *common* cooperation
strategy — only the first is what universalism asserts [S4].) Strikingly, the tradition's fiercest
critic of universal morality refuses the relativist inference too: Nietzsche dismisses as twin
naiveties the historians of morality who infer from consensus among civilised peoples that certain
moral propositions bind everyone, *and* those who infer from the necessary divergence of peoples'
valuations that no morality binds at all — for him the *value* of a morality is a further question
that neither agreement nor disagreement settles (GS §345) [S12]. The relativist's argument tacitly
treats *culture* as the natural unit of moral variation; but the descriptive picture is messier than
that. Moral intuitions vary systematically *within* a single population by age and sex (Bretl &
Goering, on American early adolescents) [S10], and even where norm *content* converges, the
*enforcement* machinery varies culturally — who is licensed to punish, and whether outgroup
wrongdoing is punished harder than the same act by insiders (Espín et al., on Gitano and non-Gitano
Spaniards) [S11]. At the population level the same picture holds: the MFQ-2 validation across 25 populations
(N = 3,902 in the main study) finds the *nomological network* of the moral foundations — which
foundations carry weight, and how they hang together — varying by cultural context, "consistent
with a pluralistic view of morality" [S20]. ▲ All three are descriptive psychology and sit strictly
on the *is* side of the wall: they sharpen the descriptive premise (variation is not neatly
culture-bounded) but license *no* conclusion that any intuition or enforcement style is better —
that would be the naturalistic-fallacy slide the note guards against below [A]. The argument is
also older than any of its modern parties: in the fifth-century *physis–nomos* debate Antiphon
held that "all people are equal and the source of this equality is physis", *nomos* producing
"factitious distinctions", while Callicles, on the *same* side of the debate, read nature as
grounding inequality [S21] — so "nature" has always been claimed by both the floor and its
enemies, which is why the floor must be argued for and cannot simply be read off human nature [A].

### The structure: a thin floor, thick variation
Blackford states the architecture precisely: moral systems "vary considerably," yet some virtues
(courage, honesty) are valued in any society, and "no human society can tolerate unlimited
ruthlessness… each society insists on limits to intra-group violence." A full account includes
"both the possibilities for variation in moral systems and the boundaries within which variants
proliferate" [S3]. This is exactly the Tinbergen frame of [[1.2-universal-patterns]] redeployed for
*evaluation*: a universal **function** (securing cooperation / reducing suffering) sets the floor;
plural **mechanisms** (the many dharmas) populate the space above it. Curry's seven cross-cultural
moral rules and the cooperative substrate give the floor empirical content ([[1.2-universal-patterns]]).
The classic anthropological objection to any such floor is Geertz's: the *consensus gentium*
strategy — hunting for customs "common to all local variants" of culture — yields only what Kroeber
called "fake universals", because to make a generalisation "stand up alike for the Confucians and
the Calvinists, the Zen Buddhists and the Tibetan Buddhists, one has to define it in most general
terms, indeed — so general, in fact, that whatever force it seems to have virtually evaporates";
substantial universals must be "specifically grounded in particular biological, psychological, or
sociological processes", and on that count the approach "moves away from" the essentials of the
human situation [S22]. The note takes the objection as a constraint on *how thin* the floor may
be drawn, not as a refutation: Geertz's own conclusion — "we are, in sum, incomplete or unfinished
animals who complete or finish ourselves through culture" — is the strongest available statement of
why the *thick* layer is constitutive rather than decorative, and it is the cooperation-grounded
rules of [[1.2-universal-patterns]], not a catalogue of shared customs, that are offered as the floor's
content [A].

### A primary-text exhibit: thin floor and thick particularism inside one tradition
The note argues this architecture abstractly, through Berlin and Nussbaum; the Bhagavad Gita
supplies the non-Western, non-secular *primary* exhibit it otherwise lacks. A single text holds
both poles at once. ▲ **Paraphrase only, by chapter.verse.** [S9] is an anonymous corpus
translation whose register is unverified and which supports no wording-level claim, so nothing in
this exhibit is quoted; the caste terms in particular are exactly the translation-sensitive
renderings the caveat exists to block. Its **thin universal** is soteriological and metaphysical:
the wise are said to look with an equal eye on the learned brahmin, the cow, the elephant, the dog
and the outcaste alike (5.18); the divine is described as impartial toward all beings (9.29); and
liberation is presented as *universally accessible*, reached by those the text's own hierarchy
ranks lowest — the sinful, women, traders, and those of servile birth — as surely as by the
virtuous brahmin (9.32). Yet the *same* text prescribes a **thick,
birth-indexed particularism** of duty: the fourfold caste is divinely authored "according to the
differentiation of *guṇa* and *karma*" (4.13), each *varṇa*'s work "determined according to the
three modes of nature" (18.41–48), and one's own caste-duty, however imperfect, is better than
another's (3.35, 18.47) [S9]. This is neither relativism (there *is* a universal — equal ultimate
worth) nor monism (duty is plural and station-indexed), but it is also not the same architecture as
Berlin's incommensurable-values or Nussbaum's capabilities: the floor here is a floor of ultimate
regard and access, not of this-worldly entitlement, and it coexists with a social order the
capabilities approach would judge a *floor-breach*. ▲ Cited for what the text argues, by
chapter.verse only; the anonymous corpus translation (register status *unverified*) supports no
wording-level claim, and per the non-privileging stance the Gita enters as one tradition's
self-theorisation to be tested against the floor, not as an authority over it [A].

### The capabilities approach: the worked-out middle path
Nussbaum and Sen turn the floor into a usable standard. Nussbaum lists **ten central human
capabilities** — life; bodily health; bodily integrity; senses, imagination & thought; emotions;
practical reason; affiliation; other species; play; control over one's environment — as a universal
threshold that can be *realised plurally* in different cultures; the list "is open-ended and has
undergone modification over time", and the approach gives "definite and useful guidance… only if we
formulate a definite list of the most central capabilities, even one that is tentative and
revisable" [S6]. The liberties that protect pluralism (speech, association, conscience) are placed
on the list precisely to give them "a central and nonnegotiable place" [S6]. Sen's founding move
was thinner and earlier — "some notion of 'basic capabilities': a person being able to do certain
basic things", introduced because neither utility nor Rawlsian primary goods capture what goods *do*
for differently placed people [S23] — and he has stayed wary of a fixed list, treating human rights
as "goals towards which every society could progress" and prioritising agency and democratic
deliberation [S6]. Either way this answers "by what standard?": **a dharma is better insofar as it
secures the central capabilities / enables flourishing for more people, more robustly** — without
dictating the thick cultural form that does so. Human rights function similarly as an *overlapping
consensus*: a floor reached by convergence across traditions rather than by imposition [A].

### So: are some dharmas better? Yes — on four criteria
A dharma is better insofar as it (1) **meets the floor** (limits violence and cruelty, secures basic
capabilities/rights); (2) **enables flourishing/cooperation under its actual conditions** (the
functional/adaptive test [A]); (3) **stays self-correcting and non-oppressive** (the reform capacity
of [[2.4-transmission-institutionalization]] and the protected dissent of [[3.1-tension-resolution]]);
and (4) **respects the autonomy of other dharmas** where reasonable. Worse dharmas breach the floor
(license cruelty, crush capabilities), ossify against correction, or impose themselves on others.
The project's settled statement of this standard is the companion essay `the-better-ones.md`:
criteria (1)–(2) are its substantive test (*holds without crushing*), criteria (3)–(4) its
procedural keystone (*revisable by the people bound by it*), and the essay — not this note — is
where the two are argued and weighted [A].

### A third architecture: Nietzsche's ranking without a floor
The note's middle path assumes any defensible ranking must run through a universal floor. The
corpus's Nietzsche primary texts (added 2026-07-27) carry the most serious historical attempt to
have **plurality and ranking with no floor at all** — no transcendent standard, no egalitarian
threshold, and yet emphatically not "anything goes". The descriptive plurality is stated as vividly
as anywhere in the literature: every people hangs its own table of goods over itself; the tables
conflict, each neighbour finding the other's good scandalous; each table is the voice of that
people's will to power; and the tables are *created* by peoples, not discovered or received from
heaven (Z I, "The Thousand and One Goals") [S13]. Moral philosophers err, he argues, by treating
the morality of their own environment as given and merely seeking a foundation for it; the real
problems of morality come into view only through comparison of *many* moralities, which is why a
typology and natural history of moral systems must precede any grounding attempt (BGE §186) [S14]
— with ancient polytheism read as the institutional prototype of plural norms, and monotheism's
single normal human type as the great stagnation-danger (GS §143) [S12]. But plurality is not the
terminus: moralities are then **ranked, as symptoms**. Moral judgments, though on his view never
literally true, remain invaluable as a sign-language of the kind of life — ascending or declining
— that produces them (TI, "The 'Improvers' of Mankind" §1); a healthy morality is governed by an
instinct of life, an anti-natural morality turns against the instincts of life (TI, "Morality as
Anti-Nature" §4) [S16]. Hence the Genealogy's programme: the value of moral values must itself be
questioned, by the standard of whether a morality has advanced or stunted the fullest power and
splendour attainable by the human type; settling the rank-order of values is named the philosopher's
future task, and the good-of-the-majority standpoint is expressly denied intrinsic superiority (GM
Preface §6; First Essay, concluding note) [S15]. European herd morality is accordingly declared one
kind of human morality among many, beside and above which higher moralities are or ought to be
possible (BGE §202) [S14].

What makes this a genuine *third* architecture — not the capabilities approach under another name —
is that the ranking **rejects the floor itself**. Moral systems are to yield precedence to rank
between persons: it is put forward as immoral to hold that what is right for one is right for
another (BGE §221); every elevation of the type "man" is credited to aristocratic societies with
their pathos of distance, requiring slavery in some form (§257); and generalised mutual non-injury,
taken as a fundamental principle of society, is diagnosed as a will to the denial of life (§259)
[S14]. On this note's four criteria, Nietzsche radicalises (2) — a functional test by the life a
morality produces — while repudiating (1) and (4): the floor, on his account, is not a neutral
threshold but the herd's own thick morality universalising itself. That is the exact mirror-image
of this note's WEIRD-as-universal warning, turned against the floor as such — and it is why he
enters this note as a challenge to be answered, not an ally [A]. The costs, stated plainly: (i) the
standard is **not neutral** — "what kind of life it produces" smuggles in a substantive,
aristocratic ideal of flourishing while claiming to stand outside all moralities, and whether his
evaluative standpoint can claim any privilege over its rivals is exactly what remains contested in
the scholarship [S17]; (ii) it risks **self-undermining** — by his own symptomatology his rankings
are also symptoms of a kind of life, a concession his own text makes about value-judgments of life
generally (TI, "Morality as Anti-Nature" §5) [S16]; (iii) the **reception history is grim** — his
sister's antisemitic editorial control of the literary estate and the mid-twentieth-century
might-makes-right reading that had to be laboriously undone [S18] — even as anarchists have re-read
him as an anti-authoritarian resource despite his open contempt for anarchists and socialists (BGE
§202) [S14][S19]. An option with a stated price, then, not a solution: its standing value for this
note is the demand it forces — the floor must be *argued for* against the charge of being one
dharma's self-universalisation, not assumed ([[3.3-grounding-problem]]) [A].

### A second counter-voice: Dumont's hierarchy as value, and Ambedkar's reply
Nietzsche attacks the floor from above; the anthropology of caste attacks it from the side, as
parochial. Dumont's *Homo Hierarchicus* opens by naming the confrontation — "a society founded on
principles exactly antithetical to those we honour. Our civilization is based on the premise of
equality" — and argues that "the castes teach us a fundamental social principle, hierarchy. We, in
our modern society, have adopted the principle contrary to it, but it is not without value for
understanding the nature, limits and conditions of realization of the moral and political
egalitarianism to which we are attached" [S24]. The caste ideology "is directly contradicted by the
egalitarian theory which we hold", and it is impossible to understand the one while the other is
"considered a universal truth, not simply qua a political and moral ideal — which is a declaration
of faith"; indeed "the ideal of equality, even if it is thought superior, is artificial. It
expresses a human claim" [S24]. That is the relativist challenge in its most serious ethnographic
form: the floor of equal capabilities is *our* thick dharma, and hierarchy is a coherent value
system, not a failure to reach ours [A]. ▲ Dumont is cited from an OCR'd scan; wording checked
against the chunks but not the printed page.

The reply the note needs is not a Western one, and the corpus now holds it. Ambedkar, in the
undelivered 1936 address, judges the ideal *Chaturvarnya* "utterly repellent" and refuses to rest
the objection "on mere grounds of sentiments": as a system of social organisation it "is
impracticable, is harmful"; "Chaturvarnya cannot subsist by its own inherent goodness. It must be
enforced by law", the proof being Rama's killing of the *śūdra* ascetic Shambuka; the *śūdra* was
denied wealth, knowledge and arms "lest he should have the means to rebel", which "is evidenced by
the Laws of Manu"; and the Hindus "observe Caste because they are deeply religious… what is wrong is
their religion… the enemy you must grapple with is not the people who observe Caste, but the
Shastras which teach them this religion of Caste" [S25]. This is the SEP "reformer objection" [S7]
made flesh: a standard *above* current practice, asserted from *inside* the tradition by one of its
own, against a scripture-grounded hierarchy — and it is a floor-argument (practicability, harm,
coercion), not an appeal to Western equality [A]. Mosse adds that the hard case is present tense:
caste effects persist inside India's globally integrating market economy, hidden by a scholarly and
policy "enclosure" of caste in the non-modern realm of religion and "caste politics" [S26]. So
Dumont's challenge stands as a challenge — the floor *is* somebody's thick dharma at the moment of
fixing — but the relativist conclusion does not follow from it, because the tradition's own
dissenters reach the floor without borrowing ours; the procedural keystone of `the-better-ones.md`
(revisable by those bound) is exactly what Ambedkar was demanding [A].

### Avoiding both failure modes
- **Against relativism.** It is self-refuting (the claim "there is no universal moral truth" is
  asserted as a universal truth); morally intolerable (it cannot condemn atrocity); and incoherent for
  *reform* — an internal reformer (abolitionist, suffragist) needs a standard *above* current practice
  to call that practice wrong, which normative relativism denies [S7].
- **Against imposed monism.** Berlin's incommensurability and the capabilities' plural realisation both
  block a single thick universal; the floor *underdetermines* the higher forms [S5][S6]. Moreover,
  imposing one thick dharma is itself a *floor-violation* (it denies others practical reason and
  affiliation). The characteristic trap is mistaking one's own local thick dharma for the universal
  floor — the WEIRD-as-universal error (see [[7.2-appropriation]]).

### The reflexive bite for this project
DD's non-privileging stance is **pluralism-with-a-floor, not relativism**. That is what lets it
coherently condemn failure modes ([[7.1-failure-modes]]) and pursue a defensible thin floor for
grounding machine ethics ([[8.4-encoding-dharma-machines]]). Blackford's "modern moral predicament" —
plural traditions inside one society, plus global problems (climate, pandemics, weapons) demanding
cross-tradition cooperation — is precisely why a thin universal floor is now *practically* necessary,
not merely theoretically tidy [S3] (→ [[8.1-planetary-dharma]]). The machine version of imposed
monism is already measured: the pluralistic-alignment literature distinguishes Overton, steerable
and distributionally pluralistic models and reports evidence "that standard alignment procedures
might reduce distributional pluralism in models" [S27] — averaging over a population's values is
a thick dharma installed by default, which is the danger [[8.4-encoding-dharma-machines]] names.

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **Where exactly is the floor?** The thin/thick line is contested and itself culturally inflected:
  one tradition's "floor" (gender equality, individual rights) is another's "thick imposition." This is
  the live Sen-vs-Nussbaum disagreement — Sen refuses a fixed list precisely to avoid smuggling in a
  parochial universalism; Nussbaum insists a list is needed for the standard to have teeth [S6]. Hard
  cases (FGM, caste, apostasy, the moral status of animals) sit right on the line. **Caste, worked as a
  concrete instance rather than a label:** the Gita's own scheme confines the *śūdra* to service and
  the *vaiśya* to husbandry and trade (18.44), and grounds the order in divine ordination (4.13)
  [S9], which on criteria (1) (basic
  capabilities) and (3) (self-correcting, non-oppressive) reads as a floor-breach — while the *same*
  text supplies the *internal* resource for reform, its universal-access verses (9.32, 5.18) [S9], which
  is exactly the SEP "reformer objection" mechanism the note invokes at [S7]: a standard *above* current
  practice, found *inside* the tradition, by which the practice can be called wrong. Since the 2026-08 corpus expansion
  Ambedkar's critique is in the corpus and worked above [S25], as are Dumont's defence of hierarchy
  as value [S24] and Mosse on caste's persistence [S26]; Gandhi's allegorical reading of the Gita and
  Matilal are still absent, so the adjudication remains one-sided on the *defence* of the text [A].
- **Incommensurability cuts both ways.** If values are genuinely incommensurable (Berlin), then ranking
  dharmas by an aggregate "betterness" is sometimes *impossible* — expect ties and tragic trade-offs,
  not a clean total ordering [S5].
- **"Adaptive = good" is a naturalistic-fallacy risk.** The corpus's evolutionary criterion (viable vs
  maladaptive [A]) can slide from *is* to *ought*: an adaptive-but-cruel dharma, or a just-but-fragile
  one, shows that survival-value and moral worth can come apart (the [[3.3-grounding-problem]] caution).
  This guard now has explicit peer-reviewed backing rather than resting on the note's own voice: the
  evolutionary "functional fitness gained from such mechanisms refers to incremental gains throughout
  evolutionary history and does not necessarily have anything to say about how beneficial these evolved
  psychological and cultural mechanisms are in the contexts of modern-day societies" [S10].
- **Corpus bias.** The corpus *wants* both non-privileging pluralism and the right to judge, but does not
  fully fix where the floor sits, sometimes asserting suffering-reduction as an uncontested standard. The
  philosophical machinery for holding pluralism and universalism together (Berlin, Nussbaum/Sen, the
  relativism taxonomy) is imported here from external sources.
- **Contestation may be built into the concept, not only the values.** Part of why dharmas resist a
  single ranking is that *dharma* — like *justice*, *art*, and *democracy* — is plausibly an
  **essentially contested concept** (Gallie): disagreement over its correct application is internal
  to the concept itself, so some dispute persists even once the floor is fixed. The semantic side of
  this plurality (family resemblance, open texture, definition-as-tool) is treated in
  [[1.3-boundary-definition]] [S8].
- **Cited via summaries — now mostly primary.** Berlin (*The Pursuit of the Ideal*), Nussbaum
  (2003), Sen (*Equality of What?*) and Gallie are now read in the corpus full texts; the relativism
  taxonomy [S7] and Sen's refusal of a list [S6] are still via SEP/IEP. The Gita is the note's
  *primary* dharmic exhibit [S9], and the Nietzsche corpus texts [S12–S16] now add a second
  primary-text body on the anti-floor side — in both cases for argument structure only: the
  translations are anonymous and unreliable (register status *unverified*), and the corpus holds
  **no secondary Gita scholarship** (Ambedkar, Gandhi, Matilal), so the caste hard-case stays an
  illustration pending external sources.
- **Unattributed — and non-uniform — Nietzsche translations.** None of the six Nietzsche corpus
  files names a translator or edition, and internal evidence shows they are **not even from one
  edition**: *Beyond Good and Evil* matches the early-twentieth-century Zimmern rendering (Oscar
  Levy *Complete Works* series), while *Twilight of the Idols* matches a mid-twentieth-century
  rendering (its fourth part is headed "Skirmishes of an Untimely Man", the 1954 Kaufmann title, not
  the Levy-series "Skirmishes in a War with the Age"). Handled as the Gita was handled: cited by
  section number (§) so claims are checkable against any edition, paraphrase throughout, and no
  claim rests on a translation-sensitive word choice (*Wille zur Macht*, *Ressentiment*,
  *Herrenmoral*). ▲ Known file defects: BGE §186 is textually corrupt at a load-bearing clause
  ("GIVE A BASIC" for "give a basis" — paraphrased here, never quoted); the *Twilight* file merges
  standard §4 of "Morality as Anti-Nature" into §3 and renumbers what follows (standard § numbers
  used here); and the *Twilight* filename year (1895) is wrong — written 1888, published 1889 [A].
- **The 2026-08 expansion supplies the missing anti-floor anthropology — on its own terms.** Dumont
  and Geertz are the corpus's first voices from the human sciences against a universal floor, but note
  what each is: Dumont defends hierarchy as an *ideology to be understood*, not caste as a practice to
  be kept, and is cited from a low-quality OCR scan; Geertz attacks *fake* universals, not grounded
  ones. Neither is a relativist in the SEP's normative sense, so the note still has no serious
  defender of normative relativism — arguably because the position has none [A].
- **The corpus fold cuts both ways here.** The 2026-07-27 anarchist corpus addition mostly deepens
  the corpus's existing anti-authoritarian, egalitarian lean; Nietzsche is the pointed exception —
  an explicitly anti-egalitarian counter-voice attacking the floor itself, which this note needs
  precisely because the rest of the corpus never attacks it. But note the channel: he arrives
  alongside an anarchist re-reading (Shahin) [S19], itself a selective appropriation of a thinker
  who despised anarchism (BGE §202) [S14], so even the corpus's counter-voice is curated by its
  lean [A].
