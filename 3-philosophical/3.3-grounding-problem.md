# 3.3 The Grounding Problem

**Question:** Can a secular dharma be normatively binding without metaphysical foundations?
How do dharmic frameworks confront the is/ought gap, and what grounds their authority once
divine command is removed?

## Synthesis

Yes — but only in the same qualified sense that *any* ethic can be, because the question
smuggles in a false premise: that divine command once *did* ground morality, so its removal
opens a deficit. The **Euthyphro dilemma** shows it never did — either acts are good merely
because commanded (morality is then arbitrary) or they are commanded because already good
(the standard is independent of God) [S5] — and the **Münchhausen trilemma** [S11] shows that *all*
ultimate justification terminates in regress, circularity, or a dogmatic stopping point [S11].
So no framework, theistic or secular, has the bedrock the question imagines. The **is/ought
gap** (Hume) is likewise real and bites everyone, including the science-of-morality program:
evolution explains why we *do* value (cooperation), not why we *should* — there is "no single
goal for moral behavior as a natural phenomenon" [S4]. What secular dharmas actually do is
ground bindingness in several **non-ultimate but sufficient** ways: a fallibly-held posit that
the highest good is to reduce suffering and promote flourishing [S1][A]; neo-Aristotelian
flourishing for a being of our kind (Foot) [S6]; normativity from the practical standpoint of
agency (Korsgaard) [S7]; coherentist reflective equilibrium [S8]; and the brute fact that we
are constituted to *care* (moral sentiments) [S9]. The resulting bindingness is **internalised,
practical, and conditional** rather than categorical — and Euthyphro suggests the "categorical,
divine" kind was always an illusion. **Nietzsche sharpens the whole question from the other
side**: the death of God is not a tidy subtraction, and the secular moralist who keeps the
inherited valuation while discarding its ground has — in his image — pulled her own footing out
from under her feet: the old morality "stands and falls with faith in God" (TI, "Skirmishes" §5)
[S13]. So a secular dharma cannot *inherit* compassion as self-evident; it must **re-earn** it.
The five routes above are exactly that attempt — and Nietzsche himself leaves the move open,
since on his own account a morality's origin, even in error, does not "touch the problem of its
worth" (GS §345) [S12].

## Findings

### Reframing: divine command never grounded morality (Euthyphro)
The advocate of Divine Command Theory faces a dilemma. *First horn*: if God forbids an action
*because* it is wrong, there is a standard of right and wrong independent of God's will — so God
is not the source. *Second horn*: if an action is wrong *merely because* God forbids it,
morality is arbitrary — God could command cruelty and it would become "good" [S5]. The popular
escape — grounding goodness in God's intrinsic loving nature — only "kicks the can": one must
still ask why *that* nature is good [S5]. The upshot: theism faces the grounding regress too, so
removing divine command does not create a *uniquely secular* problem.

### The is/ought gap is real — and bites the science of morality
Hume's gap (no "ought" validly follows from "is" alone) [S9] and Moore's "naturalistic fallacy" [S10] are
genuine constraints, at least on their standard readings (whether Hume intended a strict logical barrier is itself contested). Crucially, they constrain the *naturalist* programme as much as any other.
Mark Sloan's contribution to Sam Harris's *Moral Landscape* challenge makes the point from
inside evolutionary science: it identifies morality's universal *function* — increasing the
benefits of cooperation — but shows "there is no single goal for moral behavior as a natural
phenomenon," so it is "impossible that all questions of morality… must have right and wrong
answers that fall within the purview of science" [S4]. Evolution and cooperation supply a
powerful *description* (an "is"); the binding *ought* is a further, separate commitment.

### No system has ultimate foundations (the Münchhausen trilemma)
Any attempt at *ultimate* justification ends in one of three unsatisfactory outcomes: infinite
regress, circularity, or a dogmatic foundational stop [S11]. This is universal — theist, secular,
and scientific alike. So the demand that a secular dharma supply a metaphysical bedrock that
divine command supposedly provided sets a standard *nothing* meets. The honest question is not
"is it ultimately grounded?" but "is it grounded *well enough* to live by, and how?"

### Nietzsche's radicalisation: the death of God takes the morality with it
▲ **Translation caveat, at the point of use.** Every Nietzsche quotation in this section and the
next comes from a corpus file that names **no translator and no edition** [S12][S13][S14][S15];
the renderings are indicated only by internal evidence and remain **unverified**. Claims are
therefore cited by section number (§), checkable against any edition, and none rests on a
translation-sensitive word choice — but the quoted wording should not be pressed (see the bias
note below; [S13] in particular is a source the note's own discipline says to paraphrase).

Nietzsche turns the grounding problem from a technical deficit into a historical event. The
madman parable announces not merely that God is dead but that the *consequences* have not yet
arrived — the "prodigious event is still on its way," and its magnitude may demand that we
"ourselves… become Gods, merely to seem worthy of it" (GS §125) [S12]; §343 states what is
entailed: so much "was built upon" the belief that its undermining must bring down, "for
example, our entire European morality" (GS §343) [S12]. The sharpest formulation targets
secular moralists directly: those who are "rid of the Christian God" yet "believe all the more
firmly that they must cling to Christian morality" mistake the situation, because Christianity
is "a system, a whole view of things thought out together" — break out the faith in God and
"one breaks the whole"; the inherited morality "is by no means self-evident" and "stands and
falls with faith in God" (TI, "Skirmishes of an Untimely Man" §5) [S13]. *Beyond Good and Evil*
§186 extends the indictment to the foundation-hunt itself: philosophers set out to supply morality
with a basis while taking morality itself as "given," so that "the problem of morality itself
has been omitted" — grounding-projects are typically "a learned form of good faith in prevailing
morality," a refusal to let that morality be questioned [S15]. ▲ The basis-giving clause is
**paraphrased, never quoted**: the KB file is textually corrupt at exactly that point ("GIVE A
BASIC" for "give a basis"), as recorded in [[3.4-relativism-universalism]]. The charge lands squarely on
secular dharma: its central posit — reduce suffering, prioritise care and non-harm [S1][S2] —
is, in Nietzsche's diagnosis, precisely the pity-valuation he traces to declining life. The
*Genealogy*'s preface names Europe's spreading pity-morality as the road to "a new Buddhism? —
a European Buddhism? — *Nihilism*?" (GM Preface §5) [S14], and *Twilight* condemns "our morality
of sympathy" as decadence (TI, "Skirmishes" §37) [S13]. Euthyphro is no answer here: Nietzsche
is not a divine-command theorist but the mirror-image — he *agrees* the theistic ground was
illusory ("How the 'True World' Finally Became a Fable" ends with both the "true" and the
"apparent" world abolished, TI) [S13] and concludes that the inherited *content* must be
revalued, not kept on borrowed authority. A secular dharma therefore cannot simply retain
compassion as self-evident; it must positively defend the suffering/flourishing criterion —
which is what routes 1, 2 and 5 below attempt: in effect, the claim that care is not a Christian
residue but a species-wide endowment (cf. [[1.2-universal-patterns]]) [A].

### Genealogy — and whether knowing the origin dissolves the authority
The *Genealogy*'s method bears directly on this project's own. Its programme: "we need a
*critique* of moral values, *the value of these values* is for the first time to be called into
question — and for this purpose a knowledge is necessary of the conditions and circumstances out
of which these values grew" (GM Preface §6) [S14]; the guiding question is double — under what
conditions did man invent the judgments "good" and "evil," *and what value do those judgments
themselves possess* (GM Preface §3) [S14]. Does tracing a morality's descent *itself* dissolve
its authority? That would be the **genetic fallacy** — and Nietzsche, contrary to his popular
reputation, states the objection himself and accepts it: historians who criticise a morality's
origins "think that just by so doing they have criticised the morality itself," but a morality's
worth is "independent" of such opinions — "a morality could even have grown *out of* an error:
but with this knowledge the problem of its worth would not even be touched" (GS §345) [S12]. On
the reading now standard in the scholarship, genealogical findings subvert a morality only in
conjunction with a *further evaluative premise*; Nietzsche's demand is that the value question be
raised and answered, not that history answer it [S16]. This cuts both ways for the present
project [A]: DD's method is itself genealogical (etymology, evolved substrate, historical
emergence — [[1.1-etymology]], [[1.2-universal-patterns]]), so by the same rule its findings
neither validate dharma (evolved ≠ good — the is/ought gap above) nor debunk it (constructed ≠
worthless). What genealogy does do is relocate the question to exactly where this note stands:
given where the valuation came from, is it worth keeping — and on what showing?

### How secular dharmas actually ground bindingness (five converging routes)
1. **The suffering/flourishing posit, held fallibly.** The characteristic dharmic move is to take
   *dukkha* (suffering) as a brute given and posit its reduction as the highest good: "The highest
   good of human life is to reduce suffering and promote human flourishing for all" [S1]; "a
   morality of harm" centres preventing/minimising harm [A]. This is a posit, but a *non-arbitrary*
   one — it answers to something we already cannot not care about — and it is offered **fallibly**,
   as a "small-t" proposition tested in living, with explicit awareness of the proposer's partial
   standpoint [S1][S2].
2. **Neo-Aristotelian naturalism (Foot).** The good is what conduces to *flourishing for a being of
   our kind*; moral defect is a "natural defect," and practical rationality is species-relative —
   "our reasoning cannot ignore what we need as human beings" [S6]. This grounds ethics in human
   nature and function with no God and no spooky metaphysics — a natural home for "dharma" read as
   *that which sustains a well-lived human life* (cf. [[1.1-etymology]]).
3. **Constructivism (Korsgaard).** Normativity "springs from our own autonomy": we cannot occupy
   the practical standpoint — cannot act at all — without valuing, so obligation derives from the
   inescapable structure of agency rather than from an external fact [S7].
4. **Coherentism / reflective equilibrium (Rawls).** Justification need not rest on an axiom; it can
   come from *mutual support* among considered judgments, principles, and background theories,
   revised toward coherence [S8]. This fits secular dharma's fallibilism exactly — authority as
   earned coherence, not decree.
5. **Moral sentiments (Hume, Smith, the evolved substrate).** We are *bound* because we are built to
   care — moral distinctions arise from a moral sense rather than reason, and sympathy carries
   others' pain into our own feeling, so their suffering matters to us non-optionally [S9] (the
   cooperative substrate of [[1.2-universal-patterns]]; Stoelhorst's "moral sentiments" in
   [[3.1-tension-resolution]]). Reading that affective fact as a *grounding route* — binding that is
   psychological rather than logical — is this project's framing [A].

### What kind of bindingness results — and the dharmic specificity
The bindingness secured is **internalised, practical, and conditional** — closer to a *hypothetical*
imperative ("if you would reduce suffering / flourish / live well with others, then…") than to a
categorical, coercive "moral law" (cf. the etiquette/morality discussion in
[[1.3-boundary-definition]]). A dharma adds something distinctive: it grounds authority less in
propositional argument than in **demonstrated practice** — its claim is *ehipassiko*, "come and
see," tested experientially (does living this way actually reduce suffering and conduce to
flourishing?) [S2]. This relocates the ground from metaphysics to lived consequence — robust for
practice, though it still depends on the posited criterion (flourishing/suffering) doing the work.

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The strongest objection: no law without a lawgiver.** The hardest challenge (Anscombe's
  "Modern Moral Philosophy," and divine-command theorists) is that the very concept of a binding
  moral *"ought"/"obligation"* is a *law* notion that becomes incoherent once the divine lawgiver
  is removed — leaving only preferences dressed as duties [S17][S5]. The secular-dharma reply is
  two-fold: Euthyphro shows the lawgiver never coherently grounded the law anyway; and for beings
  like us, affective + practical + social bindingness is *enough* to live by, and is more honest for
  being revisable. Whether "enough" counts as "binding" is itself a value judgment — the objection
  does not fully dissolve.
- **Nietzsche's sharper version: the content does not survive either.** Anscombe targets the
  *form* of secular moral obligation; Nietzsche targets its *content*. On his account the
  compassion/suffering-reduction valuation at secular dharma's core is the specifically
  Christian (and, he adds, Buddhist) inheritance retained after its ground was abandoned — the
  "English consistency" he mocks in the Eliot passage [S13] — and pity-morality is itself a
  symptom of decline [S13][S14]. The reply available to this project is not Euthyphro but
  *revaluation*: defending care as a species-endowment and flourishing-requirement (Foot [S6];
  the moral sentiments [S9]; the cooperative substrate) rather than a theological residue.
  Whether that defence escapes circularity — vindicating care by appeal to what beings like us
  already care about — is the live nerve, and this note leaves it exposed rather than resolved
  [A].
- **Whose flourishing? Which suffering?** The suffering/flourishing posit re-imports the
  individual-vs-collective problem of [[3.1-tension-resolution]] (Mindful Solidarity itself expands
  the criterion to include harm from "oppressive social systems" [S1]); and "flourishing" can
  smuggle in substantive values — the very slide Sloan flags against Harris [S4].
- **Relativism worry.** If grounding is posited-and-coherentist, are all dharmas equally valid? That
  is the next note's problem — [[3.4-relativism-universalism]].
- **Corpus bias.** The corpus is firmly committed to a naturalist, fallibilist, suffering-reduction
  grounding (Batchelor, *Mindful Solidarity*, the prosocial science) and largely treats it as
  adequate; it under-engages the serious metaethical objection (Anscombe) and can drift toward
  assuming the evolved cooperative "is" supports its ethical "ought" — a drift its own Mark Sloan
  source actually checks [S4]. This note imports the strongest opposing view to balance. The
  2026-07 corpus fold (anarchist and philosophical primary texts) deepens the corpus's constitutive
  anti-authoritarian lean — but the Nietzsche texts within it are the
  rare *internal* counterweight: they attack the humanitarian suffering-reduction posit itself,
  which nothing else in the corpus does, and this note now carries that attack at full strength
  from primary text rather than at second hand.
- **Unnamed public-domain translations (Nietzsche).** None of the six Nietzsche KB files names
  a translator or edition. Internal evidence indicates the public-domain Oscar Levy *Complete
  Works* translations (1909–13) for most — the *Gay Science* file's editorial note credits
  "Miss Petre and Mr Cohn" for the poems, matching the Levy *Joyful Wisdom* (Thomas Common's
  prose); BGE's full-capital emphasis matches Helen Zimmern — while the *Twilight* file's
  section titles and phrasing match a much later rendering (Kaufmann's). These are indications,
  not verifications. All Nietzsche claims here are therefore cited by **section number (§)**,
  checkable against any edition; quotation is kept minimal and no claim rests on a
  translation-sensitive word choice — the discipline previously applied to the Bhagavad Gita
  (cited by chapter.verse pending a named scholarly edition). Note also the *Twilight* filename
  misdates the work: written 1888, published 1889, not 1895.
- **Cited via summaries.** Euthyphro/Divine Command Theory, Foot, Korsgaard, the Münchhausen
  trilemma, Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy" [S17], and reflective equilibrium are cited via
  encyclopedic/secondary sources, not primary texts; Hume's is/ought and Moore's naturalistic fallacy are now anchored to primary texts (the
  *Treatise* and *Principia Ethica*) alongside SEP. Nietzsche is now cited from primary texts in
  the KB (with the translation caveat above); Queloz & Cueni [S16] is cited via abstract and
  public preprint, not the full journal text.
