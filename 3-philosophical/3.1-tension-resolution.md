# 3.1 Tension Resolution

**Question:** How do dharmic systems navigate the fundamental tension between individual
autonomy and collective welfare? Provide specific examples of how different traditions
resolve this.

## Synthesis

The "fundamental tension" is real but partly **presupposed by a particular metaphysics of
the self** — the bounded, autonomous individual whose interests are prior to, and potentially
opposed to, the collective's. Once that is seen, the striking fact is that most dharmic
traditions resolve the tension less by adjudicating a fixed contest than by **revising what a
self is**. Where the self is taken as prior and atomic (modern liberalism — Rawls's
"unencumbered self"), the tension is sharp and is managed by *adjudication*: rights, limits,
contract [S8]. Where the self is taken as **constituted by its relations** (Confucian *ren*,
Ubuntu's "I am because we are," Buddhist *anattā*/interdependence, Sandel's "situated self"),
the tension largely **dissolves** — my flourishing is not separable from the community's
[S7][S8][S15][S17][S21]. Between these sit two further strategies: **integration via telos** — the individual
flourishes *through* a role (the Gita's *svadharma* performed as *niṣkāma karma*; Stoic
*oikeiōsis*) [S6][S8] — and **permanent management** — the evolutionary/institutional view that
the tension is ineliminable (within- vs between-group selection) and is *tilted*, not abolished,
by good institutions (Ostrom's design principles) [S3][S4]. A fifth strategy, now citable from
primary texts, **denies the opposition outright**: the anarchist claim that the tension is an
artefact of coercive institutions, so that where association is unforced, solidarity is the *means*
to individuality — Malatesta's formula, "the greatest solidarity to enjoy the greatest liberty"
[S10]. Its subsequent history doubles as evidence about the tension itself: within a century the
tradition split along precisely the line it claimed to have erased [S14]. The corpus's own "mydharma /
ourdharma" framing is a both/and: individual dharmas contribute to and shape collective ones
[A]. The honest verdict: **each resolution has a signature failure mode**, so a durable dharma
needs both a relational account of the self *and* a protected standpoint for individual
conscience. The strongest statement of why that standpoint is not optional is now in the corpus:
Berlin's warning that once the "real self" is identified with "a social 'whole'... a tribe, a race,
a Church, a State," one is "in a position to ignore the actual wishes of men or societies, to bully,
oppress, torture them in the name of their 'real' selves" [S23].

## Findings

### The root of the tension
Evolutionarily, the tension is not a philosopher's puzzle but a structural fact: under
**multilevel selection**, traits that raise an individual's fitness *within* a group (free-riding,
resource-grabbing) tend to lower the group's fitness, and vice versa; whether prosociality
evolves depends on whether between-group or within-group selection dominates [S3]. So
self-interest and collective welfare really can pull apart — the dharmas are devices for managing
a genuine, permanent conflict, not an illusory one ([[1.2-universal-patterns]]).

### The decisive move: revising the metaphysics of the self
Whether the tension is sharp or soft depends on the assumed self:
- **Prior/atomic self → sharp tension.** Liberalism imagines "a self that exists prior to its
  ends and freely chooses them"; the "unencumbered self" Sandel attributes to Rawls is independent
  of social and communal ties [S8]. Here autonomy and welfare are distinct goods to be *balanced*.
- **Constituted/relational self → dissolved tension.** Communitarians (Sandel, MacIntyre, Taylor)
  reply that the self is "not prior to its ends, but constituted by" them [S8] — a view the dharmic
  traditions hold strongly. If the person *is* a node in relationships, then "my good vs the group's
  good" is a false binary.

### A typology of resolution strategies (with examples)

**1. Adjudicate (liberal/rights).** Treat self and collective as distinct interests and balance
them through rights, limits, and contract. Its evolutionary blind spot is the *Homo economicus*
error — designing institutions for *Homo economicus* rather than, as Stoelhorst urges, *Homo
sapiens*, forgetting that
self-interest is "bounded by moral sentiments" (Adam Smith) [S5]. ▲ The case *for* this
strategy, absent from earlier passes, is now in the corpus in its classic form. Berlin's negative
liberty asks only "What is the area within which the subject... is or should be left to do or be
what he wants to do or be, without interference by other persons?" — a question about "the area of
control, not... its source," which is why "a liberal-minded despot" and a coercive democracy are
both conceivable [S23]. Adjudication's value is exactly that it keeps this area in view when the
other strategies lose it. Nussbaum's capabilities list is the strategy's most dharma-compatible
form: a "short list of fundamental liberties" that "a Roman Catholic citizen," "an Amish citizen"
or "a woman who believes that the seclusion of women is right" can each endorse "for political
purposes" because "no general endorsement of autonomy as an end tells her that her life is less
worthwhile than other lives" [S24] — adjudication that does not require the atomic self.

**2. Dissolve the dichotomy (relational self).**
- *Ubuntu* — "A person is a person through other persons." Tutu's gloss makes the dissolution
  explicit: "It is not, 'I think therefore I am.' It says rather: 'I am human because I belong. I
  participate, I share'" — and, in the register of interest, "It is the best form of self-interest.
  What dehumanizes you inexorably dehumanizes me" [S17]. Metz reads the Nguni maxim *umuntu ngumuntu
  ngabantu* as "not merely... an empirical claim that our survival or well-being are causally
  dependent on others" but "a normative account of what we ought to most value in life": one "can be
  more or less of a person," and one's "ultimate goal in life should be to become a (complete)
  person" through communal relationship [S15]. Human dignity is then the "inherent capacity to
  exhibit identity and solidarity with others," from which Metz derives human rights "without
  appeal to the Kantian notion of autonomy" — dissolution that still yields rights [S15]. The edge
  of the strategy is stated by its own exponent: in Menkiti's account "it is the community which
  defines the person as person," and "personhood is something at which individuals could fail"
  [S16] (developed in [[4.2-ubuntu]]).
- *Confucianism* — *ren* (仁) intertwines personal growth with communal harmony; the self is a
  "social self-consciousness of roles and relations," full personhood achieved *through*
  relationships (parent/child, etc.) ordered by *li* [S7]. Bellah's reading of the *Analects* makes
  the same point in the performative key: *ren*, "the highest ethical term in Confucianism," is
  "not theoretical, at least not in the first instance: it is performative, enactive, mimetic" —
  "something we do, and become" — best rendered "humaneness" as an aspiration rather than
  "humanity" as a given human trait [S18].
- *Buddhism* — *anattā* (no fixed self) and interdependence dissolve the very boundary that makes
  autonomy-vs-collective look like a contest [S7].

**3. Integrate via telos (flourish through role).**
- *Bhagavad Gita* — Arjuna's dilemma pits *svadharma* (his warrior duty) against *sādhāraṇa
  dharma* (universal non-harm). Krishna's resolution is *niṣkāma karma*: perform your role-duty
  without attachment to its fruits [S6] — the argument the text makes at 2.47 (one has a right to
  the action but not to its fruits) and 3.35 / 18.47 (one's own duty, however imperfect, is better
  than another's done well), now citable from the primary text in the corpus [S9]. ▲ Cited for what
  the text *argues*, by chapter.verse only: the corpus translation is anonymous and unreliable
  (see register), so no wording is quoted as authoritative. The premise usually read into this —
  that "there is no antithesis between the individual and society," the two being separable only in
  thought — is the commentator Arpita Chakraborty's gloss on the text's social and individual
  aspects of dharma, **not** wording found in the Gita itself [S6].
- *Stoicism* — *oikeiōsis* and Hierocles' "circles of concern": we begin caring for ourselves and
  progressively *draw the outer circles inward*, treating strangers as kin, until self-interest and
  others' interest converge through reason [S8][S19]. The IEP summary ties the mechanism to the
  virtues: we "naturally... identify with other people's interests (initially our parents, then
  friends, then countrymen)," and "justice is a natural extension of our concern for an
  ever-increasing circle of people" [S19]; the surviving Stoic corpus is thin on Hierocles himself,
  who is known only through fragments and Stobaeus [S20].
- *The narrative self (communitarian)* — MacIntyre supplies the primary text behind [S8]: "I
  inherit from the past of my family, my city, my tribe, my nation, a variety of debts,
  inheritances, rightful expectations and obligations. These constitute the given of my life, my
  moral starting point"; "I am born with a past; and to try to cut myself off from that past, in the
  individualist mode, is to deform my present relationships" [S21]. ▲ He immediately guards the
  dissenting standpoint this note insists on: "rebellion against my identity is always one possible
  mode of expressing it" [S21] — dissolution need not abolish dissent, though it relocates it.

**4. Manage the permanent tension institutionally.** Accept the tension as ineliminable and build
structures that tilt the balance toward cooperation. Ostrom's **core design principles** —
clear membership, monitoring, proportionality of costs and benefits, graduated sanctions, and
conflict-resolution mechanisms — are precisely "a set of conditions that favor pro-social traits"
and map onto multilevel-selection theory [S3]. Ostrom's own Nobel-lecture restatement (the Cox,
Arnold & Villamayor-Tomás 2009 revision) is now in the corpus: "clear and locally understood
boundaries between legitimate users and nonusers," rules "congruent with local social and
environmental conditions," and "the distribution of costs is proportional to the distribution of
benefits"; she reports that in a review of studies, "robust resource systems are characterized by
most of the design principles and... failures are not," and half-concedes that "best practices"
might have been the better term [S22]. At the limit, a **major evolutionary transition**
occurs when between-group selection so dominates that lower-level units cooperate as a new
higher-level individual (cells → organism; persons → society) — the tension "resolved" by
reorganisation to a higher level [S4].

**5. Deny the opposition (voluntary association).** The anarchist resolution neither subordinates
the individual nor dissolves it — and it is unusual among the note's examples in being argued
*explicitly, as a resolution of this tension*, by its own participants. Malatesta keeps the
individual fully intact: "the real, concrete being, the being who has consciousness and feels,
enjoys and suffers, is the individual," and society, "far from being superior to the individual, is
that individual's instrument and slave... And from this point of view it could be said that we are
all individualists" [S10]. What anarchism adds is an ethical requirement, not a metaphysical
revision: "It is not enough to rebel against oppression. We must refuse to be oppressors" — which
he classes as "a question of ethics," not economics [S10]. The claimed reconciliation is then
*causal*: because the lone individual "would be renouncing all the advantages of cooperation and
condemning himself to a wretched life," real freedom is produced by cooperation — the individualists
"fail to take into account... that real, concrete freedom is the outcome of solidarity and voluntary
cooperation" — condensed into the formula "The greater the possibility of communism, the greater the
possibility of individualism; in other words, the greatest solidarity to enjoy the greatest liberty"
[S10]. On this account the tension is an artefact of coercion, not of selfhood: in *At the Café* — a
dialogue Malatesta wrote to answer the standard objections, so both voices are his — the interlocutor
puts the objection in this note's exact terms ("Society means hierarchy, discipline, the submission
of the individual to the collective"), and the reply relocates the problem: "A society in the strict
sense of the word can only exist among equals"; relations of hierarchy and submission "are relations
between slaves and masters" [S11]. What replaces subordination is unforced agreement — "a communism
which is freely organised, from bottom to top, starting from individuals that unite in associations"
— bounded only by "the equal liberty of everybody" and achievable only "with the free consent of all"
[S11][S12]. Colin Ward supplies the mechanism: the "theory of spontaneous order" — given a common
need, people "will, by trial and error, by improvisation and experiment, evolve order out of the
situation," an order "more durable and more closely related to their needs than any kind of
externally imposed authority could provide" — with Kropotkin's stated aim of "the most complete
development of individuality combined with the highest development of voluntary association" [S13].
This is genuinely a fifth strategy, not a restatement: it is the mirror image of dissolution
(strategy 2), revising the *diagnosis* rather than the self — the individual stays primary, and the
opposition itself is denied as a product of domination; and unlike strategy 4 it holds the tension
to be removable, not permanent.

### Each strategy's failure mode (the honest core)
- **Adjudication → atomism/anomie**: an under-weighted collective fragments into isolated
  self-maximisers (the *Homo economicus* society) [S5].
- **Dissolution → collective tyranny**: if the self is wholly constituted by the group, there is no
  protected standpoint from which to dissent. This is "**dharmic tribalism**" — in-group belonging
  curdling into out-group hostility and insularity [A] — and it shows in the very examples: the
  Gita's *svadharma* resolution licenses Arjuna to kill his kin *because* caste-duty demands it
  (see [[7.3-dharma-and-power]]); Confucian role-hierarchy can subordinate the inferior party;
  Ubuntu can pressure conformity. The Ubuntu case now has an in-corpus critic: Matolino argues that
  the "ontological Ubuntu" which "depicts it as a philosophy unique to African affinity and views
  Africans as communalistic and Westerners as individualistic" is belied by "the reality of
  xenophobic practices," and proposes keeping only an "axiological Ubuntu" — "a prescriptive moral
  ideal to which all humanity *ought* to aspire" [S25]: the relational self as a norm, not a
  nature. Berlin names the general mechanism: the "real self... conceived as something wider than
  the individual... a tribe, a race, a Church, a State" that "by imposing its collective, or
  'organic', single will upon its recalcitrant 'members', achieves its own, and therefore their,
  'higher' freedom" [S23]. Where the standpoint is denied, it goes underground: Scott's "hidden
  transcript," the "social space in which offstage dissent to the official transcript of power
  relations may be voiced" [S26] — evidence that the individual standpoint survives dissolution
  even where it cannot be exercised.
- **Telos-integration → imposed roles**: "flourish through your role" is liberating only if the
  role is not dictated by an oppressive order (caste, gender, class).
- **Institutional management → exclusion**: Ostrom's "clear membership" boundary that enables
  in-group trust is the same boundary that defines an out-group.
- **Voluntary association → re-division**: the resolution proved unstable *inside its own
  tradition*, which is a finding about the tension, not just about anarchism. Malatesta's 1926
  essay is itself an intervention in a running communist–individualist schism whose polemics "have
  often absorbed much of our energy" [S10]; seventy years on, Murray Bookchin declared "a parting
  of the ways between social anarchists, who wish to focus their efforts on the revolutionary
  elimination of hierarchical and class society, and individualist anarchists," judged that
  anarchism "represents, even in its authentic form, a highly individualistic outlook that fosters
  a radically unfettered lifestyle, often as a substitute for mass action," and proposed a
  Communalism "not sullied by the individualism... of anarchism" [S14]. His editors record that
  this ended in Bookchin "breaking with the tradition entirely," resolving the tension "by giving
  freedom concrete institutional form in confederated popular assemblies" [S14]. The trajectory is
  diagnostic: when the voluntary-association resolution destabilised, its communal wing fell back
  on strategy 4 (institutions — assemblies, confederation) and, on Bookchin's account, its
  individualist wing on strategy 1's atomism ("lifestyle") [S14]. A tradition purpose-built to deny
  the tension reproduced it internally — corroborating the multilevel-selection view that the
  tension is permanent [S3][S4]. Malatesta half-concedes the premise himself: humans are "by
  nature, both egoistic and altruistic, biologically pre-determined I would say prior to society"
  [S11].

The upshot: because dissolution-type dharmas can erase the dissenting individual, a durable dharma
also needs the Axial achievement of a *protected critical standpoint* — the "intellectual elite
able to criticise power from outside" ([[2.1-axial-age]]) and the reform cycle ([[2.4-transmission-institutionalization]]) — so that "collective welfare" cannot silently become "the powerful's welfare" [A].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The corpus leans to a harmonious "both/and."** Its mydharma/ourdharma framing presents
  individual and collective as mutually shaping and complementary [A], and the secular-dharma stance
  favours the relational/dissolution pole. This under-weights **tragic** cases where the conflict is
  real and unresolvable without loss — Arjuna genuinely must choose; the whistle-blower genuinely is
  crushed. This note restores those.
- **Light on the liberal-rights resolution — partly repaired (2026-08-27).** Individual autonomy is
  not only a metaphysical error to be dissolved; it is also a hard-won *protection* against
  collective coercion. The 2026-08 corpus fold supplies the classic defence (Berlin [S23]), a
  pluralism-compatible form of it (Nussbaum [S24]), and — from inside the dissolution camp — Metz's
  derivation of human rights from communal dignity [S15]. What the corpus still lacks is a
  *rights-first* dharmic tradition read on its own terms; the deeper normative question is deferred to
  [[3.4-relativism-universalism]].
- **"No antithesis between individual and society" can be ideology.** The phrase is the commentator's,
  not the Gita's [S6] — but the harmonising reading it expresses is a common one, and a seamless
  reconciliation of individual duty with social order is also precisely what justifies a hereditary
  caste order. Harmony-talk can mask domination: the recurring warning of [[7.3-dharma-and-power]].
- **Evolutionary realism vs aspiration.** Multilevel selection says the tension is permanent [S3][S4];
  no dharma abolishes it. Framings that promise full reconciliation overclaim.
- **The anarchist addition (2026-07 corpus fold) deepens the corpus's constitutive lean.** The
  voluntary-association resolution now enters via sympathetic primary texts only (Malatesta, Ward,
  Bookchin); the corpus holds no external critic of it — the sharpest in-corpus critique of
  anarchist individualism is by another anarchist [S14]. Its central claim, that the
  individual–collective tension is an artefact of coercive institutions [S10], sits directly against
  this note's evolutionary finding that the tension is permanent [S3][S4]; the note keeps the latter
  as better evidenced, and treats the anarchist tradition's own internal schism as a data point for
  it. The claim's strongest empirical support in-corpus, Ward's everyday "spontaneous order" [S13],
  documents that voluntary cooperation is common — not that it suffices without the strategy-4
  scaffolding Ostrom describes.
- **Cited via summaries — now mostly from primaries.** The Gita's *argument* can be cited by
  chapter.verse from the corpus primary text [S9], not only via secondary commentary [S6]; but its
  anonymous, unreliable translation (register status *unverified*) means only argument structure,
  never wording, is drawn from it. Since the 2026-08 corpus fold, Ubuntu is read in Metz, Menkiti and
  Tutu [S15][S16][S17], the communitarian self in MacIntyre [S21], Confucian *ren* through Bellah's
  reading of the *Analects* [S18], and Ostrom's principles from her Nobel lecture [S22]. Still
  second-hand: the *Analects* themselves, Sandel's "unencumbered self" (via SEP [S8]), and Hierocles,
  whose circles survive only in fragments and are cited here from encyclopaedia summaries
  [S19][S20]. ▲ Tutu, MacIntyre and Scott are OCR'd scans (`text-ocr`): wording was checked against
  the chunks but should be re-checked against print before Stage 2 quotes it.
- **The Ubuntu critique is itself African (2026-08-27).** Matolino [S25] is the note's only critic
  of the dissolution strategy from inside the tradition that exemplifies it; the corpus still holds
  no comparable internal critic of Confucian role-ethics, so the "collective tyranny" failure mode
  rests on Berlin [S23], a liberal, for its general statement.
