# 1.2 Universal Patterns

**Question:** How does dharma represent a universal cognitive pattern that emerges
independently across cultures? What evolutionary pressures drive humans to create
dharmic frameworks?

## Synthesis

Dharma is best understood not as a concept that diffused across the world but as one
*culturally particular expression* of a *universal* underlying capacity: the human
"normative sense" — the disposition to represent, internalise, and enforce shared rules
about right conduct [S4][S7]. The cleanest way to hold these two facts together is
Tinbergen's distinction between a trait's *function* and its *mechanism*: morality has a
near-universal **function** (sustaining group cooperation), but a one-to-many set of
proximate **mechanisms** (duty, divine command, honour, dharma, Ubuntu) — so dharma-shaped
frameworks recur independently wherever the same social problem recurs, without any shared
origin [S4]. The evolutionary pressure that drives this is the cooperation-without-
exploitation dilemma: cooperation yields large gains but exposes cooperators to free-riders,
so humans evolved — genetically *and* culturally — a suite of adaptations (reciprocity,
reputation, norm-enforcement, in-group solidarity) to capture cooperation's benefits at
ever-larger scales [S2][S5][S18]. Comparative anthropology points the same way, though less
tidily than the headline summaries suggest: cooperation, reciprocity and "constant ethical
patterns" are catalogued as features with no known exceptions [S7], and the largest
cross-cultural test of the cooperative account found no society that reversed its seven rules —
961 of 962 coded observations of moral valence were positive [S18]. What that test cannot show
is that the seven are *evenly* present, or that its coding could have detected a reversal had
one existed; both points are pressed by anthropologists in a formal disciplinary debate over
the theory, and by the database builders' own audit of ethnographic coding [S19][S20]. The
honest caveat — visible too in the contested moralizing-gods literature — is that the
*direction* and *mechanism* of these pressures remain scientifically live, so the universality
claim is strong for the function and weaker for any single causal story [S8].

## Findings

### Two claims, kept distinct
The question bundles two things that must be separated to answer it well: (a) a **universal
cognitive pattern**, and (b) **dharma** as a specific instance of it.

- **The universal is the capacity, not the content.** Evolutionary psychology holds that
  "behaviors or traits that occur universally in all cultures are good candidates for
  evolutionary adaptations," cooperation among them [S1]. What is panhuman is the *machinery* —
  the moral/normative sense and the drive to create and enforce norms — not any particular
  code [S1][S4]. The point is made precisely, and in a peer-reviewed review rather than an
  encyclopedia, by Mesoudi & Thornton, who quote Boyd & Richerson on exactly this: "the same
  brain that allows the !Kung to survive in the Kalahari, also permits the Inuit to acquire the
  very different knowledge, tools, and skills necessary to live on the tundra… and the Ache the
  knowledge, tools, and skills necessary to live in the tropical forests of Paraguay" [S9]. One
  learning capacity; radically different local content.
- **Dharma is one mechanism the machinery produces.** In the corpus's own framing, dharma is
  treated as plural and adaptive — a "way, path, culture, or outlook" that recurs in many
  forms (personal, communal, professional, ecological) because each is a local solution to
  the same orientation-and-coordination problem [A]. ⚠ This framing is from an AI-authored
  corpus essay (see bias note), but it aligns with the scholarly picture.

### Why the pattern is genuinely universal (the comparative evidence)
- Donald Brown's *Human Universals* catalogues features "for which there are no known
  exceptions," explicitly including **cooperation, reciprocity, exchange, kinship,
  ethnocentrism, and gossip**, and finds "constant ethical patterns present in every single
  culture"; reciprocity in particular is a "universal cornerstone of morality" [S7]. Two of
  Brown's list-items no longer need to rest on the encyclopedia entry. **Reputation** has an
  argued, experimentally-tested account of *why* it is universal: a score maintained by
  observation and gossip that works "like money that can be used whenever the person needs
  help," drawing assistance even from strangers and across group boundaries [S14]. And
  **gossip** has been measured outside the laboratory — an experience-sampling study following
  309 people over ten days and 5,284 gossip events found everyday gossip is predominantly about
  targets' cooperativeness, that receivers believe it and update reputations accordingly, and
  that those updated evaluations change helping intentions [S15]. That is the enforcement loop
  this note asserts, observed rather than catalogued. ▲ Brown's list is a synthesis of
  ethnographic *reading*, not a database coding, which exempts it from the intercoder-reliability
  problems below but not from the deeper one: construct equivalence, whether a named universal
  picks out the same thing in each society [S20].
- Oliver Scott Curry's "morality as cooperation" operationalises this, and the primary paper —
  not the blog gloss — is now the citation of record [S18]. Curry, Mullins & Whitehouse
  content-analysed 3,460 ethnographic paragraphs drawn from the 60 societies of the eHRAF
  Probability Sample Files, coding seven candidate moral behaviours (love family, help group,
  return favours, be brave, defer to those above you in a hierarchy, be fair, respect property).
  Of 962 coded observations of moral valence, 961 were positive (p. 54); the single negative was
  a Chuuk report that open theft is admired as a display of dominance [S18]. Three qualifications
  travel with that finding wherever it is used.
  1. **The seven are not evenly present.** The average society showed **4.4 of 7** (minimum 1);
     property was observed in 54 of 60 societies (90%) but fairness in only **9 of 60 (15%)**,
     bravery in 53% and respect in 58% [S18].
  2. **Detection depended on an ethnographer happening to record a valence.** The authors concede
     that the number of morals observed per society correlates with sheer ethnographic coverage
     at **r = 0.43** (p. 55), and that the sample "cannot exclude the possibility" of
     counterexamples beyond the 60 [S18]. So **"not a single counter-example was found" is the
     honest gloss; "there are none" is not.**
  3. **Coder agreement was uneven.** Overall κ = .58 ("moderate"), ranging from κ = 0.75 for
     property down to **κ = 0.14, "poor," for fairness** [S18].
  ▲ Wording matters here: the published codebook's fifth rule reads "Being deferential,
  respectful, loyal, or obedient to those above you in a hierarchy" (p. 54), with **no
  legitimacy qualifier**. Any gloss as "defer to *legitimate* authority" launders a harder
  finding and must not be used [S18].
- **The coding pipeline itself is contested by its own architects.** Every "coded across N
  societies" claim — Curry's included — inherits the hazards catalogued in Slingerland et al.'s
  methods paper on comparative cultural databases, written by insiders (Ember is HRAF; the
  authors declare involvement in the DRH, eHRAF, Pulotu and D-PLACE) [S20]. Coding is "both a
  measurement and an inference problem," usually undocumented and never itself peer-reviewed;
  research-assistant coding "works best… [for] straightforward low-inference variables… or
  well-documented smaller-scale societies," and rubrics must specify when absence of evidence
  may be coded as absence — a live worry for any method that searches a corpus for positive
  instances [S20]. This is a constructive paper with seven recommendations, not a debunking:
  what it licenses is stating coded findings *as coded findings* and letting convergent
  ethnographic, experimental and historical evidence carry what the coding tables cannot [S20].
- Christakis's *Blueprint* names a "social suite" of universals (cooperation, friendship,
  in-group bias, social learning) and argues humans have "genetically internalised universal
  social axioms" — convergent behaviour arising "primarily as a consequence of our species'
  shared evolutionary past" [S2].

### The discipline-level counterweight (what anthropology says back)
Morality-as-cooperation is not a settled result that anthropology has absorbed; it was put to a
formal motion — "Morality is fundamentally an evolved solution to problems of social
co-operation" — at the Association of Social Anthropologists in 2018 and published as a GDAT
debate in the *JRAI*, Curry proposing and Mark Alfano seconding, Joanna Cook and Soumhya
Venkatesan opposing [S19]. Neither opponent denies evolved prosocial dispositions; what is
contested is the "fundamentally."

- **Venkatesan's methodological objections** are the ones this note must carry, because they
  bear directly on the 60-society study cited above: the eHRAF data "were not collected with MAC
  in mind," so the translation of evaluative terms is uncontrolled (as a Tamil speaker she notes
  the many words for "the good"); the sample tilts to small-scale societies while the claim is
  universalist; and the coding conflates categories, since "helping group" "is simply not the
  same thing as 'group loyalty'" [S19]. Curry's reply is that the charges are "simply false" —
  the ethnographers spoke the local languages, and no error has actually been demonstrated
  [S19]. That is the state of the dispute: not a produced counterexample, but a challenge to
  whether the instrument could have found one.
- **The vote.** The room went 13–6 for the motion beforehand and 4–24 against afterwards [S19].
  ▲ The chair warns that attendance churned between sessions, so the swing is unreliable; record
  it as atmosphere, never as evidence.
- **Comparison survives; monocausal explanation is what was attacked.** Cook, closing for the
  opposition, declines the lumper/splitter binary: comparison "is arguably the meat and bones of
  our discipline" and "is not the same as monocausal explanation," so "most good anthropology
  does a bit of splitting and a bit of lumping too" [S19]. A project like this one is licensed by
  an opponent to compare across cultures — provided it does not claim a single cause.
- **The gap the function/mechanism move cannot close.** Venkatesan's two cases — US
  anti-vaccination (helping family trumps helping group) and male-child preference in India,
  where obligations to ancestors yield sex-selective abortion — set two *cooperative* goods in
  collision, with the moral work lying in the adjudication; "there is no input/output machine" to
  settle it [S19]. Curry answers that no higher non-cooperative theory is needed, since MAC
  "mandates the more valuable co-operative option" on the single criterion "Does this behaviour
  promote co-operation?" [S19] — which assumes a commensurability he does not establish. ▲ Note
  for this project: the Tinbergen resolution below **cannot** dissolve this one, because the
  collision is between two instances of the *same* function, not between function and mechanism.
  Venkatesan's own remedy is a first-order/higher-order distinction — universal first-order
  morality may well be explicable in evolutionary terms, while higher-order *ethics*, the
  reflexive attempt to answer "how ought one to live?", does not simply "arise" as an evolved
  solution [S19]. That is a promising frame for what a *dharma* does, and belongs in
  [[1.3-boundary-definition]] or [[3.1-tension-resolution]] rather than here.

### Why function is universal but form varies (the key resolution)
The apparent tension — "universal" yet "emerges independently across cultures" — dissolves
under Tinbergen's four questions applied to morality [S4]:
- **Function:** in virtually all cultures most people have a sense of right and wrong tied
  to group welfare, and create/enforce norms accordingly [S4].
- **Phylogeny:** this is the product of *between-group selection* (Darwin's conjecture —
  moral groups out-compete fractious ones) opposed by *within-group selection* (self-serving
  individuals do well locally), which is why everyone behaves immorally sometimes and why
  specific moral content is so variable [S4].
- **Mechanism:** there is a "one-to-many relationship between the function of a trait and the
  proximate mechanisms that evolve to cause it" — duty, pleasure in helping, or "a ticket to
  heaven" can all drive the same cooperative act [S4]. **Dharma is one such proximate
  mechanism.** This is the crux: the same function recruits different cultural software.
- **Development:** core moral agency may be universal, but particular systems follow
  particular developmental paths — which is why Wilson reads Kohlberg's "highest stage" as a
  rare evolved *moral system*, not a universal developmental stage [S4].

### The evolutionary pressures, named
1. **The cooperation/exploitation dilemma.** Cooperation produces far more than solo effort
   but exposes one to exploitation; the adaptations that solve this (reciprocity, guilt,
   gratitude, punishment) are the raw material of morality [S18][S10] (and see
   [[1.1-etymology]]).
2. **Deep time in social groups.** For ~50 million years humans and ancestors lived in
   groups, making the *social* environment "as powerful as any predator" in shaping the
   genome — a feedback loop that internalised prosocial axioms [S2]. Christakis asserts a
   "social suite"; Migliano & Vinicius do the more careful job of naming *which* niche did the
   selecting — "social egalitarianism, sexual and social division of labour, extensive
   co-residence and cooperation with unrelated individuals, multilocality, fluid sociality and
   high between-camp mobility" — and argue this environment made the cognitive machinery of
   cumulative culture adaptive, which is why a gene–culture dual inheritance system evolved
   uniquely in humans [S17]. ▲ Provenance flag on the number: Christakis's ~50-million-year
   figure is inherited from the primate-sociality literature, tracing to Shultz, Opie & Atkinson
   (2011, *Nature*), which is paywalled and unverified by this project, and whose stepwise model
   of primate social evolution has been contested (Kappeler & Pozzi 2019). Keep the claim — deep
   time in social groups is not in doubt — but treat the figure as an inherited round number, not
   a measured constant [A].
3. **Scaling cooperation to non-kin.** Getting along at large scale required more than
   kin-selection. Handley & Mathew's field study of four northern-Kenyan pastoralist groups
   (Turkana, Samburu, Borana, Rendille; 793 individuals across 9 clans) gives empirical
   evidence that, under intergroup competition, **cultural group selection** — not genetic
   relatedness or geography — best explains cooperation with non-kin [S5]. ▲ That study is
   cited here at second hand and is *not* in the corpus; two experimental papers that are
   support the same causal link directly. Puurtinen & Mappes show that "competition between
   groups resolves the paradigmatic 'public goods' social dilemma and increases within-group
   cooperation and overall productivity," and — most relevant to a note about dharma — that
   group competition "intensifies the moral emotions of anger and guilt associated with
   violations of the cooperative norm" [S10]. Rebers & Koopmans confirm that people punish
   defectors more under intergroup competition, while "perverse" punishment of cooperators is
   unaffected — but add the deflationary finding that the effect reduces to conditionality on
   *expected* punishment levels [S11]. The mechanism is real; the cleanest version of the
   cultural-group-selection story is not the only reading of it [A].
4. **Norm psychology.** A capacity to acquire, internalise, and police shared norms (backed
   by gossip and reputation, both Brown universals) is what lets a group run as a "moral
   system" even when many individuals defect [S4][S7]. The canonical definition is now
   citable: norm psychology is "the suite of psychological adaptations involved in humans'
   capacity to establish and enforce social norms," and it cannot be understood apart from
   group identification — which is why dharmas are identity-constituting rather than merely
   rule-listing [S13]. Internalisation has a mechanism too: children acquire local norms
   early, and reward circuitry activates on compliance, making "norm compliance a goal rather
   than a burden" [S12]. That is the difference between obeying a rule and holding a dharma [A].

These pressures jointly predict exactly what we observe: independent cultures repeatedly
inventing comprehensive normative frameworks — dharmas — that bind values, duties, stories,
and practices into a cooperation-sustaining whole [S2][A][S5].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **Corpus self-reference / AI-authored sources.** The shared-corpus survey for *dharma
  specifically* returned mostly LLM-generated essays (`define-dharma-in-its-broadest-sense`,
  `why-is-the-concept-of-dharma-important_scholarly`, `Dharma-according-to-Grok-4`) plus
  AI-written aphorism commentaries. The substantive evidence here therefore rests on the
  corpus's *real* books and studies (Christakis, Curry, Wilson, Handley & Mathew) and on
  external scholarship (Brown), **not** on the dharma-essays — which are used only for the
  project's framing [A]. [bias flag]
- **Morality-as-cooperation was previously cited here at blog strength.** The earlier version of
  this note carried Curry's seven rules through a prosocial.world post [S6], which reports the
  headline and none of the argument around it. The primary literature is simultaneously
  *stronger* and *more contested*: stronger because the study is peer-reviewed, the figures are
  public, and it has been scaled up in a 256-society successor (Alfano, Cheong & Curry, *Heliyon*
  2024) [S18][S20]; more contested because the same issue of *Current Anthropology* carries four
  commentaries — Bloom on falsifiability and on sexual morality ("it is hardly plausible that
  they are upset that he's a bad cooperator!"), Gintis in support, Smith & Kurzban's "Morality Is
  Not Always Good" arguing that moral cognition is non-consequentialist and that many norms
  police no social interaction at all, and Wong arguing that "cooperation" may be "too
  indeterminate and broad" — plus the authors' Reply, which concedes that sexual morality is
  "undertheorized" and that morals do sometimes go wrong, while converting each objection into a
  prediction the theory is said already to have made [S18]. Add the formal anthropological debate
  [S19] and the insider methods critique [S20] and the position is clear: **Stage 2 must carry
  the dispute, not just the finding.** ▲ Note also that the 256-society successor runs the same
  pipeline, so it multiplies coverage rather than repairing the coding exposure [S20]; and that
  Curry's contribution to the debate was funded by the Templeton World Charity Foundation [S19].
- **Adaptationist lean, and its live contestation.** The corpus leans hard on the
  "morality/religion evolved for cooperation" narrative. That narrative is *contested* in
  exactly the place it is strongest-sounding: Whitehouse et al. (2019, *Nature*) found that
  complex societies **precede** moralizing gods (i.e. prosocial religion looks more like a
  consequence than a cause), and after a coding/missing-data critique by Beheim et al. (2021)
  the original Letter was **retracted by its authors** — who nonetheless argue the corrected
  analysis preserves the main finding [S8]. The lesson for this project: the *function*
  universal (morality tracks cooperation) is robust; any single *causal* story (e.g. "big
  gods built civilisation") is not, and should be cited cautiously. ▲ The caveat no longer
  depends on that contested exchange alone. A phylogenetic reconstruction across a global
  hunter-gatherer sample, using a time-calibrated supertree and therefore an entirely
  independent method, finds animism ancestral and concludes that "ancestor spirits or high gods
  who are active in human affairs were absent in early humans, suggesting a deep history for the
  egalitarian nature of hunter-gatherer societies" [S16]. Two unrelated lines of evidence now
  converge on moralising gods being late, which is a stronger position than one retracted paper.
- **Universality is statistical, not exceptionless.** Even the strongest sources hedge:
  morality is universal "in virtually all cultures," with psychopaths and chronic defectors
  as real exceptions [S4]. "Universal cognitive pattern" should be read as *species-typical
  and reliably developing*, not *invariant in every individual*.
- **Evolutionary psychology's own methodological critiques** (testability, the modularity
  assumption, uncertainty about ancestral environments) apply and are acknowledged even by
  the corpus's evo-psych source [S1]; "good candidate for an adaptation" is an inference, not
  a proof.
- **Provenance, substantially repaired but not complete.** An earlier version of this note
  rested on three prosocial.world posts, two Wikipedia derivatives and a trade book — five of
  seven refs secondary or tertiary. The capacity/content distinction [S9], the scaling-to-non-kin
  pressure [S10][S11], norm psychology [S12][S13], reputation and gossip [S14][S15], the
  gene–culture claim [S17] and the moralising-gods caveat [S16] now carry peer-reviewed primary
  sources; the morality-as-cooperation claim, previously carried by a blog post [S6], now rests
  on the published article with its commentaries and reply [S18], flanked by the disciplinary
  debate [S19] and the coding-methods audit [S20] — [S6] is retained only as the popular gloss
  and should carry no weight. What remains secondary: **[S1]** (a corpus copy of the Wikipedia evolutionary-psychology
  article) is retained only for evo-psych's self-critique; **[S7]** Brown (1991) is still cited via
  summary for the catalogue as a whole; **[S5]** Handley & Mathew is still second-hand and absent
  from the corpus — the claim is now carried by [S10][S11] instead. **[S4]**, Wilson's Tinbergen
  framing, is this note's structural spine, is his own synthesis, and has no primary substitute;
  it stays as-is [A].
- **Gaps for later/primary work.** Tomasello's *shared-intentionality* account of how obligation
  arises ("I" → "we" cognition) is now reachable in the corpus (O'Madagain & Tomasello 2022;
  Tomasello & Gonzalez-Cabrera 2017) but is a genuinely new argumentative move — that a dharma is
  a set of shared *reasons* rather than merely shared norms — and should be developed on its own
  terms in [[1.3-boundary-definition]] or [[5.2-evolutionary-psychology]], not smuggled in here as
  a citation swap [A]. Boyer/Atran's cognitive science of religion and Henrich's gene–culture
  coevolution remain lightly touched.
