# 1.1 Core Etymology

**Question:** Trace the etymology of 'dharma' beyond Sanskrit to Proto-Indo-European
roots. What cognitive and social functions do these linguistic origins reveal about
humanity's need for ethical frameworks?

## Synthesis

*Dharma* descends from the Sanskrit verbal root **√dhṛ** ("to hold, support, sustain"),
itself reflecting Proto-Indo-European **\*dʰer-** ("to hold, support"; Pokorny's etymon
*2. dher-*), giving the literal sense "that which holds / upholds" [S11][S7]. Its earliest
attested form is the Vedic *n*-stem **dhárman-** ("bearer, supporter"; "something established
or firm"), formulaically paired with **ṛta** — a word the Iranist mainstream renders "truth"
rather than "cosmic order" [S14] — in the oldest recoverable Indo-Iranian stratum [S8][S14].
The thematic noun *dharma-* did eventually come to cover duty, law, righteousness, and proper
conduct, but not by smooth internal broadening: across the middle and late Vedic corpora the
term was marginal and narrowing (royal and juridical), and its centrality arrives with
Buddhist and Aśokan usage [S12] (see Findings). The etymology still encodes one deep
metaphor — *order as that which holds reality together* — applied, though not in a smooth
historical sequence, to cosmos, society, and self [A]. Read through the corpus's secular / evolutionary
lens, that "holding" names a recurring human need: ethical frameworks are social
technologies that hold groups together by solving the cooperation-without-exploitation
problem, which is why convergent moral patterns recur across unrelated cultures [A][S4].
The linguistic origin and the social function thus point at the same fact — humans need
*something to hold* behaviour predictable and cooperative under impermanence and anonymity.

## Findings

### The Sanskrit root and its literal meaning
*Dharma* (धर्म) derives from the Sanskrit verbal root **√dhṛ** (धृ), "to hold," "to
maintain," "to support," "to sustain" [A][S7]. The literal nominal sense is therefore
"that which holds / upholds / sustains" — whether the referent is the cosmos, society, or
an individual life [A]. The term passes into Pali and Prakrit as **dhamma** [A][S8].

### Proto-Indo-European depth
The root traces to Proto-Indo-European **\*dʰer-** ("to hold, support"; Pokorny's etymon
*2. dher-, dherə-*, semantic field "to Hold") → Proto-Indo-Iranian **\*dʰar-** → Sanskrit
class-1 root √dhṛ [S11][S7]. Documented reflexes include Latin *firmus* ("firm, strong";
whence English *firm*) and *frēnum* ("rein"); Avestan *dar-* ("to hold, keep") and Old
Persian *dar-* ("to hold") — including the royal name *Dāraya-vahu-* (**Darius**), glossed
"upholding the good"; Greek *thronos* ("throne, chair"), whence, via Latin *thronus*,
English **throne**; Lithuanian *derė́ti* ("to be suitable, to fit"); and Old Church
Slavonic *drъžati* ("to hold, possess") [S11][S7]. ▲ Citation hazard: the LRC's adjacent
master entry 0398 is the *homophonous* root *1. dher-* "dirt, dregs" — one digit away from
entry 0399 and semantically disastrous if cited by accident [S11]. The citation of record
for √dhṛ itself remains Manfred Mayrhofer's *Etymologisches Wörterbuch des Altindoarischen*
(1992), still cited here via Wiktionary [S7].

### Vedic dhárman- and ṛta
The earliest form is the Vedic *n*-stem **dhárman-**, with a literal sense of "something
established or firm" (in the concrete sense of props or poles) and a figurative sense of
"sustainer / supporter" of the deities — semantically parallel to Greek **themis**
("fixed decree, statute, law") [S8]. Wikipedia's Horsch-derived framing makes it an aspect
of **ṛta**, glossed "cosmic order," with *dharma* in effect the *application of ṛta to
human action* [S8]. Two corrections from the primary literature: the standard Iranist
rendering of ṛta (Avestan *aša*) is **"truth"** — opposed to the Lie, with "order" only a
partial overlap (Schlerath and Skjærvø, following Lüders) [S14] — and Halbfass finds the
Vedic linkages between *ṛta* and *dharma* "casual and rare," the close-connection claim
being largely a modern postulate [S15]. What *is* verified, and older than either gloss:
the reconstructed Indo-Iranian formula **\**ṛtām dhar-*** ("to uphold the truth"), attested
in both Yasna 43.1 and the Rigveda and so recoverable from the common Indo-Iranian stratum
(~2000 BCE) — dharma's root formulaically paired with ṛta in the oldest shared poetry
[S14]. *Dhárman* occurs some five dozen times in the Rigveda (63 per Brereton as reported
by Wikipedia [S8]; 67 per the counts Olivelle reports after Horsch and Brereton [S12] —
the discrepancy is unresolved because Brereton's paper remains unobtained, being
login-walled), alongside forms such as *dharmakṛt*, *satyadharman*, *dharmavant*,
*dhariman* [S8]. Paul Horsch argues that in
the Atharvaveda the term gains a "dynamic functional sense," becoming a "cosmic law that
links cause and effect"; and the *Monier-Williams* dictionary later glosses the thematic
noun across "that which is established or firm... decree, statute, law, practice, custom,
duty, right, justice, virtue, morality, ethics, religion" [S8].

That widening — from a concrete prop to law/duty/virtue — looks like a textbook case
of **semantic broadening** (generalisation), one of the regular mechanisms (with narrowing,
amelioration, and pejoration) by which word meanings drift over time [S10]. The corpus's own
"defining definition" essay names this "evolution over time" as intrinsic to meaning — instancing
the redefinition of *planet* that reclassified Pluto — and stresses that "language is fluid" and
cultural difference adds further layers [A]. But the primary philology shows the path was
anything but a smooth widening.

Olivelle's survey of the whole middle and late Vedic corpus finds that between the Ṛgveda and
the Buddhists, dharma "was at best a marginal term": 22 passages across the four Yajurveda
Saṃhitās, 11 in the Brāhmaṇas, three in the Āraṇyakas, nine in the early prose Upaniṣads —
texts where "we would expect to find a sustained treatment of dharma... That, however, is not
the case" — and its semantic range **narrowed**, "restricted for the most part to Varuṇa and
his earthly counterpart, the king": royal and juridical vocabulary, not a general ethic [S12].
On Olivelle's hypothesis (his own word for it, used repeatedly — this part is conjecture, the
counts are not), centrality arrived from outside orthodox Brahmanism: the early Buddhists
borrowed *dharma* along with a suite of royal symbolism (the wheel, the *cakravartin*, the
*śāsana* as counterpart of a royal edict), Aśoka's edicts amplified it imperially (~111 uses
of *dharma* in that brief corpus, excluding repetitions, against the double-digit tallies of
the vastly larger Vedic literature), and the Brahmanical Dharmaśāstra literature emerged as a
*response* to Buddhist and Aśokan reforms [S12]. On the Buddhist side, Gethin shows the senses
of *dhamma* (teaching, conduct, truth, nature, natural law, basic mental or physical
"quality") are neither homonyms nor accidents — he rejects homonymy outright: they cohere
genealogically and functionally around the root-notion of supporting/maintaining; and the
Vedic usage was plural-first (*dharmāṇi*, the "first dharmas" of ritual), the singular
abstract gaining prominence only later [S13]. So dharma's protean range is still an ordinary
linguistic process observed in slow motion across three millennia — but the mechanism was
marginality, then appropriation and contest between interested parties, not gentle
generalisation.

### What the origins reveal: cognitive & social functions
**Cognitive (the root metaphor).** A single embodied image — *holding / supporting /
making-firm* — is projected from physical stability onto moral and cosmic order. Ethics is
conceptualised spatially, as a structure that keeps things from collapsing; this is why the
same word can mean a literal prop, the order of the universe, and a person's duty [A].

**Social (dharma as coordination technology).** Anthropologically, a dharma is a "bundle"
of values, norms, stories, practices, and institutions [A] that operates as a "social
technology" solving enduring coordination problems — trust, conflict, belonging, and
meaning — by making behaviour predictable and enabling cooperation at scale [A].

**Evolutionary (why the need is universal).** In the prosocial corpus, morality is
"always and everywhere a cooperative phenomenon" that evolved to capture the benefits of
cooperation without being destroyed by exploitation [S4][S5]. Curry, Mullins and
Whitehouse's "morality as cooperation" study content-analysed ethnographic accounts from 60
societies (>600,000 words) and found seven recurrent moral rules — love your family, help
your group, return favours, be brave, defer to those above you in a hierarchy, be fair,
respect others' property — for which not a single counter-example was found (961 of 962
coded valence observations were positive) [S4]. Cross-cultural moral diversity is then explained largely by *whom* one
must cooperate with and the in-group/out-group markers that bound it, not by the absence of
a shared cooperative core [S6]. This is why "dharma-shaped" frameworks recur independently:
they are cultural elaborations on a common cooperative substrate. The linguistic need
("something to hold us") and the evolutionary need ("something to hold cooperation
together") are two readings of one fact.

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The corpus is partly self-referential / AI-generated.** Several shared-corpus
  "sources" (`etymology-origins-of-dharma.md`, `defining-dharma_gpt41.md`,
  `Dharma-according-to-Grok-4.md`, `combined_dharma_keep_notes.txt`) are themselves
  LLM-authored essays — some apparently produced by the sibling *DefiningDharma* pipeline —
  not primary philology. They agree on the √dhṛ root but cannot count as *independent*
  authorities. This is precisely why METHODOLOGY mandates an external philology pass; the
  PIE reconstruction now rests on Pokorny via the LRC adaptation [S11] (Wiktionary retained
  as a convenience link), the Vedic semantic history on Olivelle and Gethin first-hand
  [S12][S13], and Wikipedia remains the conduit only for Horsch and Brereton [S8].
- **Secular / evolutionary lean.** The corpus frames dharma functionally (social
  technology, cooperation) and explicitly de-privileges religious/metaphysical readings.
  That lens is productive here but underweights the *internal* self-understanding of dharma
  in Hindu and Buddhist traditions, where it is experienced as revealed or transcendent
  rather than merely adaptive. The Vedic *ṛta* framing [S8] — even in the Iranist "truth"
  rendering rather than "cosmic order" [S14] — partially restores that dimension.
- **A philological caveat the corpus missed.** The *word* is securely PIE (√dhṛ < \*dʰer-),
  but the religious/ethical *concept* dharman looks substantially **Vedic-internal**: the
  only Iranian counterpart, Old Persian *darmān*, means "remedy" — semantically divergent —
  which suggests the ethical sense developed after the Indo-Iranian split [S8]. So "PIE
  roots" ground the etymon, not the concept of cosmic/ethical order; the corpus's confident
  cognate lists (*firmus*, *derė́ti*) risk implying more *conceptual* continuity than the
  evidence supports.
- **A verify-don't-trust instance.** An automated search initially reported the Greek
  parallel as *ethos*; the primary article gives *themis*. Corrected above. Minor, but it
  is exactly the kind of slip the citation discipline is meant to catch.
- **The note's earlier smooth-broadening framing followed tertiary sources.** An earlier
  revision of this note narrated a tidy cosmic→social→personal widening, on the strength of
  Wikipedia and corpus essays. The primary literature tells a different story —
  marginality-then-appropriation: dharma contracted into royal/juridical vocabulary across
  the middle and late Vedic corpora and was made central by Buddhist borrowing and Aśokan
  imperial usage, on Olivelle's (explicitly flagged) hypothesis [S12][S13]. Likewise the
  ṛta = "cosmic order" gloss is contested by the Iranist mainstream, whose standard
  rendering is "truth" [S14], and the ṛta→dharma derivation itself is largely a modern
  postulate [S15].
- **Unconsulted primaries (narrowed gap).** Mayrhofer (*EWAia*), Horsch, and Brereton
  remain cited second-hand (Brereton's paper is login-walled and unobtained — hence the
  63-vs-67 count discrepancy left unresolved above); Olivelle and Gethin are now consulted
  first-hand [S12][S13]. A Stage-2 essay leaning hard on the philology should still consult
  Mayrhofer and Brereton directly.
