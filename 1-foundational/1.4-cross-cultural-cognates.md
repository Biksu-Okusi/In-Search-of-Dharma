# 1.4 Cross-cultural Cognates

**Question:** How does dharma compare with cognate ordering concepts from other
traditions — Greek *nomos* and *logos*, Chinese *Tao*, Vedic *ṛta*, Egyptian *ma'at*,
and Western *natural law*? What is genuinely shared across them, and what is distinct
to each?

## Synthesis

Across the great civilisations, complex societies independently converged on a single
idea: that reality has an intrinsic **order** which fuses the cosmic, the social, and the
moral, and that human virtue consists in *aligning* with it while disorder (chaos, the lie,
anomie) consists in violating it [A][S5][S8][S19][S21]. Ṛta, Egyptian *ma'at*, Zoroastrian
*asha*, the Greek *logos*, the Chinese *Tao*, and the Western *natural law* are all variants
of this "cosmic-order" intuition — the same "that which holds" impulse traced etymologically
in [[1.1-etymology]] — and beneath their divergent cosmologies sits a convergent behavioural
kernel (the near-universal Golden Rule, reciprocity) [S4]. But they are **not all the same
kind of thing**: they separate along two axes — order *discovered* (ṛta, asha, ma'at, Tao,
logos, natural law) vs order *made* (Greek *nomos*, which is actually convention), and
order *personal/sacralised* (ma'at, asha, ṛta) vs *impersonal/rational* (Tao, logos, natural
law) [S16][S23][S25][S27]. Dharma's oft-cited distinctive contribution is a **two-level
architecture** — ṛta naming the impersonal cosmic order, *dharma* its application to human
conduct and role [S7][S19] — though that tidy two-storey reading is itself contested: per
Halbfass the Vedic linkages between the two words are "casual and rare," and the
close-connection claim is largely a modern (Neo-Hindu) postulate [S15]. If "dharma" ports
readily into secular use, the carving-off of a lived, this-worldly layer from the cosmology
owes more to modern reinterpreters than to the Vedic texts themselves [S15].

## Findings

### A category correction first
The question (and the corpus, which lists "Ma'at, Kittu, Dao, Ṛta/Dharma" together as
"comprehensive orders... part cosmology, part law, part etiquette" [A]) lumps two different
classes:
- **Cosmic-order cognates** — ṛta, ma'at, asha, Tao, logos, natural law: principles that an
  order *exists in reality* and conduct should conform to it.
- **Greek *nomos* is the outlier.** In the Greek *nomos / physis* debate, *physis* is nature
  (how things are independent of us) and *nomos* is **convention** — the human-made laws,
  customs, and moral codes — *ho nomizetai*, "what is customarily" [S23]. Antiphon put the
  contrast at its sharpest: "the works of law are factitious, whereas those of nature are
  necessary; and the works of law, being conventional, are not natural" — so the
  law-breaker who escapes notice escapes harm, while the violator of nature is harmed "not
  because of opinion but in truth" [S23]. The Sophists used exactly this to argue that
  justice is *nomos*, not *physis* [S23]. So *nomos*
  belongs with custom/law as dharma's **contrast** (see [[1.3-boundary-definition]]), not with
  the cosmic-order family. The true Greek cosmic-order cognate is **logos** — Heraclitus'
  Word, "in accordance with" which "all things come to pass" [S26].
- And social dharma-analogues like Ubuntu or Bushidō [A] are lived *ethical systems*, not
  cosmic-order principles — kin to dharma-as-conduct, not to ṛta-as-cosmos.

### The cognates, compared

| Concept | Tradition | Core meaning | Distinctive accent |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ṛta → Dharma** | Vedic / Indic | ṛta = "truth" (Iranist rendering [S8]); dharma = its manifestation in living beings, duty, role [S7][S19] — a two-level reading that is largely modern [S15] | **Two-level** (as commonly presented): cosmic order *plus* a separate applied human-conduct layer keyed to role/stage of life — but see [S15] |
| **Asha** (vs *druj*) | Zoroastrian | "Truth" — opposed to *drug-* "the Lie"; "order" only a partial overlap [S8] | **Dualistic**: active combat between *asha* (truth) and *druj* (the Lie); the same Indo-Iranian word as ṛta (\**ṛtá-*) [S8] |
| **Ma'at** (vs *isfet*) | Ancient Egyptian | Truth, justice, balance, cosmic order, against chaos (*isfet*) [S5][S13] | **Sacralised & political**: a goddess; pharaoh rules "in Ma'at"; post-mortem Weighing of the Heart |
| **Logos** | Greek (Heraclitus → Stoic) | The rational principle governing the cosmos; "all things come to pass in accordance with this Word" [S26]; Stoic God = eternal reason (*logos*) [S27] | **Rationalist/epistemic**: order = intelligibility; cosmos is logical and grasped by reason |
| **Tao / Dao** | Chinese (Daoist / Confucian) | The "Way": "Heaven takes its law from the Tao. The law of the Tao is its being what it is" (DDJ 25) [S25][S14] | **Naturalistic/spontaneous**: alignment via *wu wei* (non-coercion); least personified, least legalistic |
| **Natural law** | Greco-Roman → Christian | Universal moral law knowable by reason; Cicero's *recta ratio*; Aquinas' participation in eternal law [S16][S17] | **Propositional/universalist**: yields explicit, argued, universally-binding norms; basis for rights |
| *(Nomos)* | Greek | Human convention/law (the *physis* contrast) [S23] | *Not a cosmic-order cognate — the "made," relative pole* |

### What is genuinely shared
1. **Fusion of cosmic and moral order.** In each (bar nomos), the same principle governs the
   stars *and* conduct — there is no is/ought gap because the cosmos is already normative.
   Ṛta and *aša* each "primarily refer to the cosmic order and extend to moral laws on one
   hand and ritual practices, particularly sacrifices, on the other"; ṛta "guides the motions
   of the sun, moon, stars" and is "the foundation of both the material and ethical world"
   [S19]. Ma'at, in Assmann's three-level gloss as Bellah reports it, is *order* as the Maat of
   the cosmos, *good government and justice* as the Maat of society, and *truth* as the Maat of
   understanding [S22]. *Asha* nonetheless belongs here on its own source, as the Indo-Iranian
   cognate of *ṛta* whose standard rendering is "truth", opposed to *drug-* "the Lie", with
   "order" only a partial overlap [S8]. Logos is the Word by which "all things come to pass"
   [S26], and for the Stoics God is "eternal reason (*logos*)" immanent in a cosmos "inherently
   governed by a rational force" [S27]; the Dao is the last term in a chain of law — "Man takes
   his law from the Earth; the Earth takes its law from Heaven; Heaven takes its law from the
   Tao" [S25]; natural law makes morality "the highest reason, implanted in nature" [S16][S17].
2. **Virtue = alignment; vice = disorder.** Each pairs the order with its negation — *adharma*,
   *druj* (the Lie), *isfet* (chaos) — and casts the good life as conformity to the order and
   evil as its disruption [S5][S8].
3. **A discovered, not invented, standard.** All claim the order is *found* in reality, giving
   morality an authority beyond mere human stipulation — the very move *nomos* denies [S16][S23].
4. **A convergent behavioural kernel underneath.** Despite radically different cosmologies, the
   conduct they license rhymes: Sapolsky notes the **near-universal Golden Rule** (in positive
   "do unto others" and negative "do not" framings) and reciprocity as the common currency,
   plus Shweder's cross-cultural ethics of autonomy/community/divinity and Haidt's moral
   foundations [S4]. The cosmology varies; the cooperation it underwrites is broadly constant
   (cf. [[1.2-universal-patterns]]).

### What is distinct to each
- **Ṛta/dharma** is presented as building an explicit *two-storey* system (impersonal cosmic
  ṛta + applied human dharma), tying duty to social role and life-stage — the cosmic→social
  mapping as its signature [S7][S19]. ▲ That architecture is contested: Halbfass shows the Vedic
  linkages between the two words to be "casual and rare" (pp. 314–15), and close-connection
  claims to be modern Neo-Hindu postulates [S15]. What survives on bedrock is older and
  stranger: the reconstructed Indo-Iranian formula \**ṛtām dhar-* "to uphold the truth"
  (Y. 43.1 + Rigveda) — dharma's root formulaically paired with ṛta in the common stratum of
  ~2000 BCE [S8].
- **Asha** frames order as *cosmic dualism* — a live war between truth and falsehood — more than
  any other; and is not merely a relative but the *same* Indo-Iranian word as ṛta (\**ṛtá-*;
  Old Persian *ạrta*) [S8]. The standard rendering is **"truth"** — opposed to *drug-* "the
  Lie," rendered *alētheia* by Plutarch — with "order" only a partial overlap [S8]. The Gāthās
  stage the dualism directly: "the two primal Spirits, who reveal themselves in vision as
  Twins, are the Better and the Bad, in thought and word and action" (Y. 30.3), and the hymn
  demands "decision between the two Beliefs, each man for himself" (Y. 30.2) [S20]; Mahmoudi
  reads Y. 30.4 and 30.10 as the two commands — lasting pain for those "deceived by *druj*,"
  salvation for the followers of *aša* [S19]. Schlerath
  reconstructs nine shared Indo-Iranian formulas containing \**ṛtá*, including \**ṛtām dhar-*
  "to uphold the truth" [S8]. Neither daughter word broadened into an ethic: *ṛtá* died out of
  post-Vedic Sanskrit, and *aša* survived only as a Zoroastrian technical term [S8].
- **Ma'at** is uniquely *personified and politically load-bearing*: cosmic order is a goddess;
  the presentation-of-Maat rite was a royal monopoly bound to legitimacy — the king upholds a
  primordial order established at creation which he never makes, across some 205 catalogued
  scenes from Thutmose III to the Napatan king Aspelta [S5] — and individual fate is judged
  against her feather in the Weighing of the Heart [S13]. Maat was also a *lived,
  practice-defined ethic*: two millennia of tomb autobiographies claim "doing Maat" in
  enumerable deeds — veracity, fair dealing, beneficence — cosmic in principle but
  bottom-weighted in the ground-level sources; Lichtheim explicitly rejects Assmann's
  theologised heaven–state–ethics fusion [S13]. ▲ Caveat: popular maat shifted "from primarily
  an ethical concept that was a standard of human behavior, to a funerary concept" in the
  Ramesside period (Teeter), so "lived moral code" claims rest on the autobiographies, not on
  the royal rite [S5][S13]. Two further notes from the corpus sharpen the profile. Ma'at is
  reciprocity made divine: Bellah cites a royal inscription of c. 1700 BCE — "The reward of one
  who does something lies in something being done for him. This is considered by god as
  ma'at" — and observes that what elsewhere stays generalised reciprocity "for the Egyptians
  became substantial in the form of a goddess" [S22]. And the Egyptian order was *comfortable*:
  Jacobsen contrasts an Egyptian cosmos "eminently reliable and comforting," with its
  "reassuring periodicity," against a Mesopotamian one whose rivers "rise unpredictably and
  fitfully," so that "the cosmos was seen as a state" — order as a polity of contending wills,
  not a rhythm [S21][S22]. "Cosmic order" was not one mood even within the Near East.
- **Logos** is the most *intellectualist*: order is reason/intelligibility, and the route to it
  is understanding rather than ritual or duty — Heraclitus' complaint is precisely that "men
  are as unable to understand it when they hear it for the first time as before they have heard
  it at all" [S26]; the Stoics then make it God, "the corporeal mind present everywhere" [S27].
- **Tao** is the most *naturalistic and non-coercive*: order is spontaneous flow, accessed by
  *not* forcing (*wu wei*) — "The Tao in its regular course does nothing (for the sake of doing
  it), and so there is nothing which it does not do" (DDJ 37) [S25]; the Daoist accent on
  ultimate cosmic principle contrasts with the Confucian accent on deliberate ritual (*li*) and
  social hierarchy — in the *Analects* "the Dao is not so much the Way of the Cosmos as it is
  the Way of the ancients, the Way of the former kings," paired with *de* as the ruler's
  charisma [S22]. Nor is the Daoist Dao single: Michael separates the *Constant Dao*, whose
  only activity is *wuwei*, from the *Dao of Heaven*, which exacts retribution for injustice —
  the text's justice is the "natural equality" (*zijun*) of DDJ 32, sweet dew "falling with
  natural equality on all things without a single person ordering it" [S24]. But *wu-wei* itself
  is not a Daoist peculiarity: Slingerland documents it as "the central ideal for all of the
  early mainstream Chinese thinkers" — from the *Book of Odes* through Confucius, Mencius, and
  Xunzi to Laozi and Zhuangzi — a pan-Chinese spiritual ideal of effortless harmony with a
  normative cosmic order — "it is only by harmonizing with the objective, normative order of
  the cosmos that the individual is able to realize it" [S14]. That strengthens the convergence point: "going with the grain"
  is the shared Chinese frame, over which the schools disagreed only about the *path*. The
  built-in "paradox of wu-wei" — how can one *try* not to try? — is a tension the tradition
  metabolised rather than resolved (see [[3.1-tension-resolution]]) [S14].
- **Natural law** is the most *propositional and universalising*: it converts the cosmic order
  into argued, rationally-derivable rules binding on all rational agents — the lineage that
  feeds modern human-rights universalism [S16][S17][S28]. The primary texts anchor this directly.
  Cicero's reconstructed *De Re Publica* 3.33, in Barham's Victorian rendering: "There is a
  true law, a right reason, conformable to nature, universal, unchangeable, eternal…" [S16]
  (▲ quote Barham *as* Barham — his "It needs no other expositor and interpreter than our own
  conscience" supplies "our own conscience" with no warrant in the Latin). Aquinas defines
  natural law as "the participation of the eternal law in the rational creature," known by
  "the light of natural reason" (ST I-II q. 91 a. 2) [S17]. Note that both texts already
  contain the reason-accessible lower storey: Aquinas's first precepts are self-evident to
  natural reason, revelation being required only for the supernatural end (q. 91 aa. 2, 4)
  [S17], and Cicero's "God" is glossed by his own spokesman as "nature, reason, energy, mind,
  divinity, or some other word of clearer signification" [S16]. So any claim that natural law
  is uniquely un-secularisable is wrong: it *was* secularised — Grotius's *etiamsi daremus*
  pivot — at the cost of the law metaphor [S16][S17]. Llano Alonso pushes the point further
  back: Cicero's *natura* "is not some objective reality outside of man, but rather inherent to
  him, forming part of his essential rationality" — unlike Ulpian's *ius naturale* that "nature
  has taught all animals" — so that in obeying natural law man "obeys a law … which ultimately
  he gives to himself as an autonomous legislator" [S28]. On that reading the Roman cognate was
  already half-way to Kant.

### Why this matters for the project
The recurrence of cosmic-order concepts across unconnected civilisations is the
**cosmological-scale evidence** for the universal pattern of [[1.2-universal-patterns]]: where
that note argued from the evolution of cooperation, this one shows the same impulse surfacing
as metaphysics. And dharma's two-level structure is commonly said to explain its special
portability: a *secular* dharma can keep the applied conduct-layer (compassion, situational
ethics, cooperation) while dropping or naturalising the cosmic-order metaphysics [A]. Two
corrections now temper that point. Natural law is *not* the un-secularisable contrast case:
its own texts contain the reason-accessible storey (Aquinas's "light of natural reason";
Cicero's near-interchangeable "nature, reason, energy, mind, divinity"), and it *was*
secularised, via Grotius, at the cost of the law metaphor — and Cicero's *natura* was already
inward, a law man "gives to himself" [S28] — so dharma's portability is a difference of
degree, not kind [S16][S17]. And dharma's "pre-loosened" two-storey design is
itself largely modern carpentry [S15]: the porting is real, but recent.

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The predicted corpus gap has since been closed — by building the shelf.** When this note
  was first written the shared corpus *named* the cognates and gave the ṛta link and the
  Golden-Rule kernel [A][S4], but supplied essentially no substantive content on nomos, logos,
  ma'at, Tao, or natural law; ~7 claims rested on web summaries. The corpus now carries a
  dedicated cosmic-order collection (Teeter, Lichtheim, Frankfort, the Barham Cicero, the
  Dominican Aquinas, Legge's *Texts of Taoism*, Burnet's Heraclitus, the Mills Yasna, Halbfass,
  and the recent Mahmoudi, Michael, Gülenç and Llano Alonso papers), and every placeholder
  citation has been re-anchored to it (2026-08-27). ▲ Most of the older primaries are page
  scans: Halbfass, Teeter, Lichtheim, Frankfort, Legge and Burnet are `text-ocr`, sound as
  pointers, garbled at the word (Halbfass's "casual and rare" survives only as "ca-sual";
  Budge's *Book of the Dead* scan is unusable). Quotations above were checked against the
  chunk and, where the scan is doubtful, against the printed pagination in the register. The
  original bias flag stands in a weaker form: the shelf was curated *for this question*, so it
  is Indic-, Greek- and Egyptian-heavy and still has no Mesopotamian *kittu/mīšaru* primary
  beyond Jacobsen's chapter [S21]. [bias flag]
- **Equivalence is being asserted, partly by modern synthesisers.** Statements like "one can
  recognise Ma'at in all pagan societies; in India it is Rta and Dharma" [S6], or Mahmoudi's
  opening premise that belief in good and evil "is almost universally shared or innately held
  by all humans" with cosmic order as its "earliest" criterion [S19], risk a *perennialist*
  flattening — treating distinct concepts as one trans-cultural truth. The axes above are an
  attempt to resist that: real structural differences (personal vs impersonal; discovered vs
  made; and — with the Halbfass caveat noted above [S15] — one-level vs two-level) must not
  be dissolved into "they're all the same."
- **Translation distortion.** Each term is famously untranslatable; rendering ṛta, ma'at, Tao,
  and logos all as "cosmic order" imports a common frame that none of the traditions used of
  itself, and can over-state similarity (the same caution flagged for the dharman/themis parallel
  in [[1.1-etymology]]). The deeper reason is **Saussurean**: a term's meaning is its *value within
  its own system of contrasts* — "in the language there are only differences without positive
  terms" — so *ṛta* signifies by standing against *anṛta*, *ma'at* against *isfet*, and *Tao*
  against the forced and unnatural, in webs that do not line up; collapsing each into the shared
  English label "cosmic order" discards exactly those system-relative differences [S12].
- **Nomos's mis-listing is itself informative.** That the question grouped *nomos* (convention)
  with cosmic-order concepts shows how easily the "order" family absorbs its own opposite — a
  useful warning for [[3.4-relativism-universalism]], since the *nomos/physis* debate is the
  original relativism-vs-universalism dispute.
- **A cluster-level convergence, now properly sourced.** Ma'at (Teeter) and early dharma
  (Olivelle) both begin as **king-maintained legitimacy orders that are only later ethicised
  downward**: the presentation of Maat was a royal monopoly enacting the king's upholding of a
  primordial order, with popular maat migrating toward funerary ethics [S5]; middle/late Vedic
  dharma was royal and juridical vocabulary tied to Varuṇa and the king, generalised into an
  ethic only via Buddhist and Aśokan usage [S18]. Two unrelated Bronze/Iron Age traditions
  independently locate "cosmic order" first in the vocabulary of kingship and legitimation —
  a stronger, sourced cross-cultural pattern than generic "cosmic order" talk. Relatedly, the
  note's earlier web sources systematically **over-fused "order" glosses** that the primary
  scholarship keeps apart: asha/ṛta as "truth" with order only a partial overlap [S8], the
  ṛta→dharma relay as a modern postulate [S15], maat's cosmic leg thin in the ground-level
  sources [S13].
- **"Tao" is not one thing either.** The Confucian Dao of the *Analects* is the Way of the
  former kings, not of the cosmos [S22]; the *Daodejing* itself runs two Daos, a Constant Dao
  that only does *wuwei* and a Dao of Heaven that punishes [S24]. The table's "Tao" row is the
  Daoist cosmic reading, and the Slingerland point [S14] is what licenses treating it as
  pan-Chinese: the schools shared the *order*, not the *word's* reference.
- **Still second-hand.** Premnath's *Rta and Maat* comparison [S6] and the superseded S7 blog
  gloss remain web-only and are retained for provenance; Aquinas's q. 91 a. 4 (revealed law for
  the supernatural end) is cited from the New Advent text but its chunk was not individually
  verified. Everything else in the note now resolves to a corpus chunk.
