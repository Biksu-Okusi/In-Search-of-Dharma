# 1.3 Boundary & Definition

**Question:** What makes something a *dharma* as opposed to mere custom, law, etiquette,
or ideology? Where are the boundaries of the concept, and what is the minimal set of
features a framework must have to count as dharmic?

## Synthesis

A dharma is distinguished from its neighbours not by *content* but by a *combination* of
four features: (1) **comprehensiveness** — it bundles values, norms, justifying stories,
cultivating practices, and institutions into a single architecture of how to live, rather
than governing one slice of conduct [A]; (2) **internalisation** — it binds through
conviction, identity, and meaning rather than external coercion (unlike law) or mere social
expectation (unlike etiquette) [S5][S8][S10]; (3) **ethical orientation toward the good** —
it concerns the "ought," not pragmatic advantage (the classical Indian split between
*dharma* and *artha*) [S10]; and (4) **transformative telos** — it tells you not just what
to do but why reality is so and who to become, with practices to get you there [A].
By these criteria, **custom/etiquette are too thin** (single-domain, authority-contingent,
no transformative aim), **law is too coercive and external**, and **ideology overlaps most**
but is typically defined by its service to power and lacks dharma's self-cultivation core.
Crucially, the boundary is **graded, not sharp**: there is no single necessary-and-sufficient
mark, the line between "moral/dharmic" and "merely conventional" is itself drawn differently
across cultures [S7], and the corpus's own analysts admit the concept risks becoming "too
broad to be analytically discriminating" [A]. Dharma is best treated as a **family-resemblance
category** with a prototypical core — in the vocabulary of philosophy of language, a
*family-resemblance* concept (and, in its ethical-path sense only, an *essentially contested*
one) whose boundary is a **precising stipulation chosen for a purpose**, not an essence to be
discovered [S13][S14][S16][S17].

## Findings

### The positive core: what a dharma *is*
The corpus's anthropological working definition treats a dharma as an evolving constellation
of five elements — **values** (what is good), **norms/duties** (what one should do),
**stories/justifications** (why life is so; what counts as meaning), **practices/disciplines**
(habits, training, rituals, contemplative methods), and **institutions/reinforcement** (roles,
communities, sanctions) [A]. The decisive point is that a dharma is *enacted*, not merely
believed: "humans rarely just believe ethics; they perform ethics" [A]. It is therefore both
**embodied and institutionalised**, and functions as a "meso" unit linking individual
psychology to macro-level culture, subject to cultural selection [A].

**Proposed minimal feature set (a checklist).** For a framework to count as dharmic it needs,
at minimum: (a) a **normative core** — an account of how one ought to live / what is good;
(b) **comprehensive scope** — it orients a whole life or whole domain-of-life, not a single
act-type; (c) **cultivation practices** — training that forms disposition, so its rules are
"rules of training" rather than mere commandments [S5]; (d) a **legitimating narrative** —
an account of why this is so and what it means [A]; and (e) **internalisation + transmission**
— it is carried by identity and community and reproduced across generations [A]. Frameworks
with only some of these sit at the blurry edge (below).

### Drawing the boundaries (the contrastive analytics)

**vs Custom (folkways & mores).** In Sumner's classic typology, *folkways* are implicit
usages learned by immersion and *mores* are the morally-weighted norms of a group; both arise
spontaneously from repeated practice without deliberate enactment [S9]. Custom is thus a
*component* of a dharma — its norm layer — but lacks the integrating narrative, the
cultivation practices, and the reflective telos. A custom tells you *what is done*; a dharma
tells you *what is good, why, and how to become it*. Dense mores shade into a dharma only once
they acquire justification, practice, and a transformative aim.

**vs Law.** Sumner calls laws "codified mores" — deliberately enacted by the state and
enforced by organised coercion, in contrast to spontaneous, uncodified custom [S9]. The
classical Indian distinction is sharper still: *daṇḍa* (punishment, derived from *dama*, "to
restrain") is the coercive instrument, whereas "dharma is beyond law and the process of
determining what is good is self-oriented rather than coercive" [S10]. This matches Turiel's
empirical finding that moral norms are judged **authority-independent** (wrong regardless of
what any authority decrees) and **general in scope**, whereas legal/conventional norms are
authority-contingent [S8]. Net: law is external, institutional, coercive, and changeable by
fiat; a dharma is internalised, binding by conviction, and not abolished by repealing a statute.

**vs Etiquette.** Etiquette is the paradigm *conventional* domain in Turiel's scheme:
context-dependent, authority-contingent, and harmless when violated (wearing pyjamas to
school) [S8]. Etiquette governs surface coordination — manners — and supplies only
"if-you-want-to-be-polite" reasons; a dharma claims to govern the good life as such. Etiquette
can be a *stylistic expression* of a dharma (monastic decorum, professional bearing) but is
never the whole of one.

**vs Ideology.** This is the hardest boundary, because both are comprehensive meaning-systems
fusing values and narrative — which is exactly why the corpus extends "dharma" to cover "any
lived ethical-cultural system (environmentalism, punk, military ethos, humanism)" [A].
Distinguishing marks: (i) *ideology* is typically theorised as serving or legitimating power,
and potentially distorting reality (the critical/pejorative sense), whereas a dharma centres
self-cultivation and the reduction of suffering; (ii) dharma's **practice-and-discipline layer**
(contemplative training, ritual) is essential to it but inessential to ideology; (iii) dharmas
aim at personal transformation and flourishing, ideologies at social-political mobilisation.
But the boundary is *functional, not absolute*: a dharma can operate ideologically (legitimating
caste, hierarchy, or conquest) — see [[7.3-dharma-and-power]].

**vs Artha (the tradition's own boundary).** The four *puruṣārthas* separate **dharma**
(righteous conduct, the ethical ends) from **artha** (material prosperity and statecraft, the
pragmatic means), **kāma** (desire/pleasure), and **mokṣa** (liberation) [S10]. Kautilya's
*Arthaśāstra* deliberately brackets morality — "it does not address the moral dimensions of
sovereign power... those are subjects for separate texts concerned with dharma" — while Manu's
*Dharmaśāstra* supplies the ethical ends [S10]. This is the tradition's own **is/ought (means/ends)
boundary**, and a clean template: dharma is precisely the part of life that is *not* reducible to
efficacy, prosperity, pleasure, or coercion.

### Where the boundary blurs (and why that is expected)
No single feature is necessary and sufficient; dharma is a **family-resemblance** concept.
Prototypical cases (Buddhism, Stoicism, Ubuntu) exhibit all five features; marginal cases
(a professional code, a fandom, a political movement) exhibit some. The corpus's habit of
calling punk rock, environmentalism, and "democratic dharmas" dharmas [A] is analytically
useful for comparison but courts the **over-extension failure** its own analysts flag: the
more things are dharmas, the less the term discriminates [A]. Two principled blur-sources:

1. *Graded membership.* The edge cases are real, not errors — they are frameworks with a
   subset of the features, and where one draws the cut-off is a stipulation, not a discovery.
2. *The moral/convention line is itself cultural.* The Turiel distinction was built largely on
   Western samples and is contested cross-culturally: reported via [S7], Shweder, Mahapatra &
   Miller found Indians moralise much that WEIRD subjects treat as mere convention, and
   Fessler et al. (2015) and Barrett et al. (2016) document wide cross-cultural variation in
   moral parochialism and the role of intentions [S7]. ▲ **All three reach this note only
   through the reference list of the [S7] blog post; none of the primary papers has been
   consulted.** So the very boundary between "dharma" and "mere custom" shifts by
   culture — and, tellingly, the tradition that coined *dharma* casts the moral net wider than
   the Western moral/conventional distinction assumes. It also strains a *second* Turiel
   criterion. The "vs Law" boundary above leans on Turiel's mark that moral norms are **general
   in scope** [S8]; yet the Gita makes paradigmatic dharma **agent-relative** — one's own duty,
   indexed to one's nature and station, is better than another's, "however imperfect" its
   performance (the argument at 3.35 and 18.47) [S11]. *Svadharma* is thus dharmic *and*
   agent-indexed at once, which the "general in scope" test would misclassify. ▲ Cited for what
   the text argues, by chapter.verse only; the anonymous corpus translation (register status
   *unverified*) carries no wording-level weight.

### Naming the apparatus: a family-resemblance concept (essentially contested only in its path sense)
The blur just described is not a local quirk of *dharma*; it is what twentieth-century
philosophy of language predicts for any rich human concept, and naming the apparatus sharpens
the analysis. Wittgenstein showed that *game* has no feature common to all its instances — only
"a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing" — so such concepts cohere
by **family resemblance**, not by a shared essence [S13]. Cognitive psychology made this precise
as the **prototype** model — and measurably so: Rosch & Mervis showed that a member's
family-resemblance score (its attributes weighted by how widely other members share them)
predicts rated prototypicality at ρ = .84–.94 across six superordinate object categories, so
the "textbook case vs blurry rim" structure this note relies on is quantifiable, not a metaphor
[S14]. (▲ Honesty about reach: *bird* was not among the categories tested — the robin/penguin
gradings come from Rosch's companion 1975 paper; the evidence is concrete-object categories
normed on ~1,076 US undergraduates, and extending the structure to a normative category like
*dharma* is this project's own wager [S14].) And Waismann's **open texture** shows that even a
well-made definition cannot anticipate every future case, so an empirical concept is never fully
closed [S15]. ▲ Two caveats on the borrowing: Waismann confines open texture to **empirical
description** — his furthest reach is dispositional ascriptions like "intelligent" (p. 124); the
extension to legal rules is Hart's, and the extension to *dharma* is this project's own step. And
he insists open texture is *not* vagueness: vagueness "can be remedied by giving more accurate
rules, open texture cannot" (p. 123) — so most of this note's *present* borderline cases (is a
professional code a dharma?) are remediable vagueness, and the strict open-texture claim is only
the forward-looking one: no definition of dharma can forestall the forms a dharma might yet take
[S15]. The corpus reaches the same place in
its own idiom, contrasting "sharp and homogenous" definitions (the triangle) with the "borderless
and diffuse" definitions typical of human concepts like *freedom* or *intelligence*, whose clarity
varies with field, purpose, culture, and time [A].

For an *ethical* category, Gallie's **essentially contested concepts** supplies a further twist:
for concepts such as *art*, *democracy*, *social justice*, and *adherence to a religion* — his
four live examples — disagreement about the correct application is not a defect awaiting a better
definition; it is *internal to the concept itself* [S16]. But the full paper sets a test the
label must pass, and the note must apply it. Gallie's conditions I–V establish only that a
concept *is* contested; he adds (VI) derivation from "an original exemplar whose authority is
acknowledged by all the contestant users" and (VII) the plausibility that continued competition
sustains or develops the exemplar's achievement — and VI–VII are precisely what distinguish
essential contestedness from a term that is merely **"radically confused"**, cloaking several
distinct concepts (p. 180) [S16]. Dharma in the plural, taxonomic sense this note uses likely
*fails* VI: Vedic ritualists, Abhidharma scholastics, Jain physicists, and secular Batchelorians
do not acknowledge one exemplar, and are largely not contesting the same achievement at all.
Gallie's concepts are, moreover, *appraisive* — rivalries over a single prize — where DD's usage
is a taxonomy of instances. The essentially-contested claim is therefore **restricted here to
dharma's ethical-path sense**, where an exemplar (the founder-teacher's taught path) arguably
does operate and rivals do contest one achievement; the wider plurality rests on **family
resemblance**, the structurally correct tool for graded membership in a category [S13][S14][S16].
What survives unrestricted is Gallie's practical payoff: recognising contestedness makes rival
uses "of permanent potential critical value to one's own use" and should raise the quality of
argument (p. 193) [S16]. The corpus's standing dare — "I dare you: define dharma!" [A] — is best
read in that spirit: within the ethical-path sense the endless dispute is constitutive, not a
failure; across the wider taxonomy, the blur flagged above [A] is graded family-resemblance
membership, not contest over one prize [S14].

This reframes the task. A definition is not one true statement of an essence but a **tool**, and
there are several kinds for different jobs — *lexical* (reporting use), *stipulative* (assigning a
meaning), *precising* (sharpening a vague term for a purpose), and *ostensive* (pointing) [S17].
"What *is* a dharma?" therefore has no context-free answer; the productive question is a
*precising* one — for *this* comparative purpose, where shall we draw the line, and what do we
lose by drawing it there? The feature-checklist above is offered in exactly that spirit: a
stipulation fit for cross-cultural comparison, not a discovery of dharma's hidden essence — which
is why its edges are meant to be graded and its cut-off admittedly chosen rather than found.

### The power objection: Asad's genealogy, and what actually answers it

The strongest standing objection to this note's whole apparatus is not semantic but
genealogical, and it must be met by name. Talal Asad, against Geertz's universal definition of
religion, argued "that there cannot be a universal definition of religion, not only because its
constituent elements and relationships are historically specific, but because that definition is
itself the historical product of discursive processes" [S18]. The point is not that "religion"
is fuzzy; it is that the very *project* of lifting a transhistorical, functional category out of
the world's ways of living is an act with a specific Christian-then-secular European history,
entangled with administration and empire — an ideological act wearing the costume of an analytic
one [S18]. Every word of this applies to "dharma" as this project universalises it: a Western,
secular, English-language corpus stabilising a Sanskrit word into a cross-cultural functional
category is exactly the move Asad anatomised, and the project's own note on the "world
religions" containers ([[7.5-orientalism]], and Part 6 of the essays) makes the same charge
against the nineteenth century without, until now, turning it on the project's own container [A].

An earlier version of this note answered Asad with *disclosure*: concede the genealogy, offer
the definition as an authored, purpose-relative stipulation rather than a discovery [S17], and
let the removal of concealment do the work. That answer does not survive contact with the
literature that has fought this exact battle over "religion." Disclosure is not among the
defences that work: Asad's own closing caution is that the definitional endeavour belongs to a
particular history of knowledge and power *whether or not* its author owns up (p. 54) [S18], and
Schilbrack's survey adds that a polythetic, family-resemblance framing buys no neutrality either
— "the polythetic identification of a prototypical religion is equally ethnocentric" — while a
functionally defined universality "is not a discovery but a product of one's definition" [S19].
Disclosure is accordingly demoted to what it is: good methodological practice, the precondition
of assessing a stipulation by its purpose, not an answer to the genealogy.

The defences that do work are four, and the note now rests on them. (1) **Realism about social
structures, plus testability** (Schilbrack): a form of life can instantiate a social kind
without possessing the concept for it — a person "can speak a Uralic language" without the
concept *Uralic* — and an invented map remains answerable to a non-conceptual territory; the
invention argument shows only that the *modern* concept is modern, unless the critic can name
the modern feature smuggled into the definition [S19]. (2) **Concede the genealogy, keep the
category** (McKinnon): there is no natural boundary to such a concept, but Wittgenstein's
licence — "You can draw one; for none has so far been drawn" (PI §68) — permits drawing one for
a purpose, judged by usefulness; what saves the result from arbitrary stipulation is the term's
real circulation in ordinary language [S20]. ▲ That condition is met by "religion" (censuses,
law, UDHR Article 18) and **not, at present, by this project's expanded "dharma"**: no ordinary
English speaker, asked their dharma, answers "environmentalism" or "punk" [S20]. The strong
McKinnon-style claim is therefore restricted to dharma's home traditions, where the word is a
lived first-order term; for the wider category the honest position is that the usage may be
*entering* circulation — Western convert and punk-Buddhist milieux already self-describe with it
(Dharma Punx, Rebel Dharma) [S23] — early, thin evidence of spread, not yet a common language
game. There is, however, a further reply available on the project's own stated stance [A]:
the book uses "dharma" as an avowed **placeholder** — a term of art for a concept-family whose
*local* names (ubuntu, ma'at, Tao, "our way") circulate vigorously in their own communities.
McKinnon's condition demands that the *concept* be a lived, ordinary-language reality somewhere,
not that one scholarly cover-label be in universal circulation; "religion" itself was once
exactly such a cover-label, extended from Latin. What the project may not do is quietly slide
from placeholder to lived term; the essay now marks the placeholder status explicitly. (3) **The Riesebrodt fork**: a *substantive* definition (religion as practices premised on
generally invisible superhuman powers) earns a falsifiable, empirically open universality — but
only by ejecting the secular cases, since Riesebrodt expressly severs religion from philosophy
and ethics [S21]. A functional definition keeps the secular cases and forfeits discovery-status.
This note's four-feature test is functional, so it cannot have both: its implication that every
society grows a dharma is a **modelling claim, not an empirical finding**, and must be stated as
such [S21][S19]. (4) **The Taves escape** — the recast this note adopts: relocate the
universality claim a level down. What is plausibly universal is not "a dharma" but the *building
blocks* — singling things out as special, and deeming practices efficacious toward goals deemed
special (her "special path," a schema drawn partly from Buddhist *mārga* theory and the nearest
published analogue to dharma-as-path) — while "a dharma" becomes a historically assembled
**composite formation**, comparable across cultures without pretending to be a natural kind
[S22]. What cannot be claimed after Asad is innocence; what can be claimed is a map on the
record, kept testable against the territory [S18][S19]. (The companion essay
`defining-definition.md` develops the disclosure move and should now be read with this demotion
in mind.) Asad's parallel argument that "the secular" is itself a positive, constructed
formation rather than religion's residue belongs to the Part 7 frame and is carried in
[[6.5-secularization-nones]].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The corpus admits this exact gap.** Its analytical essay's "Limitations" section calls for
  "clearer criteria distinguishing 'dharma' from adjacent constructs (ideology, worldview,
  moral foundation, habitus)" and warns the framework can become "too broad to be analytically
  discriminating" [A]. The corpus supplies a strong *positive* definition but no *contrastive*
  criteria — the entire boundary analysis here was imported via the external pass (Turiel,
  Sumner, the puruṣārthas). [bias flag]
- **Secular over-extension.** Following the project's non-privileging stance, the corpus
  stretches "dharma" to any "lived ethical-cultural system" [A]. This maximises comparative
  reach but sacrifices precision; this note pushes back by proposing an explicit feature
  checklist and a family-resemblance model.
- **The four-feature test's edges are load-bearing, not decorative.** Schilbrack's warning
  about dimension-lists applies squarely to this note's own apparatus: creed, cult, and code are
  "not even distinctively religious" until a delimiting criterion is added, since such dimensions
  "will be found in any collective movement or cultural tradition" [S19]. The checklist above
  (comprehensiveness, internalisation, orientation to the good, transformative telos) is exactly
  such a list; until the note says which feature or threshold does the delimiting — and whether
  the test is bounded, open, or anchored polythetic [S19] — it risks sorting nothing. Compounding
  this is the McKinnon circulation gap: the expanded "dharma" does not circulate in ordinary
  language the way "religion" does [S20], so outside its home traditions the category currently
  has only a scholar's-stipulation defence, not a living common term's.
- **A WEIRD lens smuggled in by the very tools that fix the gap.** Turiel's moral/conventional
  distinction — the cleanest instrument for "dharma vs etiquette" — rests on contested,
  largely Western data [S7][S8]. Using it to draw the dharma/convention boundary partly imports
  a Western framing (developed further in [[5.3-moral-psychology-development]]).
- **"Situational ethics" resists checklists.** Corpus voices insist dharma's essence is
  *situational appropriateness* guided by compassion, not rule-following [A]; this sits in
  tension with any feature list that foregrounds "norms." Resolved by treating a dharma's norms
  as *defeasible guides*, not algorithms.
- **Cited via summaries.** Turiel (1983), Sumner's *Folkways* (1906), the Arthaśāstra/
  Dharmaśāstra, and the puruṣārthas are cited via the SEP and encyclopedic/policy summaries,
  not the primary texts. **Shweder, Mahapatra & Miller (1987/1990), Fessler et al. (2015) and
  Barrett et al. (2016) belong in this list too**: they are named with dates in the findings
  above, but reach this note only through the reference list of the [S7] prosocial.world blog
  post — no primary paper consulted, no figures or wording drawn from them. Philosophical
  treatments that would sharpen "vs etiquette" (Foot 1972)
  and "vs law" (Hart 1961) are noted but not yet consulted. The Gita is the one primary dharmic
  text now available in the corpus, but only for argument-structure claims: its translation is
  anonymous and unreliable (register status *unverified*), so it can stress-test the checklist
  (as with svadharma's agent-relativity above [S11]) without supplying any word-level evidence.
- **The agent-relativity point cuts both ways.** That *svadharma* is agent-indexed [S11] is a
  genuine strain on the "general in scope" criterion, but it is drawn from *one* tradition's
  self-theorisation and should be tested against the checklist, not treated as overturning it: the
  checklist is an avowed precising stipulation [S17], and a prototype tradition exhibiting an
  atypical feature is exactly the graded-membership case the family-resemblance model predicts, not
  a refutation of it [A].
- **Asad was absent until July 2026 — a retrieval-loop instance.** The strongest published
  opponent of this note's central move appeared nowhere in the note, the essays, or the corpus:
  the shared library's curated lean carries no genealogical critique of the "religion"/"dharma"
  category, and similarity retrieval never surfaced what the shelf does not hold (the
  mechanism documented in [[8.4-encoding-dharma-machines]]). The engagement above was added
  after the project's critical review named the gap; ch. 1 has since been read in full and
  [S18] is now cited first-hand, with the reply rebuilt on Schilbrack, McKinnon, Riesebrodt,
  and Taves [S19][S20][S21][S22] rather than on disclosure alone.

VII), the extension to dharma this project's own. <https://www.jstor.org/stable/4106531> [src:waismann-1945-verifiability]
- [S16] W. B. Gallie, "Essentially Contested Concepts," *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society* 56 (1956), 167–198 — concepts "the proper use of which inevitably involves endless disputes about their proper uses on the part of their users" (p. 169); the live examples are art, democracy, social justice, and adherence to a religion (p. 180). Conditions I–V establish that a concept is contested; conditions VI (derivation from "an original exemplar whose authority is acknowledged by all the contestant users") and VII (competition plausibly sustains or develops the exemplar's achievement) are what distinguish essential contestedness from a "radically confused" term (p. 180); recognising contestedness should treat rival uses as "of permanent potential critical value to one's own use" and raise the quality of argument (p. 193). <https://academic.oup.com/aristotelian/article-abstract/56/1/167/1793543> [src:gallie-1956-essentially-contested-concepts]
- [S17] Anil Gupta & Stephen Mackereth, "Definitions," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* — the kinds of definition (lexical/reportive, stipulative, ostensive, precising) and definition as a purpose-relative tool rather than a statement of essence. <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/definitions/>
- [S18] Talal Asad, *Genealogies of Religion: Discipline and Reasons of Power in Christianity and Islam* (Johns Hopkins UP, 1993), ch. 1 ("The Construction of Religion as an Anthropological Category"), pp. 27–54 — "there cannot be a universal definition of religion, not only because its constituent elements and relationships are historically specific, but because that definition is itself the historical product of discursive processes" (p. 29, verified verbatim); the critique of Geertz's universal definition; definition-making as an act within a history of knowledge and power; and the closing caution that the aim is "to problematize the idea of an anthropological definition of religion by assigning that endeavor to a particular history of knowledge and power" (p. 54) — a charge that holds whether or not the definer owns up. *Read in full.* <https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/1644/genealogies-religion> [src:asad-1993-genealogies-of-religion]
- [S19] Kevin Schilbrack, "The Concept of Religion," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (first published 28 Mar 2022) — the defences that work: realism about social structures (a person "can speak a Uralic language… even if they lack these concepts," §3) plus the map's testability against a non-conceptual territory; the sleight-of-hand diagnosis of invention arguments — "*modern* religion did not exist outside modernity" (§3). Against this note's comfort: a polythetic framing is "equally ethnocentric" (§2.2); functionally defined universality "is not a discovery but a product of one's definition" (§1); and dimension-lists like creed/cult/code are "not even distinctively religious" without a delimiting criterion (§2.1). <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/concept-religion/> [src:schilbrack-2022-concept-of-religion-sep]
- [S20] Andrew M. McKinnon, "Sociological Definitions, Language Games and the 'Essence' of Religion," *Method & Theory in the Study of Religion* 14:1 (2002), 61–83 — concede the genealogy, keep the category: no natural boundary exists, but one may be drawn for a purpose (PI §68) and judged by usefulness rather than truth; what saves "religion" from being an arbitrary stipulation is its competent circulation in ordinary language (censuses, law, UDHR Article 18, 300-plus translations). ▲ That condition is met by "religion" but not by this project's expanded "dharma" — hence the restriction argued in the body. <https://doi.org/10.1163/157006802760198776> [src:mckinnon-2002-sociological-definitions]
- [S21] Martin Riesebrodt, *The Promise of Salvation: A Theory of Religion*, trans. S. Rendall (Chicago UP, 2010) — the post-Asad substantive strategy: religion as a complex of practices premised on generally invisible superhuman powers (pp. 74–75), leaving universality empirically open (pp. 169–70) and the salvation-promise thesis "falsifiable" (p. 89); the price is exclusion of the secular — religion expressly severed from philosophy and ethics (p. 78), morality needing no religion (pp. 183–84). The fork this note faces: substantive falsifiability or secular inclusion, not both. [src:riesebrodt-2010-promise-of-salvation]
- [S22] Ann Taves, *Religious Experience Reconsidered: A Building-Block Approach to the Study of Religion and Other Special Things* (Princeton UP, 2009) — relocate universality below the aggregate: simple ascriptions of specialness are "the basic building blocks" from which religions and spiritualities are assembled (p. 46); the "special path" — practices deemed efficacious toward goals deemed special, a schema drawn partly from Buddhist *mārga* theory (p. 47) — is the nearest published analogue to dharma-as-path; the universality wager attaches to the singularising tendency, "a deeply rooted human characteristic" (p. 34), offered as a testable hypothesis (p. 163), not to the composite category.
