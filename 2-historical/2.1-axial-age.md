# 2.1 Axial Age Catalyst

**Question:** What specific socio-economic and psychological pressures during the Axial
Age (800–200 BCE) catalyzed the simultaneous emergence of dharmic thinking across
disconnected civilizations?

## Synthesis

No single cause carries the Axial Age; the best account is a *convergence* of socio-economic
pressures (rising affluence, the invention of coinage and impersonal markets, urbanisation,
endemic iron-age warfare, and spreading literacy) with psychological ones (the rise of
second-order/reflexive thought, a newly perceived chasm between a transcendent and a mundane
order, a deepened sense of the individual self, and an orientation toward mortality and
salvation) [A][S2][S3][S4][S8]. The signature of the period is a *pivot in the content of
religion itself*: from **propitiation** (perform the ritual and sacrifice to secure material
prosperity) to **ethicisation** (live rightly, master desire, and thereby align with — or
transcend — the order of things) [S2][S10]. That pivot is exactly the move from cosmic order to
ethical conduct traced in [[1.4-cross-cultural-cognates]] — and it is what makes this the
catalyst question for *dharmic* thinking specifically. The apparent "simultaneity" across
disconnected zones is best read not as contact but as **parallel structural conditions
producing parallel solutions** — the universal-pattern logic of [[1.2-universal-patterns]]
localised to one historical window [A]. The concept is, however, genuinely contested [S5], and
the largest systematic test to date (Seshat, ten regions, two millennia) finds that the
"axial" traits appear hundreds or thousands of years earlier, and in regions the thesis
excludes, so that there was no circumscribed *age* of axiality — only a family of
transformations arriving "in fits and starts" [S11]. The question therefore survives as a
question about *pressures*, not about a *moment* [A].

## Findings

### The phenomenon to be explained
Karl Jaspers coined *Achsenzeit* ("Axial Period" in the 1953 English translation) for the
"spiritual process that occurred between 800 and 200 B.C.": "the most deepcut dividing line
in history. Man, as we know him today, came into being" [S8]. The near-simultaneous
appearance, in mutually disconnected civilisations, of foundational ethical-philosophical
traditions — Confucius and Laozi in China, the Buddha (and the śramaṇa movements) in India,
Zoroaster in Persia, the Hebrew prophets in Israel, the pre-Socratics in Greece — is the datum
[S8][S5]. Jaspers's own characterisation of the change is psychological before it is
doctrinal: man "experiences absoluteness in the depths of selfhood and in the lucidity of
transcendence. All this took place in reflection. Consciousness became once more conscious of
itself, thinking became its own object"; and "the step into universality was taken" [S8]. The
crucial change is in religion's *content*: before the Axial Age, religion centred on "rituals,
sacrifices, and respecting taboos... believed to ensure prosperity"; the new doctrines instead
valued "personal transcendence," a moral existence, control of material desire "through
moderation, asceticism, and compassion," and the salvation of a soul that survives death [S2].
Halton's recovery of John Stuart-Glennie (who described a "Moral Revolution" of the same
centuries in 1873, seventy-five years before Jaspers) characterises the shift as "conscience
over custom" and "inner power over the status quo of external power" [S16].

### Socio-economic pressures
- **Affluence / energy capture.** In the strongest empirical account, Baumard et al. (2015,
  *Current Biology*) argue that rising **affluence** — measured by energy capture — was the
  key driver: affluence predictably shifts human motivation from "fast" (immediate, materialist)
  toward "slow" strategies (self-control, long-term, ascetic), the very ethic the new religions
  prized. Their models found affluence markers, *not* political-success markers, predicted where
  axial religions emerged; and historically these movements appeared not in the largest empires
  (Assyria, Egypt, Persia) but in **smaller prosperous polities** — Greek city-states, the Indian
  Mahajanapada, the developed Chinese states [S2]. In their own addendum the authors frame this
  as a rival *definition* of the Axial Age — "a change in reward orientation, from a short-term
  materialistic orientation to a long-term spiritual one" rather than the standard "change of
  cognitive style" — and they restrict the model's scope: it "applies to the initial emergence
  of Axial Age doctrines as an elite interest, not to their subsequent diffusion among large
  populations," since "most people in Iron Age societies still lived only a little way above
  bare subsistence level" [S10].
- **Coinage and impersonal markets.** Graeber (*Debt*) notes that "in Lydia, India, and China,
  local rulers decided that whatever longstanding credit systems had existed in their kingdoms
  were no longer adequate, and they began to issue tiny pieces of precious metals" — coinage,
  which "made possible the creation of markets in the now more familiar, impersonal sense of the
  term," and was "far more appropriate for an age of generalised warfare, for the obvious reason
  that they could be stolen" [S9]. He ties the era together as a **"military-coinage-slavery
  complex"** — professional armies paid in coin, markets fostered to fund them, an explosion of
  slavery, and chronic violence — and reads the Axial Age as having "saw the emergence of
  complementary ideals of commodity markets and universal world religions": once a social space
  is given over to selfish material acquisition, another arises to preach that material things,
  and even the self, are ultimately unimportant [S9][S4]. Graeber deliberately stretches the
  period to "800 BC to 600 AD" so that it covers the birth of all today's world religions, and
  observes that Jaspers's core period "corresponds almost exactly to the period in which
  coinage was invented" [S9].
- **Warfare, at scale.** The Seshat team's post-retraction analysis (Turchin, Whitehouse et al.
  2022) finds that "intergroup warfare, supported by resource availability, played a major role
  in the evolution of both social complexity and moralizing religions," so that the correlation
  between the two "seems to result from shared evolutionary drivers, rather than from direct
  causal relationships" [S12]. Warfare is thus the one socio-economic pressure with a
  quantitative, cross-regional result behind it — and it is the same pressure Graeber's complex
  puts at the centre [A].
- **Urbanisation and large states.** Jaspers himself located the breakthrough partly in the new
  "depths of selfhood" [S8] facilitated by urbanisation and large-scale empires [S3] — and in the
  dissolution of kin-based moral orders into anonymous societies (treated in depth in
  [[2.2-urban-complexity]]).
- **Literacy and a leisured scholar/priest class.** Baumard's open questions point here (did a
  scholar class with time and resources, and rising literacy among believers, enable more abstract
  religions?) [S2]. Bellah, following Merlin Donald, ties "theoretic culture" to external memory:
  "graphic invention, external memory, and theory construction," with early writing "a
  significant step beyond painting in the amount of cognitive information that could be stored"
  though "precursors to, rather than full realization" of theoretic culture; "writing was a
  precondition for theoretic culture," yet "oral culture has survived as an indispensable
  supplement to literacy" [S7].

### Psychological pressures
- **Second-order thinking ("theoretic culture").** Bellah identifies the rise of *reflexivity* —
  thinking about thinking, the capacity for theoretic (not merely mythic or practical) belief — as
  a defining Axial change: his book ends "with the axial age, the emergence of theoretic culture
  and the reorganization of the relation between mimetic, mythic, and theoretic elements that
  that requires," the theoretic becoming "partially, never totally — disembedded from the mimetic
  and mythic" [S7]; this is the metacognitive shift examined in [[3.2-metacognitive-function]].
  Jaspers had already put it in almost the same words: "thinking became its own object" [S8].
- **The transcendental/mundane disjunction.** Eisenstadt's influential reformulation locates the
  breakthrough in the "emergence, conceptualization, and institutionalization of a basic tension
  between the transcendental and mundane orders" — a perceived chasm bridged by reconstructing
  human conduct on "higher moral precepts," which also created a "new type of intellectual elite"
  able to criticise worldly power "from outside" [S6].
- **A new interiority and individual responsibility.** The period foregrounds personal moral
  responsibility and an inward spiritual journey, not just communal ritual conformity [A];
  Jaspers describes "spiritual conflicts... attempts to convince others through the communication
  of thoughts, reasons and experiences," with "discussion, the formation of parties and the
  division of the spiritual realm into opposites" [S8].
- **Mortality and salvation.** The new orientation toward a soul surviving death, with salvation
  contingent on moral self-control, reframes ethics as existentially urgent rather than merely
  socially useful [S2].
- **Reward orientation, not cognition — and its failure at the individual level.** Baumard's
  alternative is that the psychological change was motivational (life-history "slowing"), not
  cognitive [S10]. The direct cross-cultural test of that mechanism failed: Purzycki et al.
  (2018), with 592 individuals in eight societies, found "no reliable evidence of a relationship
  between number of children, material security, or formal education and the individual-level
  religious beliefs and behaviors," and "conclude... that life-history theory is an inadequate
  interpretation for the emergence of factors typifying the moralistic traditions" [S13]. The
  affluence correlation at the level of polities therefore stands without the individual-level
  mechanism that was proposed to explain it [A].

### How these converge into *dharmic* thinking
The common output across all five zones is an **internalised, universalisable ethic** that
transcends local ritual and tribal boundary — the Buddha's dharma, the Confucian ethical Way,
the Greek philosophical *logos*, prophetic justice. Structurally this is the same pivot in each
case: from order-as-ritual-propitiation to order-as-ethical-conduct, i.e. from *ṛta* toward
*dharma* (cf. [[1.4-cross-cultural-cognates]]). The simultaneity follows from the universal
pattern of [[1.2-universal-patterns]]: when broadly similar structural conditions (iron-age
affluence + coinage + urban anonymity + literacy + organised violence) recur independently,
broadly similar dharmic solutions recur with them — no diffusion required [A]. Seshat's finding
that the same traits recur in Egypt, Anatolia, Italy, Japan and Cambodia at other times [S11]
is, on this reading, not a refutation of the convergence account but a confirmation of it
stripped of Jaspers's calendar: the pressures, wherever and whenever they assemble, produce the
pivot [A]. Mullins et al. grant as much — "the allegedly axial ideologies explored here do
exhibit more pronounced claims of equality than most ideologies that preceded them,
particularly by limiting the authority and privilege of rulers" [S11]; but the *dharmic*
content is not an artefact of the "age" [A].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The Axial Age concept is heavily contested — and the corpus now holds the critics.** Boy &
  Torpey treat it as a 20th-century construct: Jaspers's scheme, though meant as "provincializing
  Europe," "appears in certain respects to reproduce earlier Eurocentric models of universal
  history, though now broadened into a Eurasiancentric paradigm," with a **diffusionist** picture
  of "islands of light amidst the broad mass of the rest of humanity" and the Americas, Africa
  and Japan as non-axial [S5]. Aleida Assmann charges Jaspers with repeating "the exclusive,
  ethnocentric perspective he had sought to overcome" and insists Egypt and Mesopotamia belong;
  Jan Assmann finds "a too radical discontinuity between the pre- and post-axial worlds" [S5].
  The charge of **teleology/chronocentrism** — that a singular "spiritual breakthrough" is rooted
  in a Christian salvation-history schema — is carried in the external reading of the same
  authors [S5 external]; it was not located in the corpus text.
- **The strongest counterweight is empirical.** Mullins et al. (2018), using Seshat across the
  five axial regions and five "non-axial" ones (Egypt, Turkey, Italy, Cambodia, Japan) over two
  millennia, conclude that the concept "does not hold up to systematic empirical scrutiny; the
  historical evidence simply fails to corroborate the idea of an Axial Age that is clearly
  circumscribed geographically or temporally": the key proxies are present in the core regions at
  the expected time, but also "hundreds and in some cases thousands of years prior," and in the
  excluded regions "both before and after." Their table of open questions begins: "Why did these
  developments occur when and where they did, given that there was no specific 'age' nor
  synchrony to axial transformations?" [S11]. The note's Synthesis has been reframed accordingly:
  pressures, not a moment.
- **Self-undermining exclusion.** Egypt and Mesopotamia — whose *ma'at* and *kittu* the corpus
  itself named as cosmic-order cognates in [[1.4-cross-cultural-cognates]] — are typically
  *excluded* from the Axial club; Seshat finds Egypt "display[s] very clearly axial-type
  transformations well before the lifetime of the axial sages" [S11]. The project's own prior
  note thus stands with the Assmanns and with Seshat against the Axial Age's tidiness.
- **The competing causes do not agree, and are hard to adjudicate:** affluence (Baumard) vs the
  transcendental breakthrough (Eisenstadt) vs coinage/markets (Graeber) vs reflexivity (Bellah)
  vs warfare-and-scale (Turchin). Notably, Baumard's finding that *political success did not
  predict* axial religions cuts against pure empire accounts, while Turchin et al. 2022 put
  warfare at the centre of both complexity and moralising religion [S12]; and Baumard's own
  proposed individual-level mechanism failed its direct test [S13]. Mullins et al. also record
  Baumard's concession that his affluence and complexity proxies "remain very crude" [S11].
  ▲ An earlier draft buttressed this with the moralizing-gods result (complex societies *precede*
  moralizing gods). That claim has been **removed**: its source, Whitehouse et al. (2019, *Nature*),
  was **retracted on 7 July 2021** — the authors "accept that we should have labelled moralizing
  gods as 'absent' or 'inferred absent' rather than 'unknown'" [S15] — after Beheim et al. showed
  that the treatment of missing data determined the conclusion [S14]. [[2.2-urban-complexity]]
  states the project rule — the 2019 Letter is not to be cited in either direction — and it
  applies here. Where an independent line is wanted, use the phylogenetic evidence (Peoples,
  Duda & Marlowe 2016, carried in [[1.2-universal-patterns]] as its S16), or the 2022 re-analysis
  [S12], which does not depend on the retracted coding.
- **Corpus bias.** At first writing the shared corpus offered only a generic, AI-authored Jaspers
  summary that framed the Axial Age teleologically as "a crucial step in the evolution of human
  thought" [A] — itself an instance of the progressivist reading Assmann criticises — and all
  substantive causal theory came from the prosocial corpus (Baumard) [S2] and external sources.
  Since the 2026-08 corpus expansion (`DD/sources/1-axial-age/`, `2-moralizing-gods/`,
  `9-economics/`) the primaries are in-corpus: Jaspers 1953 and Halton 2014 (both `text-ocr`),
  Bellah 2011, Graeber's *Debt* (`text-ocr`), Boy & Torpey, Baumard's addendum, Mullins et al.,
  Turchin et al. 2022, Purzycki et al. 2018, Beheim et al., and the *Nature* retraction note.
  The corpus's lean is now visible in a different place: the Seshat/cliodynamics and
  cultural-evolution literature is thickly represented, the *religious-studies* and *philological*
  defences of the Axial thesis (Eisenstadt, Arnason, the Bellah & Joas volume) are still only
  cited via summaries [S6]. The supernatural-punishment strand (Johnson) is surfaced but not
  developed here.
- **Still second-hand.** Eisenstadt [S6], Armstrong [S3], and the Assmanns' salvation-history
  charge [S5] remain cited via secondary sources. Graeber's line that world religions preach that
  "material things... [and] the self" are unimportant is paraphrase carried from the 2009 essay
  [S4]; the closest in-corpus wording is his "complementary ideals of commodity markets and
  universal world religions" [S9].
