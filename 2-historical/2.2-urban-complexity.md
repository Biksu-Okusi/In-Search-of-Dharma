# 2.2 Urban Complexity

**Question:** How did the transition from kinship-based tribes to anonymous urban
societies necessitate explicit dharmic codes? What problems, individual and societal,
did these frameworks seek to solve?

## Synthesis

At small scale, cooperation runs on **kinship plus face-to-face reciprocity**; below
roughly Dunbar's ~150, everyone can monitor everyone and reputation is direct [S3][S6].
Scaling up — agriculture, settlement, the city — forces "one-off transactions with
anonymous strangers," and the small-group toolkit breaks down: I might help you and never
see you again, so the free-rider can win [S1][S3]. Cooperation among strangers therefore
needs a **scale-free substitute**: shared identity markers, indirect reciprocity, and
norms that are *monitored and sanctioned* — by the community, and, on one influential
account, by omniscient moralizing "Big Gods" acting as societal glue [S2][S3][S4]. An
explicit, codified, universalizable ethic — a *dharma* — is exactly this technology: it
converts a stranger into "one of us" (co-religionist, co-citizen) and makes conduct
predictable without personal acquaintance [S2][S4], solving **societal** problems
(free-riding, stranger-trust, coordination, conflict resolution, division-of-labour
legitimacy) and **individual** ones (moral orientation and meaning once kin-intuitions no
longer reach). But "necessitate" overstates the case: Graeber & Wengrow show that scale
neither requires coercive hierarchy nor presupposes prior kin-tribes [S5], and Henrich &
Schulz show that dharmic codes can *cause* stranger-society rather than merely respond to
it [S7]. The accurate verdict: scale made dharmic codes powerfully *advantageous*
(selected-for), and codes and stranger-society **co-produced** each other. The expanded
corpus now holds the primaries behind every step of that argument and adds three things:
direct experimental support for the co-religionist mechanism across fifteen societies
[S11]; a settlement-scaling literature showing that cities are "social reactors" that
magnify interaction — and its costs — superlinearly [S12]; and archaeology of the earliest
cities showing that the problems codes addressed (internal violence, flight, the strong
preying on the weak) were generated by urbanism itself, and were as much the state's
problems as the citizen's [S13][S18][S19].

## Findings

### The mechanism that breaks down at scale
Robin Dunbar's "social brain hypothesis" ties stable-relationship capacity to neocortex
size, yielding a predicted human group size of ~150 — a figure Dunbar himself matched to
Neolithic Mesopotamian villages (150–200), Hutterite colonies that split at 150, and a
Tennessee mountain community of 197 [S6]. The number is a regression extrapolation, not a
measurement: re-running it with phylogenetic methods gives averages of 69–109 or 16–42 with
confidence intervals of 4–520, so "specifying any one number is futile" [S14]. Within
that range, kinship and direct reciprocity — plus the fact that everyone can watch everyone
— sustain cooperation. Pagel makes the structural point: **kinship caps group size** ("your
family's never going to be that huge"), and all other animal cooperation rests on kinship or
direct back-scratching; this is *why* no animal society lives in the billions. Humans needed
"a different mechanism… a scale-free mechanism" of shared identity and *indirect* reciprocity
to cooperate beyond kin [S3]. The transition to agriculture and settlement meant social life
was "no longer based exclusively upon face-to-face interactions": people must "frequently
engage in one-off transactions with anonymous strangers," and "traditional models of
cooperation break down at this scale" — the core "problem of cooperation" [S1]. Graeber & Wengrow counter that this premise is not universal: among modern hunter-gatherers (Hadza, Martu) residential groups are often not biological kin at all, and cities have existed without rulers or temples — so scale did not mechanically necessitate hierarchy or codified authority [S5].

### The dharmic solutions (what explicit codes supply)
- **Norms + monitoring + sanctions.** Henrich: the cultural evolution of social norms
  strengthens cooperation by "bring[ing] the community in as monitors"; sanctions for
  violations even reinforce kin altruism [S4]. Codified norms externalise and stabilise what
  face-to-face groups did informally.
- **Shared identity → indirect reciprocity.** A common code lets a stranger be treated as a
  co-member, so reputation and indirect reciprocity can operate among people who will never
  transact twice [S3].
- **Supernatural monitoring ("Big Gods").** Shariff & Norenzayan (2007) found that
  implicitly activating God concepts made subjects give more to anonymous strangers in the
  dictator game — an effect "at least as large as" priming secular moral institutions, and
  unrelated to self-reported religiosity [S15]; Purzycki et al. (2016, *Nature*) linked
  moralistic, punishing gods to the expansion of human sociality [S2]. The follow-up across
  fifteen socio-ecologically diverse populations (2,228 participants; foragers to wage
  labourers; Buddhists, Christians, Hindus, animists) found that rating one's god as
  monitoring and punishing "reliably predicted decreased local favoritism" and "increased
  resource-sharing with distant co-religionists," while effects toward religious *outgroups*
  varied by site [S11]. Belief that an omniscient deity monitors and punishes thus acts as
  "societal glue," extending cooperation to distant co-religionists where human supervision
  is absent [S2] — the stranger-into-co-member conversion, measured.
- **Division of labour under competition/warfare** selected for these institutions (Turchin &
  Gavrilets) [S2].

Together these *are* an explicit dharma: a transmissible, universalizable ethic that makes
conduct predictable among strangers — the "social technology for cooperation at scale" of
[[1.3-boundary-definition]] and the universal pattern of [[1.2-universal-patterns]].

### What problems, individual and societal
- **Societal:** the free-rider/defection problem at scale; trust and impartiality among
  strangers; coordination; conflict resolution beyond kin ties; legitimacy of impersonal
  authority; a workable division of labour [S1][S2][S3][S4].
- **Individual:** moral orientation and meaning once kin-based intuitions no longer suffice;
  navigating anonymity; an internalised compass — the "new interiority" and emergence of the
  individual already noted at the moment of codification in [[2.1-axial-age]].

### A concrete historical mechanism — and a reversal of the causal arrow
Henrich & Schulz (2019, *Science*, "The Church, intensive kinship, and global psychological
variation") supply a documented case: from ~500 CE the Western Church's **Marriage and Family
Program** banned cousin marriage, polygamy, and concubinage, dissolving intensive kinship into
"small, nuclear households, weak family ties, and residential mobility," which "fostered
greater individualism, less conformity, and more impersonal prosociality" — a precursor of
civic society; across their psychological measures, longer exposure to the Western Church
predicts greater impersonal prosociality with correlations of 0.43–0.69 [S7]. This **reverses the naïve arrow**: rather than anonymous cities forcing new
codes, a *dharmic code* actively dissolved kinship and manufactured the stranger-society
psychology suited to large-scale cooperation. Codes can be cause, not just effect.

### The monitoring need is context-dependent
Botero et al. (2014, *PNAS*), "The ecology of religious beliefs", is a **cross-sectional**
comparative analysis of 583 **traditional** societies. What it reports is a
**correlation across societies**: after controlling for shared ancestry and diffusion, belief
in moralizing high gods is "more prevalent among societies that inhabit poorer environments
and are more prone to ecological duress" — and, a detail the blog drops, "more likely in
politically complex societies that recognize rights to movable property" [S16][S2].

▲ **Study design.** Botero is ecological and cross-sectional. It did **not** track any society
over time, and it did not observe contemporary societies *becoming* less religious as they grew
better off. The correlation is between duress and moralizing gods, full stop.

The wider claim that as prosperity and stability rise — and impersonal institutions (rule of
law, bureaucracies, reputation systems) take over the monitoring role — the need for divine
surveillance *declines* is a **temporal and causal reading that Botero cannot supply**. It is an
inference: consistent with the cross-sectional pattern, and it is the existential-security
literature (Norris & Inglehart and successors) that actually tested it longitudinally.
▲ **That literature is not yet held in this note's Sources.** The nearest corpus test is
Purzycki et al. (2018): an individual-level check of the affluence hypothesis across 592
people at eight field sites (Vanuatu, Fiji, Mauritius, Tyva, Hadzaland, Brazil) that finds
"no reliable evidence of a relationship between number of children, material security, or
formal education and the individual-level religious beliefs and behaviors" [S17]. That is a
null on the *individual* mechanism, not a longitudinal test of societies; until Norris &
Inglehart or a successor is held, treat the secularisation-with-prosperity step as
*unsourced inference*, not as a finding.

Read that way, the section is still the bridge to **secular** dharma (see
[[6.1-secular-translation]]) and still sits beside the affluence finding in [[2.1-axial-age]],
provided the two are kept apart: Baumard's affluence bears on the historical *emergence* of an
ascetic, moralizing religious *type* as prosperity first rose, whereas Botero's duress
correlation bears on *where*, across societies, surveillance-heavy gods are found — a
distribution, not a trajectory [S2].

### Cities as reactors — and as generators of the problems the codes name
Settlement-scaling theory treats cities as "social reactors": larger settlements "magnify
social interaction opportunities," raising socio-economic output faster than population; the
same scaling is found in pre-Hispanic central Mexico as in modern cities, so the mechanism
is not a modern artefact [S12]. The authors add that "negative effects of intensified social
interaction, such as contagious disease and violence, also increase superlinearly with
population" and can "destroy the necessary conditions for large-scale settled sociality"
[S12]. The archaeology of the earliest cities bears this out: fourth-millennium BCE Tell Brak
grew rapidly and left three mass graves of mainly young adults, each of "up to several
hundred individuals," which the excavator reads not as external attack but as "civil war
generated by internal social stresses associated with urbanism"; these northern cities also
ran with low-density zones and flexible space, and "succeeded despite new sources of social
stress" [S13]. Scott adds the state's-eye view: the early state was "a variable, and a very
wobbly one," and "flight from the early state domains to the periphery was quite common"
but, contradicting the civilising-benefactor narrative, "is relegated to obscure legal codes"
[S18]. The codes, in other words, are themselves evidence of the problems: Hammurabi's
prologue declares the purpose of his law "to bring about the rule of righteousness in the
land, to destroy the wicked and the evil-doers; so that the strong should not harm the weak,"
and its provisions reach the widow remarrying without the judge's knowledge and the man who
buys her children's utensils [S19]. So the **societal problems** listed above are not a
theorist's reconstruction; they are what the earliest explicit codes say they are for — and
some of them (flight, the strong preying on the weak) were problems for the ruler at least
as much as for the ruled, which is [[7.3-dharma-and-power]]'s question arriving early [A].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The question's framing is doubly contestable — and the corpus itself supplies the
  counter-evidence.** Graeber & Wengrow (*The Dawn of Everything*, in the corpus) attack both
  ends: (a) the **kin-tribe premise** — statistical work on modern hunter-gatherers (Hadza,
  Martu) shows residential groups "turn out not to be made up of biological kin at all," and
  many humans actively avoid their relatives; ancient HGs look similar; (b) the
  **scale→hierarchy inference** — "the mere fact of urban life does not, necessarily, imply any
  particular form of political organization, and never did"; early cities ran for centuries
  without temples, palaces, or a ruling stratum [S5]. So "anonymous urban societies necessitated
  explicit codes" overstates: scale made codes *advantageous*, not mechanically necessary, and
  did not require coercive hierarchy.
- **Dunbar's number is itself contested** — Lindenfors, Wartel & Lind re-ran the extrapolation
  with phylogenetic methods and got averages of 69–109 (Bayesian) or 16–42 (GLS) with 95%
  intervals of 4–520 and 2–336: "a cognitive limit on human group size cannot be derived in
  this manner" [S14]. Dunbar's own 1993 support was a scatter of ~150-sized communities, not a
  measured ceiling [S6]. The "150" should be treated as a heuristic, not a constant.
- **The Big Gods thesis is correlational, and its best-known test has been retracted.** Even its
  proponents' data establish only correlation (Purzycki) and strong context-dependence (Botero) [S2].

  ▲ **Correction (2026-07-28).** This note previously reported that Whitehouse et al. (2019) found
  complex societies *precede* moralizing gods, with the Letter "later retracted on a coding critique,
  main finding largely upheld." That gloss cannot stand. *Nature* retracted the Letter on 7 July 2021
  [S8]. Beheim et al. showed that 61% (n = 490) of the moralizing-god observations passed to the
  statistical tests were originally missing values recoded as known absences — the correlation between
  missingness and recorded absence is r = 0.97 — and that under standard imputation the reported
  finding **reverses**, moralizing gods preceding rises in social complexity [S9]. Worse for the old
  wording: "main finding largely upheld" was the *retracting authors' own* characterisation of their
  retraction [S8], not an independent verdict, and three co-authors declined to sign it at all [S8].
  The same authors' reworked analysis no longer runs the original race, attributing both moralizing
  supernatural punishment and social complexity to a common driver — warfare intensity, supported by
  resource availability and agricultural productivity [S10].

  A further caution runs the other way. Lightner, Bendixen & Purzycki show that the Standard
  Cross-Cultural Sample's "moralizing high gods" variable is defined by a "theoretically
  irrelevant criterion" — whether the god is a *creator* — and so "underestimates the presence
  of moralizing gods by systematically producing false negatives," more often at lower social
  complexity, "strongly favoring a spurious positive association between social complexity and
  the presence of moralizing gods" [S20]. The "moralising gods are late" reading is therefore
  vulnerable from the data side as well as the analysis side.

  **What survives.** Nothing here restores gods as the *prime mover* of scale; nothing establishes the
  reverse either. The causal direction is genuinely open, so this note's conclusion now rests on the
  surviving correlational evidence alone: moralizing religion looks like one tool among several
  (alongside secular institutions) rather than the necessary cause. Stage 2 must not cite the 2019
  Letter in either direction. See [[1.2-universal-patterns]], where the "moralising gods are late"
  claim is carried by an *independent* phylogenetic line and does not depend on this exchange.
- **Corpus bias.** This note is unusually corpus-rich (5 KB sources, real books — Pagel, Henrich,
  Graeber & Wengrow) — the inverse of the cognate/Axial notes. But the corpus leans functionalist
  ("religion built cities / societal glue"), a frame that risks the same teleology Assmann criticises
  in [[2.1-axial-age]]; the Graeber & Wengrow material (also in the corpus) is the internal
  counterweight, and is given full weight here.
- **Now read in the primary (2026-08-27).** Dunbar 1993, Lindenfors et al. 2021, Shariff &
  Norenzayan 2007, Botero et al. 2014, Schulz et al. 2019, the Whitehouse retraction, Beheim et al.
  and Turchin et al. 2022 are all in the corpus and cited by chunk; the blog essay [S1][S2] is kept
  only for its framing and for Purzycki 2016 and Turchin & Gavrilets 2009, which remain second-hand.
  The Šuruppak instructions (ETCSL) are in the corpus but the file is a broken table and unusable;
  Hammurabi is cited from LW King's translation (Avalon), a 1910 rendering.
- **Corpus bias, second pass.** The expansion is heavily Big-Gods-debate material (a dozen files)
  and settlement-scaling; the counterweights present are Graeber & Wengrow, McMahon's archaeology of
  urban violence, and Scott's state-sceptical reading — all of which push against functionalism, in
  line with the corpus's anti-authoritarian lean. Missing still: a defender of the state-and-code
  as genuinely public goods (a Hobbesian or a Fukuyama), and any non-Mesopotamian early code
  (Egyptian instructional literature is in the corpus in Lichtheim's volumes but is not used here).
