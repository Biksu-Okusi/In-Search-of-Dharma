In 2016, and again in 2017, nine women farmers from the Kendeng hills of Central Java sat down at the gates of the Presidential Palace in Jakarta and poured wet cement around their feet.

They are Sedulur Sikep. Their way of living has no scripture, no clergy, no institution and no founder to consult: it was worked out by an illiterate farmer on a forest road in the 1890s. A century later it was still load-bearing enough to put nine women in concrete in front of a president. After the second protest, one of them, Patmi, died the night the cement came off her feet.

That is Part 3. There are nine others.

"In Search of Dharma" starts today. Preface, eight parts, a coda. Five and a half hours, read in full.

The argument is that a dharma is not a revelation but a technology: a piece of social engineering a clever, quarrelsome animal worked out for a permanent problem, then told itself it had found rather than built. The Sanskrit root is dhṛ, to hold. So the question is plain enough. What holds a life, a people, a world together?

And there is a test, which the book turns on its own hero case, and on itself. A dharma is better to the degree that it holds without crushing, and that it stays revisable by the people bound by it.
