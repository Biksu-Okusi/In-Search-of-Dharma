# 7.3 Dharma & Power

**Question:** How do dharmic frameworks both challenge and reinforce existing power structures? When do they liberate vs oppress? Are they inclusive or exclusive?

## Synthesis
Dharma has a double face toward power because its core asset — **transcendent normative
authority** — is a weapon available to whoever can wield it. Rulers capture it to sanctify
domination (varṇa, divine right, karma-as-desert, Calvinist election); the oppressed wield it
to indict that domination (the prophets, liberation theology, Gandhi, Ambedkar, Tolstoy's
Christian anarchism) [S4][S6][S7][S8][S18].
The deep irony, grounded in Christopher Boehm's evolutionary anthropology, is that morality
*originated* as an **anti-domination device** — egalitarian foragers' "reverse dominance
hierarchy" leveling would-be tyrants through ridicule, ostracism, and worse — so dharma's
liberatory face is its primordial function, and its legitimating face is a later capture by the
very hierarchies it evolved to prevent — a reversal Boehm ties not to agriculture as such
(settled agricultural tribesmen, he notes, "lack strong leadership and domination" and keep
"an egalitarian ideology") but to the chiefdom, where land ownership "stabilizes material
differences among households" [S1][S3]. The hinge between the two faces is whether the transcendent standard
sits *above* the throne (chastising power, as the Axial breakthrough made possible) or *below*
it (sanctifying power) [S5] — though Bakunin's maximal case denies the hinge altogether: any
master above the throne is still a master, and when God is abolished the legitimating function
simply migrates to a new warrant, History or Science [S16][S17]. On inclusion: every dharma
draws a boundary
([[7.1-failure-modes]]), and both universalist dharmas (open to all) and bounded dharmas
(guarding a people) have liberatory and oppressive modes — universalism can erase difference
imperially even as it includes. Dharma is therefore neither inherently liberating nor
oppressing but **contested, power-laden terrain** — and the same text can wear both faces at
once: Bellah's "civil religion" is by his own account "not… a form of national self-worship but…
the subordination of the nation to ethical principles that transcend it in terms of which it
should be judged" [S26], while Weber's sociology reminds the corpus that domination is
ordinarily *legitimate* in the eyes of the dominated, resting on law, tradition or charisma
rather than on fraud [S27]; and, as Foucault insists, even its
liberations install new disciplines, because there is no power-free dharma [S9].

## Findings

### The deep origin: morality as anti-domination
Boehm's *Hierarchy in the Forest* (in the corpus) argues that egalitarianism among
hunter-gatherers "arises from a collective commitment to personal freedom and autonomy,"
maintained through a **reverse dominance hierarchy**: "while humans have an innate tendency
toward social dominance," foragers "acting as moral communities" curb upstarts through
"ridicule, criticism, and ostracism," with leaders "expected to be generous and unassuming"
and serious domination episodes punished, "including execution, if necessary" [S1].
Egalitarianism, in Boehm's phrase, is thus "a hierarchy in which the weak combine forces to
dominate the strong" — and crucially "this political direction was somehow reversed after the
invention of agriculture, and an 'orthodox' version of social dominance hierarchy reappeared"
[S3]. The implication for dharma is profound: the moral capacity dharmas formalise *began* as
a leveling check on power, so dharma's anti-domination face is its original function, not a
later humanitarian veneer.

### Hierarchy before class — Bookchin's radicalisation, and a darker corollary
Murray Bookchin pushes the origin story one step further back, and in doing so complicates the
clean Boehmian arc. Where Marxists locate domination in class and the organisation of labour,
Bookchin argues that **hierarchy precedes class**: in early "organic societies" the roles based
on sex, age and lineage "do not seem to have been structured along hierarchical lines; nor do
they seem to have involved the domination of human by human" [S14], and hierarchy "eroded the
complementarity of relatively egalitarian communities long before the appearance of property
ownership" (Janet Biehl's editorial synopsis) [S14]. In Bookchin's own summary, "the idea of
dominating nature first arose within *society* as part of its institutionalization into
gerontocracies… and in patriarchies"; "various modes of social institutionalization, not modes
of organizing human labor," were "the first sources of domination," so domination "can be
definitively removed only by resolving problematics that have their origins in hierarchy and
status, not in class and the technological control of nature alone" [S14]. Even the drive to
dominate nature is, on this account, a projection of social domination — in Biehl's rendering of
his long-held position, it "could only have stemmed historically from the domination of human by
human, and not the other way around" [S14].

Two consequences for this note. First, if domination survives the abolition of class, a dharma
that only redistributes wealth or office has not touched the root, and every reform remains
exposed to re-capture at the level of status — which sharpens the Category-8 question below [A].
Second, and darker for the arc just drawn from Boehm: Biehl's synopsis lists "the domination of
whole communities by shamanistic and priestly guilds" among hierarchy's *earliest* elaborations,
before outright class [S14], and Bookchin himself writes of theistic systems "often formulated by
shamans and priests for their own self-serving ends, as well as by apologists for hierarchy…
calculated to produce subservience and quietism in the face of the social powers that be" [S15].
If that is right, the dharma-specialist is not merely a later captive of stratified power — the
priesthood was among hierarchy's founding forms, and the reinforcing face is nearly as primordial
as the liberating one. The same caution applied to Boehm applies here: Bookchin's organic-society
anthropology inherits the contested egalitarian baseline that Graeber and Wengrow dispute [S12].
But the structural point stands independent of the prehistory — status hierarchy and class are
separable axes of capture, and a dharma can legitimate the former while denouncing the latter [A].

### Capture I — legitimation by consent (the reinforcing face)
Once societies stratified, that moral authority was captured to sanctify the new hierarchies.
The mechanism is not mainly coercion but **consent**. In Gramsci's own formulation the
dominant group rules through "the 'spontaneous' consent given by the great masses of the
population to the general direction imposed on social life by the dominant fundamental group",
a consent "'historically' caused by the prestige (and consequent confidence) which the dominant
group enjoys because of its position and function in the world of production" — with the
state's coercive apparatus held in reserve "in anticipation of moments of crisis of command and
direction when spontaneous consent has failed" [S4]. Religion is one of the institutions through
which that consent is organised, though Gramsci is careful that it is never one thing: "every
religion, even Catholicism… is in reality a multiplicity of distinct and often contradictory
religions: there is one Catholicism for the peasants, one for the *petits-bourgeois* and town
workers, one for women, and one for intellectuals" [S4] — a dharma captured for hegemony is
captured stratum by stratum, not whole [A]. This is Marx's "opium" ([[7.1-failure-modes]])
given a finer mechanism — and Marx's sentence, read in full, already carries this note's
double face: religious suffering "is, at one and the same time, the *expression* of real
suffering and a *protest* against real suffering. Religion is the sigh of the oppressed
creature, the heart of a heartless world, and the soul of soulless conditions" [S23]. The
opium clause is the reinforcing face; the protest clause is the liberating one, in the same
breath [A]. The worked instances run through the whole project: *varṇa* and karma-as-desert
sacralising caste, Calvinism-to-meritocracy sanctifying inequality (both
[[6.3-economics-work-inequality]]), divine right, the Constantinian turn of a persecuted sect
into an imperial church, and the sacralisation of gender hierarchy ([[6.4-gender-and-dharma]]).
The *varṇa* case can now be shown from a primary dharmic text rather than named at second hand:
the Bhagavad Gita has the divine speaker author the fourfold caste by the differentiation
of *guṇa* and *karma* (4.13), assign each *varṇa*'s duties by the three modes of nature
(18.41–48), bind Arjuna to his hereditary warrior duty on pain of *sin* and a social disgrace
the text ranks worse than death (2.31–36), and rank one's own caste-duty above another's however
imperfectly performed (3.35, 18.47) [S10]. Transcendent authority, hereditary station, and
shame-sanction are fused in the scripture itself — the consent mechanism this section attributes
to Gramsci, visible in the primary text. ▲ **Paraphrase only, by chapter.verse.** Cited for what
the text argues; the anonymous corpus translation (register status *unverified*) carries no
wording-level weight — so nothing above is quoted, the caste terms in particular being exactly
the translation-sensitive renderings the caveat exists to block — and per the non-privileging
stance this is one tradition's duty-ethic shown to be constitutively tied to
*varṇa* — the analytical point — not an indictment of "Hinduism" as such [A].

### Capture II — the productive face (Foucault)
Foucault complicates the simple "repression" picture: power is not only top-down prohibition
but **productive**, making subjects: "We must cease once and for all to describe the effects
of power in negative terms: it 'excludes', it 'represses', it 'censors'… In fact, power
produces; it produces reality; it produces domains of objects and rituals of truth" [S9]. His
**disciplinary power** produces "docile bodies" [S9].
His **pastoral power** — the shepherd who cares for his flock across a whole life; an
individualising power requiring detailed knowledge of each subject, directing them toward a
prescribed self-knowledge through quasi-therapeutic authorities — belongs to a *different* text
[S21]. ▲ **Placeholder, not an established reading.** [S21] ("Omnes et Singulatim", 1979) is
**not held** — the Tanner Lectures site refuses scripted fetches — so the pastoral paragraph is
under-sourced and the wording above is deliberately paraphrase, not quotation. It must not be
tagged to [S9]: the word "pastoral" does not occur once in *Discipline and Punish* (verified by
full-text search), which supports the productive-power premise only. One manual download closes
this. On the pastoral reading *if it holds*, the confessional, the spiritual director, and the
meditation hall are technologies of power that *form* the self, not merely cages that constrain
it — which would mean even a liberating dharma installs a regime of self-governance. There is no view from
outside power.

### Capture III — the power to name
Power over a dharma is also power over its *definition*. Bourdieu's **symbolic power** is
precisely the stake of "the symbolic struggle for the production of common sense or, more
precisely, for the monopoly of legitimate naming as the official — i.e. explicit and public —
imposition of the legitimate vision of the social world", a struggle in which agents "bring
into play the symbolic capital that they have acquired in previous struggles" [S11] — the
authority to fix what counts as orthodox and what as heresy, what is *dharma* and what is
*adharma*. Whoever controls the definition controls the boundary of
[[7.1-failure-modes]], and thus who is inside the community and who is cast out: ecumenical
councils fixing the canon and naming heretics, Brahminical authority over who may even hear or
interpret *śruti*, the modern state's monopoly on legitimate classification. The corpus puts the
question in exactly these terms — *"Daring to define. By whose right?"* — noting that defining is
inseparable from "linguistic baggage… and etymological evolution," yet is needed because "we
need to be speaking the same language" [S22]. So definition itself wears the double face: a
shared dharmic vocabulary enables cooperation, but the right to set that vocabulary is a prize the
powerful seize — this note's whole argument, run at the level of meaning (whose semantics belong
to [[1.3-boundary-definition]]).

### Capture IV — the anarchist maximal case: transcendence itself enslaves
The Axial argument [S5] treats transcendence as the hinge that lets dharma chastise the throne.
Bakunin denies the hinge outright: "The idea of God implies the abdication of human reason and
justice; it is the most decisive negation of human liberty, and necessarily ends in the
enslavement of mankind, both in theory and practice"; "If God is, man is a slave; now, man can
and must be free; then, God does not exist" — and, reversing Voltaire, "if God really existed,
it would be necessary to abolish him" [S16]. His ground is structural rather than merely
anticlerical: "a master, whoever he may be and however liberal he may desire to show himself,
remains none the less always a master" [S16]. A standard *above* the king is, for Bakunin, just
a higher king; on this maximal reading there is no liberatory transcendence at all, and the
Axial "higher law" is the purest form of the legitimation problem, not its solution. (Morris's
treatment shows Bakunin subtler than the slogan: religion was also "the first gleam of human
truth," perhaps a "historic necessity" and not an "absolute evil," and free-thought propaganda
cannot kill it because people go to church as they go to the pub, "to alleviate their misery"
[S20] — the arch-critic conceding that dharma answers a real need.) And Bakunin's maximalism
stops short of the caricature: "Does it follow that I reject all authority? Far from me such a
thought. In the matter of boots, I refer to the authority of the bootmaker… But I allow neither
the bootmaker nor the architect nor the *savant* to impose his authority upon me" [S16]. What he
indicts is not authority but authority the taught cannot revoke — the distinction on which the
whole of this note's anti-authoritarian sourcing turns, and which the project treats as
constitutive rather than as a bias to be corrected [A].

What makes the anarchist case load-bearing rather than merely polemical is its **prediction**:
abolishing God does not abolish legitimation, because the legitimating function migrates.
Bakunin forecast that a Marxist seizure of the state would produce what Parker summarises as
"a new class of experts, scientists and professional politicians," their domination "far more
secure" through "the mystification and legitimacy granted by the claim to acting in accordance
with scientific laws" [S17] — a secular priesthood claiming Science as its *śruti* — and
**Parker's own verdict** is that the Bolshevik police state bore the prediction out [S17].
▲ That last step is the movement pamphlet's verdict, not a scholarly consensus, and is carried
here as such (the discipline of [[7.6-cpa-case]]); [S17] is used for Bakunin's documented
arguments and predictions, not for its judgements.

Tolstoy converged from the religious side: modern science, he argued, has itself become "a superstition" whose aim "is to maintain superstition
and deception among the people and thus hinder the progress of humanity towards truth and
welfare" [S18]. The lesson for this note is that the capture mechanisms of Sections I–III are
substrate-independent: God, History, Science, or the Model ([[8.4-encoding-dharma-machines]])
can each serve as the transcendent warrant a ruling stratum hides behind, and secularisation
relocates the altar without demolishing it [A].

### The return of the repressed: the liberating face
Yet the same transcendent authority is a weapon the powerless can seize, and the Axial
breakthrough ([[2.1-axial-age]]) made this structural. Bellah and Eisenstadt argue that Axial
transcendence "implied a certain relativization of politics… an assertion of the inherent
subordination of earthly government to higher objectives" — "not a secularization of power…
but rather a certain potential for its disenchantment or chastisement" [S5]; in Bellah's own
later formulation, Axial experiences "provide an insight into reality so deep that the whole
empirical world is called into question… the world is relativized in the light of an
all-encompassing truth" [S5]. A higher law above the king is a standing indictment of any king.
Walzer shows the mechanism in one book of one scripture: the Exodus — "the idea of a
deliverance from suffering and oppression: this-worldly redemption, liberation, revolution" —
has been read for "generation after generation of religious and political radicals" so that
"revolution has often been imagined as an enactment of the Exodus and the Exodus has often
been imagined as a program for revolution"; "wherever people know the Bible, and experience
oppression, the Exodus has sustained their spirits and (sometimes) inspired their resistance"
[S24]. From this vantage flow the resistance traditions:

- **Liberation theology** — Gutiérrez's *A Theology of Liberation* (1971) and its "preferential
  option for the poor," binding salvation to the material liberation of the oppressed [S6];
  Walzer records the Argentine theologian Croatto's "If we take the Exodus as our theme, we do
  so because in it Latin American theology finds a focal point… and an inexhaustible light"
  [S24].
- **Ambedkar's Navayāna Buddhism** — the 1956 mass conversion of ~500,000 Dalits to a Buddhism
  recast "as a tool for social equality, justice, and empowerment," which pointedly *denies
  karma and reincarnation* and reframes suffering "from internal to societal" [S7]. This is the
  thesis made surgical: Ambedkar's own indictment is that caste "is not merely a division of
  labourers… it is a hierarchy in which the divisions of labourers are graded one above the
  other", a division "not based on natural aptitudes" but on "the slight and stigma cast upon
  them by the Hindu religion" [S28]; so, to turn the dharma against caste oppression, he deleted
  the exact doctrine (karma-as-desert) that *legitimated* it — the doctrine the Gita states as divine
  ordination at 4.13 and 18.41–48 [S10]. The captured and the liberating dharma quarrel over the
  same verses.
- **Gandhi's satyagraha** — "truth-force" / "soul-force" nonviolent resistance, where dharma
  ("duty, religion, and ethics," measured by "love, compassion, truth") is mobilised against
  empire [S8].
- And the everyday register — Scott's "weapons of the weak" and hidden transcripts, drawn
  from "slavery, serfdom, and caste subordination" because there "the divergence between what I
  call the public transcript and the hidden transcripts was greatest", and privileging "the
  issues of dignity and autonomy, which have typically been seen as secondary to material
  exploitation" [S25] ([[4.1-samin-of-java]]), and Ubuntu mobilised in post-apartheid reconciliation via Tutu's Truth and Reconciliation Commission ([[4.2-ubuntu]]).

The believer's engine here is often a **providential faith** that, in King's words, "the arc of
the moral universe is long, but it bends toward justice" — a "potent source of hope… for people
struggling against injustice" [S2]. But Sandel notes the catch: this is providentialism, and
"the right side of history" is claimable by tyrants too (Putin, Assad "prevailed") — so the
moral arc is a motivating *wager*, not a guaranteed fact [S2].

### One gospel, two dharmas: Tolstoy against the throne
The Gita/Ambedkar pairing above shows one scripture read for caste and against it. The corpus
now documents the Christian parallel at full strength. The late Tolstoy — ever more radical with
age — concluded from "a serious study of the Bible" that "the basic teachings of Jesus were
absolutely opposed to violence of any kind," and built on that reading a fully-fledged
**religious anarchism**: *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* (1894) and the essays anthologised
as *Government is Violence*, though because anarchism then connoted bomb-throwing he never
adopted the label himself [S18]. The same canon the Tsarist church-state read as divine sanction
for autocracy yielded, in Tolstoy's hands, the doctrine that the state is organised violence and
that his own landowning caste — the book is pointedly addressed to "our caste" — should simply
"get off their backs, stop exploiting the working people" [S18]. Gandhi, already on this note's
liberating side [S8], was in Morris's words Tolstoy's "equally famous disciple" [S18]. And
Tolstoy, "long before the Russian revolution," predicted that the Jacobin theory of revolution
"would inevitably lead to another form of oppression" [S18] — the same forecast Bakunin derived
from opposite, God-denying premises [S17]. Convergent predictions from a Christian mystic and a
militant atheist suggest the object of the critique is the structure — institutionalised
coercive hierarchy — not any particular theology [A].

The pairing that keeps this honest is Goldman. Her 1913 essay — polemic, not scholarship, and
cited as such — reads the identical gospel as a slave-training manual: Christianity "is most
admirably adapted to the training of slaves"; rulers foster it because "the subtleness of the
Christian teachings is a more powerful protection against rebellion and discontent than the club
or the gun"; the Sermon on the Mount is "a eulogy on submission to fate"; and, against exactly
Tolstoy's move of rescuing Jesus from the church, the abuses of Christianity "are conditioned in
the thing itself, and are not to be charged to the representatives of the creed" [S19]. Two
anarchists, one scripture, opposite verdicts. This does not settle whether the gospel "really"
liberates or enslaves; it demonstrates the note's structural claim at maximum resolution — the
text underdetermines its politics, and a dharma's valence is fixed by the reading community and
its position toward power, not by the words on the page [A]. Even Bookchin, no friend of
religion, concedes the dialectic runs both ways: "Christian theology led logically to a growing
respect for individual worth and finally to radical conceptions of social freedom" [S15].

### The hinge, and inclusion vs. exclusion
Why the double face? Because institutions are usually held by the powerful, legitimation is the
default and resistance the harder act of wresting the tradition back; but the moment a
transcendent standard is posited, it judges the powerful too. Two primaries now in the corpus
sharpen the hinge. Dumont observes that in India "there has never been spiritual power, i.e.
a supreme spiritual authority, which was at the same time a temporal power. The supremacy of the
spiritual was never expressed politically. Thus in the theory of the varnas one finds that
status and power are differentiated" [S29] — the standard sat *above* the throne and *apart*
from it, which is exactly the arrangement that lets the same dharma sanctify the king's
station and deny him the priest's; and Bellah's "civil religion" is the modern nation-state's
version, a set of shared sacred references that he defends as "the subordination of the nation
to ethical principles that transcend it in terms of which it should be judged", while conceding
"the everpresent danger of national self-idolization" [S26]. Weber supplies the vocabulary the
corpus otherwise lacks for the reinforcing face without a conspiracy: "there are three pure
types of legitimate domination", resting on legal, traditional or charismatic grounds, and "a
certain minimum of assured power to issue commands, thus of domination, must be provided for in
nearly every conceivable case" [S27] — domination is ordinarily *believed in*, which is what
makes capture cheap and resistance costly [A]. As for inclusion: universalist
dharmas (Buddhism, Christianity, Islam — "neither Jew nor Greek" [S13]) include across ethnic lines
but carry an imperial edge (convert-or-erase; the universal that flattens difference, cf.
[[7.2-appropriation]]), while bounded/ethnic dharmas can oppress internally yet also *protect*
a minority's identity against absorption. Every dharma draws a boundary
([[7.1-failure-modes]]); the liberatory question is where it is drawn and how permeable it is.

### Reflexive turn: the machine dharma as a power question
A dharma encoded into machines ([[8.4-encoding-dharma-machines]]) is the purest version of this
note's problem: whoever controls the model controls the encoded ethic. Will a machine dharma
function as a Boehmian *reverse-dominance leveler* — a higher standard that chastises the
powerful — or as the new legitimation ideology of a tech aristocracy, sanctifying whoever owns
the compute? The answer is not in the ethics encoded but in who holds the institution.

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias
The corpus is **power-attentive and serves this question well** — Boehm, Graeber, Scott, and
Sandel are all alert to domination, and Boehm's reverse-dominance thesis [S1] is a genuinely
strong, in-corpus spine. But that same roster tilts **anti-authority and egalitarian** (Boehm's
and Graeber's leanings especially), so the corpus reads dharma-as-resistance more readily than
dharma-as-legitimate-order, and under-weights the conservative case that dharma rightly
*stabilises* society — that some hierarchy and authority are legitimate and necessary (the
Confucian and Burkean defence of order, and the dharma-as-social-glue established in
[[5.2-evolutionary-psychology]] and [[6.2-digital-age-dharma]]). I have stated that
counter-case explicitly rather than inheriting the corpus's tilt.

Three further cautions. **First**, Foucault's point that power is productive and ubiquitous
[S9] dissolves any clean liberate-vs-oppress binary: "liberation" installs its own disciplines,
so the honest question is *which* regime of power-formation, not power vs. freedom. **Second**,
the resistance strands were typically marginalised by their own institutions — the Vatican's
doctrinal office disciplined liberation theology; Ambedkar's movement still fights caste [S6][S7]
— a reminder that the institutional centre usually sides with order, so the liberating face is
the harder-won exception. **Third**, Boehm's "reversal after agriculture" is a powerful but
contested thesis: the egalitarian-forager baseline is not uniform (some foragers were
hierarchical, and Graeber & Wengrow's *Dawn of Everything* disputes any single original
condition) [S12], so the clean arc from leveling to capture should be held as a strong hypothesis,
not settled fact. The deepest unresolved question — handed to Category 8 — is whether a dharma
can check power *without itself becoming a power*: Boehm's leveling had no permanent office, but
every institutionalised dharma becomes a new hierarchy ([[7.1-failure-modes]] routinization).

**On the 2026-07-27 corpus fold.** The anarchist accession (Bookchin, Bakunin, Goldman,
Tolstoy-via-Morris) deepens the corpus's constitutive anti-authoritarian lean, which obliges
disclosure rather than balance: every new voice treats institutionalised religion as an apparatus of domination, and the
mediating commentators are advocates writing in movement registers — Morris closes his Tolstoy
essay declaring "there really is no alternative to the kind of anarchism he espoused" [S18],
Parker's pamphlet is an activist primer [S17], Goldman is open polemic [S19]. I have used them
documentarily, for what Tolstoy and Bakunin argued (quotations verified against the corpus
texts), while discounting their verdicts; Morris's aside that "no refutation has ever been
forthcoming" of Bakunin on religion [S20] is a partisan flourish, not a finding. On balance the
additions strengthen the *reinforcing* face at least as much as the liberating one — the
priesthood among hierarchy's founding forms [S14][S15], transcendence as such indicted [S16],
scripture as slave-training [S19] — so the note has not drifted toward the romance of
resistance. But the conservative counter-case that dharma legitimately *stabilises* order still
rests entirely on out-of-corpus sourcing, and grows relatively weaker with every anarchist
accession; the reader should weigh that asymmetry [A].
