# 7.8 Tribalism & Dharma

**Question:** Every dharma binds an *us*. What is the evolved machinery it binds with, how does a
dharma become a badge of membership rather than a path, where has that badge turned lethal, what
has the networked age added, and what do dharmas themselves hold against the fence they build? The
book treats parochial altruism in Parts 2, 5, 6 and 8 under other names; this note gathers that
evidence and adds the modern political and identity form, religion as team colours, heresy as
defection, the Indonesian cases, and the test the floor of
[`the-better-ones.md`](../the-better-ones.md) can put to all of it.

## Synthesis
Tribalism is not a defect that dharmas acquire but the substrate they are built on. The brain
sorts faces into Us and Them within milliseconds, infants prefer speakers of their own language,
and an arbitrary label is enough to make strangers favour their own group and punish the other
[S8][S37][S39]. Parochial altruism, in-group generosity fused with out-group hostility, is one
leading account of how human cooperation evolved at all, and the chimpanzee border patrol is its
oldest model, though the models are sensitive to their assumptions and in-group love does not
require out-group hate [S1][S2][S5][S6][S43]. Shared ordeal fuses people until they will fight and
die for one another; that fusion is the proximal cause of the sacrifice every dharma honours and
every war exploits [S10][S11][S13][S47]. A dharma becomes a tribe when its membership outruns its
path: when the nation inherits religion's power to "turn chance into destiny", when "unbelievers
… not more to be esteemed than beasts" can be counted as "one and a half human beings", when
Pancasila is declared a philosophy under which "differences in values are impossible"
[S19][S26][S62]. Heresy is the in-group's name for revision, and schism sits beside parricide among
the five acts a Buddhist cannot undo; tribalism therefore attacks the procedural test directly
[S67][A]. The networked age has not invented any of this but has built an engine for it: platforms
that "exploit the human brain's attraction to divisiveness", moral-emotional words that spread 20%
faster per word, exposure to the other side that makes people *more* partisan, and a Facebook that
was "a useful instrument for those seeking to spread hate" in Rakhine [S17][S52][S53][S59]. The
antidotes are also inside the dharmas: superordinate goals, the widening circle, the Order of
Interbeing's refusal to be "bound to any doctrine, theory, or ideology, even Buddhist ones", and
the Maluku women who read a peace declaration on alternate days [S25][S38][S70][S73]. For the floor,
the badge is where a dharma stops being revisable: a tribe can change its enemies but not its
fence [A].

## Findings

### 1. The machinery: speed, labels, and the chimpanzee border

- **Milliseconds.** "Our brains form Us/Them dichotomies … with stunning speed. … fifty-millisecond
  exposure to the face of someone of another race activates the amygdala, while failing to activate
  the fusiform face area as much as same-race faces do — all within a few hundred milliseconds.
  Similarly, the brain groups faces by gender or social status at roughly the same speed" [S8:339].
  Yet "not everyone's amygdala activates in response to an other-race face … subtle manipulations
  rapidly change the amygdaloid response to the face of an Other" [S8:75]. Infants "prefer to look
  at a person who previously spoke their native language", and the preference "may serve as a
  foundation for later-developing preferences and conflicts among social groups" [S39].
- **An arbitrary label is enough.** In Tajfel's minimal groups, "the subjects favoured their own
  group in the distribution of real rewards and penalties in a situation in which nothing but the
  variable of fairly irrelevant classification distinguished between the ingroup and the
  outgroup", and they sought "a maximum difference between the ingroup and the outgroup even at the
  price of sacrificing other 'objective' advantages" [S37]. Christakis: "If you make your altruism
  contingent on the other person being a member of your group … you can increase the odds that he
  or she will be willing to pay you back", so group identity "helps facilitate cooperation, even
  with strangers — as long as the strangers are 'one of us'" [S7:265].
- **Love without hate.** Brewer's review finds "ingroup identification is independent of negative
  attitudes toward outgroups and … much ingroup bias and intergroup discrimination is motivated by
  preferential treatment of ingroup members rather than direct hostility toward outgroup members"
  [S43]; Silva and Mace's Northern Ireland field experiments found conflict depressed out-group
  cooperation while leaving in-group cooperation untouched, so the two halves of parochialism can
  come apart (see [[5.2-evolutionary-psychology]]) [S3].
- **Parochial altruism and war.** Choi and Bowles modelled a late-Pleistocene world in which
  "neither parochialism nor altruism would have been viable singly, but by promoting group
  conflict, they could have evolved jointly" [S1][S36]; Bowles later estimated that "the estimated
  level of mortality in intergroup conflicts would have had substantial effects, allowing the
  proliferation of group-beneficial behaviors that were quite costly to the individual altruist",
  and called conflict "altruism's midwife" [S36]. Darwin had said it first: a tribe whose members,
  "from possessing in a high degree the spirit of patriotism, fidelity, obedience, courage and
  sympathy, were always ready to give aid to each other and to sacrifice themselves for the common
  good, would be victorious over most other tribes" [S5:160]; Sumner's "Loyalty to the group,
  sacrifice for it, hatred and contempt for outsiders, brotherhood within, warlikeness without —
  all grow together, common products of the same situation" is the sociological form [S7:269].
  ▲ Dyble's re-run of the model found the result "highly sensitive to population structure and to
  civilian and fighter mortality" [S2]; Puurtinen and Mappes show cooperation rising under
  between-group competition in the laboratory without anyone dying [S9]. The link is real but not
  automatic [A].
- **The border patrol.** At Ngogo, "over nine years, 114 of these patrols attacked and killed 21
  members of other chimpanzee troops … about three-quarters of the chimpanzees in the other group
  can expect to be murdered by a patrol before dying of old age", until in 2009 the Ngogo troop
  simply moved into the emptied range [S6:202]. Wrangham's imbalance-of-power hypothesis holds that
  coalitional killing "is the expression of a drive for dominance over neighbors", requiring only
  "a state of intergroup hostility" and "sufficient imbalances of power between parties that one
  party can attack the other with impunity" [S42]; the human ideal "has always been the surprise
  attack with overwhelming force that leaves the opponents defeated, captured, or dead — and the
  attackers unharmed: the very model of coalitionary" killing [S5:281]. Henrich's conclusion is the
  uncomfortable one: the social norms that "weave together our communities, create interdependence,
  foster solidarity" were "gradually selected by intergroup competition over eons" [S6:381];
  Turchin's version is "We cooperate to compete" [S44]. ▲ Henrich also notes that chimpanzees have
  no norms, so intergroup competition "may have preceded the emergence of cultural evolution" and
  shaped norms "as soon as they began to emerge" [S6:207].
- **The hormone, again.** De Dreu's finding that oxytocin "drives a 'tend and defend' response …
  promoted in-group trust and cooperation, and defensive, but not offensive, aggression toward
  competing out-groups" [S41] is the claim the book withdrew in Part 5: intranasal oxytocin
  studies "are generally underpowered and … there is a high probability that most of the published
  intranasal OT findings do not represent true effects" [S41]. What survived is behavioural, not
  hormonal: across 51 nations "people invested more when facing an in-group rather than out-group
  member or stranger", a "nasty neighbor" effect that "emerges when people perceive within-group
  resource scarcity" [S41]. The fence is built by habit, scarcity and story, not by a molecule
  (see [[5.1-neurological-basis]]) [A].
- **Robbers Cave, with its caveat.** Sherif's 1954 study found that "the mere fact of contact had no
  positive effect toward reducing existing hostility", while cooperation on superordinate goals
  "will have a cumulative effect in the direction of reduction of existing tension between groups"
  [S38]. ▲ Bregman and Perry record that Sherif's earlier 1953 study failed because "the boys kept
  working together and the research team ended up at each other's throats trying to provoke them",
  and that "the competitions were fabricated by the staff" [S45]. Boys will tribe if you make them;
  they do not always do it on their own [A].

### 2. Fusion: why people die for the group

- **Identity fusion.** "Identity fusion is a relatively unexplored form of alignment with groups
  that entails a visceral feeling of oneness with the group … measures of fusion are exceptionally
  strong predictors of extreme pro-group behavior" [S47]. "Sharing painful experiences produces
  'identity fusion' – a visceral sense of oneness – which in turn can motivate self-sacrifice,
  including willingness to fight and die for the group" [S10]. Among 179 Libyan revolutionaries in
  2011, front-line fighters were more fused with their battalion than with their own families, and
  fusion was "a key proximal cause of personally costly, progroup behavior or 'parochial
  altruism'" [S11:0,1]; "degree of shared dysphoria in battle predicted strength of fusion with
  brigade" [S15:10]. Positive high-arousal rituals do it too (Brazilian jiu-jitsu) [S12], as does a
  lifetime of football [S14]. In Ukraine, fusion "with the country, the leader, and a core value
  (freedom)" predicted costly action for allies, mediated by "spiritual formidability" — the will to
  fight that US intelligence called "imponderable" [S13:3,27].
- **The tragedy of commonsense morality.** Greene: "commonsense morality requires that we
  sometimes put Us ahead of Me… the same disposition also leads us to put Us ahead of Them"; our
  emotions "make us social animals, turning Me into Us. But they also make us tribal animals,
  turning Us against Them" [S40]. Haidt's Part III title is "Morality binds and blinds", his
  metaphor "90 percent chimp and 10 percent bee" [S40]; the intuitionist model underneath it is
  that "moral emotions and intuitions drive moral reasoning, just as surely as a dog wags its tail"
  [S16]. ▲ Part 5 already carries Greene's two tragedies; this is the research behind it.
- **The relational definition of extremism.** "Radicalization is an inherently relational concept.
  One can only be radical in relation to someone who is not" — and the processes that produce it
  are "normal psychological processes", parochial altruism among them [S4]. New religious movements
  that make "a sharp distinction between 'us' and 'them'", describing non-believers as "agents of
  The Enemy" or "Babylon", are the ones Barker's watchers flag as potentially violent [S23].

### 3. The badge: when the dharma stops being a path

- **Religion scaled cooperation and conflict together.** Norenzayan's eight principles run from
  "Watched people are nice people" to "Religious groups cooperate in order to compete"; "sincere
  faith in watchful Big Gods unleashed unprecedented cooperation within ever-expanding groups, yet
  at the same time it introduced a new source of potential conflict between competing groups"
  [S46]. Taylor lists the great widenings — "the New Testament, the call by Mohammad to go beyond
  tribe or nation in founding the new Umma, Stoicism, the Buddha's stepping beyond caste and other
  ritual distinctions" — and notes that "these moves always arouse both opposition, resistance,
  from some people, but open up new vistas" [S21]. Lecky's circle: "At one time the benevolent
  affections embrace merely the family, soon the circle expanding includes first a class, then a
  nation, then a coalition of nations, then all humanity, and finally, its influence is felt in the
  dealings of man with the animal world"; Singer made it his epigraph [S71]. Each widening dharma
  then drew a new fence at its new edge [A].
- **The nation as the successor badge.** Anderson's nation is "an imagined political community —
  and imagined as both inherently limited and sovereign" [S19][S56]; the eighteenth century "marks
  not only the dawn of the age of nationalism but the dusk of religious modes of thought", and
  nationalism supplied "a secular transformation of fatality into continuity, contingency into
  meaning … It is the magic of nationalism to turn chance into destiny" [S19:16]. Its ritual is
  "unisonance": "Singing the Marseillaise, Waltzing Matilda, and Indonesia Raya provide occasions
  for unisonality, for the echoed physical realization of the imagined community … How selfless this
  unisonance feels!" [S19:200]. Gellner: "Nationalism is primarily a political principle, which
  holds that the political and the national unit should be congruent" [S57]; Hobsbawm's invented
  traditions "seek to inculcate certain values and norms of behaviour by repetition, which
  automatically implies continuity with the past" [S58]. Schmitt made the fence the whole of
  politics: "The specific political distinction to which political actions and motives can be
  reduced is that between friend and enemy" [S59]; Asad notes that if secularised concepts "retain
  a religious essence, we might be induced to accept that nationalism has a religious origin" [S20:229].
  Ibn Khaldūn's *ʿaṣabiyya* is the oldest theory of the badge: "When group feeling is destroyed, the
  tribe is no longer able to defend or protect itself, let alone press any claims", and "as a rule
  no dynasty lasts beyond the life (span) of three generations" [S60].
- **Identities are codes, and codes can be switched.** Scott's Mons and Burmans differed in "body
  markings (Burmans tattooed below the waist), hair style … clothing, and language. A change in
  identity was a matter of substituting these codes … As Ava's power waxed, so did the proportion of
  the population adopting Burman cultural codes" — "the conflict itself served to crystallize and
  politicize certain cultural markers that later came to be seen as ethnic" [S22]. Appiah: "people
  who share an identity share a label, not an essence" [S63]. The aesthetic-as-fence finding of
  [[1.5-aesthetics-and-dharma]] (schismogenesis) is the same point from the other side [A].
- **Secular violence needs no scripture.** "When General Ali Haidar of Syria, under the orders of
  his secular president Hafez al-Assad, massacred 30,000 to 40,000 civilians in the rebellious town
  of Hama in 1982 — he did not invoke the Qur'an — nor did the secularist Saddam Hussein when he
  gassed thousands of Kurds" [S20:10]. Al-ʿĀmirī, a thousand years earlier, had to answer the charge
  that Muslims "kill each another (and even each other's children!)", and blamed the empire's new
  converts for the sects [S33]. Tribalism is prior to the creed that dresses it [A].
- **Adat realigned to one religion.** In Indonesia, "a concept of *adat* once capable of
  accommodating people of diverse religious faiths in a shared *adat* community has declined or
  been rearticulated in less inclusive ways … redefined in relation to just one religious identity";
  in Banda in 1999–2000 "the entire indigenous Christian minority was driven from the islands"
  [S25:44]; religious identity is "an increasingly important source of symbolic capital" [S25:128].
  Yet Bräuchler argues that *adat* "and the village alliance system it enshrines can provide the
  integrative push necessary for reconciliation by encompassing the entire Moluccan society and
  overcoming religious divisions" [S25:84]. The same institution is fence or bridge depending on who
  is allowed to define it [A].

### 4. Heresy: tribalism's attack on revision

- **The unforgivable act.** The Buddha's five "fatal wounds" that "lead to a place of loss, to hell"
  are "murdering your mother or father or a perfected one; maliciously shedding the blood of a
  Realized One; and causing a schism in the Saṅgha" (AN 5.129) [S67] — the charge the lamas levelled
  at the Rigpa eight (see [[6.6-sexuality-and-dharma]]). Amsterdam's rabbis on Spinoza, 27 July
  1656: "Cursed be he by day and cursed be he by night; cursed be he when he lies down and cursed be
  he when he rises up. Cursed be he when he goes out and cursed be he when he comes in" [S68]. Caste
  reintegration "requires the sanction of religious and even royal authority … rites which are both
  expiations and purifications" [S30]. Classical Islam set the bar against *takfīr* high — "outward
  appearance of major impiety does not automatically translate into permission to engage in takfir;
  one has to proceed on a case-by-case basis" — which is exactly the constraint the modern jihadists
  removed [S69].
- **Why the fence outlives the argument.** Kuran's preference falsification, "the act of
  misrepresenting one's wants under perceived social pressures", explains how a tribe can be
  hollow for years and still hold [S61]; the apartheid-era crowds that killed suspected
  *impimpis* show the in-group policing itself with rumour, "far more patterned, directed and
  limited than usually depicted" [S24]. ▲ For the floor this is the decisive observation: tribalism
  is the condition under which revision from inside is read as defection, and the procedural test
  — *revisable by those bound* — cannot be met, because the person who tries to revise ceases to be
  one of the bound [A].

### 5. The lethal cases

- **"One and a half human beings."** The *Mahāvaṃsa* has eight arahants console Duṭṭhagāmaṇī after
  the slaughter of the Tamils: "Only one and a half human beings have been slain here by thee, O
  lord of men. The one had come unto the (three) refuges, the other had taken on himself the five
  precepts. Unbelievers and men of evil life were the rest, not more to be esteemed than beasts"
  [S64]; Tambiah's *Buddhism Betrayed?* traces the line from that chronicle to Sinhala Buddhist
  nationalism [S64]. In October 2017, "amidst the atrocities in Rakhine", Sithagu Sayadaw preached
  the same passage to Burmese soldiers [S28:17].
- **Myanmar.** Buddhist protectionism is "political and legal activism aimed at protecting the
  buddhasasana from perceived external threat, including measures to maintain ingroup purity"; its
  four "race and religion" laws (2015) grew from the fear "that Buddhist women marry non-Buddhist
  men, leading to a demographic shift" [S28:4]; "the night before the violence rumors about rape of a
  Buddhist girl by a Muslim man went viral on social media, including on a Facebook account in the
  name of U Wirathu" [S28:17]; at Meikhtila in 2013 "outside groups [were] again bused in to lead a
  full pogrom", 43 dead and 830 buildings destroyed [S29:15]; monks of the Saffron Revolution were
  offered money to join Ma Ba Tha, which "is controlled by the military" [S29:17]. The UN mission
  found "the role of social media is significant. Facebook has been a useful instrument for those
  seeking to spread hate, in a context where, for most users, Facebook is the Internet", and that the
  crimes "are similar in nature, gravity and scope to those that have allowed genocidal intent to be
  established in other contexts" [S65]. ▲ The Dhammapada's "hostilities are never appeased by
  hostility" is quoted by the same monks' critics [S29:5]; the text did not restrain the badge [A].
- **Hindutva.** Savarkar: "to every Hindu, from the Santal to the Sadhu this Bharata bhumi this
  Sindhusthan is at once a Pitribhu and a Punyabhu — fatherland and a holy land", whereas for
  Muslims and Christians "Their holyland is far off in Arabia or Palestine" [S66]. King notes the
  irony "that the very Hindu nationalists who fought so vehemently against British imperialist rule
  themselves accepted the homogenizing concepts of 'nationhood' and 'Hinduism', which ultimately
  derived from their imperial rulers" [S31:137]; the discourse "excludes (such as Indian Muslims) at
  the same time as it subsumes others (such as Buddhists, Jains, and Sikhs) through the elision of
  'Indian' with 'Hindu'" [S31:275]; Halbfass records Golwalkar's and Savarkar's claim that "Hindu" was
  never a foreign word [S32]. The Indian government's own count for Gujarat 2002: "254 Hindus and 790
  Muslim … were killed in the post Godhra riots", 1,044 in all, with 223 missing [S66].
- **Indonesia, 1965–66.** Suharto's account — "Thousands fell victim in the provinces because the
  people acted on their own, and because of nasty prejudices between social groups" — is, Roosa
  shows, the regime's etiology of spontaneous violence from below, which "many Indonesians, even
  those otherwise critical of state propaganda, believe" [S27]. Robinson's history concludes that
  the killings, "leaving some five hundred thousand people dead and more than a million others in
  detention", were "the product of a deliberate campaign, led by the Indonesian Army", with civilian
  militias as its instruments [S72]; Cribb's collection gives the range of estimates, from a
  government report's 75,000 to the émigrés' two million [S72]. The sexual slander that lit it is in
  [[6.6-sexuality-and-dharma]].
- **Tanjung Priok, 1984.** Amir Biki, at a protest meeting on 12 September 1984 "shortly before the
  speaker and up to 300 locals were gunned down by the military": "If at 11 tonight our friends are
  not turned over to us, we will make blood flow in Priok!!" — and, in the same speech, "the Muslim
  community does not hate Christians. No, my friends" [S26:309,316,311]. The badge and the
  disclaimer in one breath [A].
- **Maluku and Poso, 1999–2002.** At least 5,000 dead, perhaps 10,000, and some 700,000 displaced
  before the Malino accords (Poso, 20 December 2001; Maluku, February 2002) [S74]; the conflict
  "evolved to take on a religious frame and narrative, pitting Muslims against Christians" [S25:84].
  On 6 August 1999 Catholic nuns and Protestant women pastors met at Rinamakana and invited Muslim
  women in; "Muslim and Christian women alternately read the declaration on different days, always
  accompanied by security escorts", the Protestant reading "accompanied by 250 other Christian women"
  and the Muslim reading the next day [S25:192,193].
- **Ahok, 2016–17.** The Jakarta election "saw unprecedented mobilization of Muslims outside any
  formal political groups, and the defeat of a popular governor, Basuki Tjahaja Purnama (Ahok), a
  Christian of Chinese descent, for, at least partly, religious reasons" [S25:246]; more than 500,000
  gathered at Monas on 2 December 2016, and on 9 May 2017 the court found him "convincingly guilty of
  committing blasphemy" and gave two years, more than the prosecutors asked [S73]. Mietzner and
  Muhtadi found that "conservative attitudes among Indonesian Muslims were declining rather than
  increasing prior to the mobilisation" — the badge was raised, not the belief [S73]. Hefner and
  Bagir: "the success achieved in the Jakarta election, grounded in a discriminatory idea toward
  citizens from minority backgrounds, constitutes a serious challenge to pluralist ideas; the same
  strategy may be deployed again … as was attempted in the 2019 presidential elections" [S25:267];
  the mobilisation's ground was that "the Muslim majority must have a Muslim as their state"
  leaders [S25:264]; in Manado, Christian *adat* paramilitaries "rose up, demanding that the FPI be
  disbanded" [S25:258]. Hefner's later verdict: a "'conservative turn' in Islamist circles committed
  to the creation of a religiously differentiated citizenship", though "the supporters of a
  religiously inclusive Islam have by no means been vanquished" [S75].
- **The riot's shape.** Horowitz's deadly ethnic riot is "an intense, sudden, lethal attack by
  civilian members of one ethnic group on civilian members of another ethnic group", "directed
  against a precisely specified class of targets", triggered by rumour, with "the surprisingly
  limited role of deliberate organization" [S80]. Sapolsky names the propagandist's tool:
  "Dehumanization, pseudospeciation … Thems as rodents, as a cancer … Thems as shit. Get the insulae
  of your followers to confuse the literal and metaphorical, and you're 99 percent of the way there"
  [S8:511].

### 6. The engineered unity: a dharma against tribalism that became a tribe

- **"Differences in values are impossible."** Ali Moertopo, information minister, 24 May 1980:
  "our nation's philosophy is Pancasila and our state's ideology is Pancasila. It is all uniform, so
  conflict is impossible, quarrels are impossible, differences in values are impossible"
  [S26:237]; Suharto on the 1975 party law: all parties "base themselves on one ideology, Pancasila",
  and when one party "added another principle … we were compelled to accept the situation while
  continuing our attempts to bring them around" [S26:219]. The P4 indoctrination decree followed in
  1978 and the *asas tunggal* law in 1985: "Organisasi Kemasyarakatan berasaskan Pancasila sebagai
  satu-satunya asas" [S76]. Roosa's "state religion, complete with sacred sites, rituals, and dates"
  (see [[6.6-sexuality-and-dharma]]) was its liturgy. Hefner and Bagir's question for the Jokowi
  revival — "overlapping consensus or tool of exclusion" — is the right one: the same Pancasila was
  used to ban HTI and to reassure minorities [S25:268]; Muhammadiyah reads it as "a national
  consensus [*dar al-ʿahdi*] and a testimony" that "is not religion, but its substance contains
  Islamic values" [S25:146]. The motto underneath, *Bhinneka tunggal ika tan hana dharma mangrwa* —
  "they are indeed different, but they are of the same kind, as there is no duality in Truth" — comes
  from Mpu Tantular's fourteenth-century Buddhist–Śaiva poem [S77]. ▲ An anti-tribal dharma that
  forbids difference has not abolished the tribe; it has made the state the tribe, and defined the
  communist, the Ahmadi and the atheist as its Them (see [[7.7-engineered-dharmas]]) [A].

### 7. The amplifier: affective polarisation and the machine

- **Affect, not ideology.** "Both Republicans and Democrats increasingly dislike, even loathe, their
  opponents", while their policy disagreement is "inconsistently (and perhaps artifactually)"
  related to that affect [S48]; "ordinary Americans increasingly dislike and distrust those from the
  other party … We trace its origins to the power of partisanship as a social identity" [S49].
  Mason's "mega-identities": "once these mega-identities get formed, we start to think of out-group
  partisans as quite different from us" [S50]; Klein: "Our political identities have come to engulf
  all our other identities" [S51]. Chua: "the tribal instinct is not just an instinct to belong. It
  is also an instinct to exclude" [S54]; Fukuyama's *thymos*, "the part of the soul that craves
  recognition of dignity", is the engine of identity politics on every side [S55]. Putnam's 2020
  afterword adds Asmolov's reading of influence campaigns: their purpose "is not to shape people's
  perceptions about reality, but rather to dissolve horizontal ties among people by increasing the
  impact of conflict-related social categorization" [S18:505].
- **The engine.** Facebook's own researchers in 2018: "Our algorithms exploit the human brain's
  attraction to divisiveness", delivering "more and more divisive content in an effort to gain user
  attention & increase time on the platform"; executives shelved the research [S17:6]. Brady and
  colleagues: "the presence of moral-emotional words in messages increased their diffusion by a
  factor of 20% for each additional word" [S52] — and "when a liberal posted a tweet with
  moral-emotional words, its reach substantially increased among other liberals, but declined with
  conservatives", so the incentive is to alienate the other side [S17:137]. Bail's bots: "Republicans
  who followed a liberal Twitter bot became substantially more conservative posttreatment" [S53].
  "We enjoy being outraged. We respond to it as a reward" — the platforms learned to supply "a rush —
  of purpose, of moral clarity, of social solidarity" [S17:82]. "People, as a rule, perceive
  out-groups as monoliths … Political scientists call this 'false polarization'" [S17:142]; the
  Russian playbook was "Appeal to people's group identity. Tell them that identity was under attack.
  Whip up outrage against an out-group" [S17:148]. Berger's "crisis-solution" spiral — "there is a
  crisis, the out-group is responsible, your in-group offers the solution" — runs "until destroying
  the out-group becomes the core of the in-group's shared identity" [S17:211].
- **Babel, not Babylon.** Haidt's diagnosis of the 2010s is "the story of Babel … But Babel is not
  a story about tribalism; it's a story about the fragmentation of everything" [S79]. Putnam's
  "Daily Me" and the backfire effect say the same from the civic side [S18:503]. ▲ The research is
  young, US-centred and contested; the Myanmar evidence [S65] is the one case where a UN body has
  put the platform in the causal chain [A].

### 8. What dharmas hold against the fence

- **Superordinate goals and contact that works.** Sherif's finding that contact alone does nothing
  and shared tasks do [S38]; Brewer's that the in-group can be loved without the out-group being
  hated [S43]; Sapolsky's that the amygdala's response "rapidly change[s]" with recategorisation
  [S8:75]. Christakis: "we can see ourselves as *all* being part of the same group … We can efface
  the tribalism of small groups and find a kindness for large groups" [S7:2].

### 9. Reading the assertion with the two tests [A]

- **The badge fails the procedural test by construction.** A dharma held as a tribe can change its
  enemies but not its fence, because the fence is what membership is; revision from inside is
  heresy, and the reviser is expelled before the revision can be heard. This is the general form of
  the Rigpa case in [[6.6-sexuality-and-dharma]] and of every "we must have a Muslim leader" or "one
  and a half human beings". The substantive test fails too, on the other side of the fence, which is
  where the crushing happens [A].
- **The no-out-group problem.** [[8.1-planetary-dharma]] already notes that the planetary problems
  exclude nobody; this note adds that the machinery which built every cooperative dharma was
  selected *by* out-groups, so a dharma without a Them is being asked to run without its engine. The
  evidence of section 8 is that the engine can be re-aimed (superordinate goals, fusion with a value
  rather than a people, the widened circle) but not removed [A].
- **Where it goes in the second edition.** Part 6 has the pogrom; Part 8 has the climate case. What
  the book lacks is the modern political form (section 7), religion as badge (section 3), heresy as
  the attack on revisability (section 4) and the Indonesian cases (sections 5–6). A few paragraphs
  in Part 6 after the parochial-altruism passage, and a paragraph in the Coda, would carry it; the
  aesthetic-as-fence finding of 1.5 is the natural bridge [A].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The corpus lean.** The knowledgebase answers from evolutionary and cognitive science (Choi and
  Bowles, Henrich, Wrangham, Sapolsky, Christakis, the Oxford fusion school), from the Indonesian
  politics the author has curated (Hefner and Bagir, Bourchier and Hadiz, Roosa, Frydenlund), from
  Fisher's journalism on social media, and from anarchist theory of federation. It holds no
  political psychology of US polarisation (Iyengar, Mason, Klein are external), no nationalism
  theory beyond Anderson, no theology of heresy, and nothing on Hindutva from inside India except
  Truschke, King and Halbfass. The anarchist alternative in section 8 is the corpus's constitutive
  lean, flagged as such and used only as a comparative case.
- **Contested evidence.** The coevolution model is sensitive to assumptions (Dyble) and Northern
  Ireland separates the two halves of parochialism (Silva and Mace); the oxytocin leg is withdrawn;
  Robbers Cave was staged; the social-media literature is young and partly US-only, and Haidt's own
  reading is "not a story about tribalism". The identity-fusion programme is mostly one laboratory's.
  Figures for 1965–66 range from 75,000 to two million; the Gujarat 2002 count is the government's
  and is disputed upward; the Malino II date is given as 12 or 13 February 2002.
- **Cited at second hand.** Greene's and Haidt's book pages; Sapolsky's chapter 11 wording beyond the
  corpus; Norenzayan's "parochial" phrasing (not found; the Princeton blurb and the 2008 *Science*
  paper used instead); Mason, Klein, Chua, Fukuyama, Appiah, Kuran (publisher pages and reviews);
  Anderson's, Gellner's, Hobsbawm's and Schmitt's page numbers; Tambiah's own text (Geiger's
  *Mahāvaṃsa* was read); Robinson's and Cribb's texts (publisher and review pages); the Ansor/Banser
  role (Fealy, snippet); Nadler's p. 120; Sabrang's higher Gujarat figure. Haidt's Atlantic essay was
  read in an MIT-hosted PDF; the UN report, Savarkar, Lecky, Hafez and UU 8/1985 were read in full.
- **OCR sources.** Wrangham, Dumont, King, Halbfass and Asad are `text-ocr`; the quotations were
  checked in context and should be verified against print before Stage 2 quotes them.
- **The author's yardstick.** To call the badge a failure is to rank the path above the membership,
  which is the book's position and not every tradition's; a Sinhala nationalist, a Hindutva
  theorist and a Pancasila ideologue would each say the fence *is* the dharma. The note records their
  words and judges them by the floor, not by their own lights.
- **What remains unresolved.** Whether fusion with a *value* ("freedom" in Ukraine) escapes the
  out-group logic or merely relocates it; whether any large-scale cooperative order has run without
  an external Them; and whether the networked fragmentation Haidt describes is tribalism's
  intensification or its successor.
