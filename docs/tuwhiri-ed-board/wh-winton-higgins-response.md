# Winton Higgins: Response to Gary Dean, *In search of dharma*

- Source: `WH - Response to Gary Dean In search of dharma.pdf`
- Reviewer: Winton Higgins (WH), Tuwhiri editorial board

This ms is a tour de force across many disciplines – comparative religion, social anthropology, neuroscience, behavioural psychology, evolutionary biology, philosophy and Buddhist studies. Perhaps others as well. They all contribute to a clear discursive arc based on the examination of ethical systems which have underpinned human communities and societies beyond small-scale foraging bands. Historically most societies have adorned their systems with a religious rationale that embeds them in belief systems which enmesh members in an encompassing way of life and a sense of their place in the universe as mandated by one or several gods.

The ms is co-written with an AI. The writing is clear and accessible. The argument is well organised.

My main issue is with the use of the word 'dharma' as a catch-all for the many diverse traditions in question on the one hand, and the actual focus of the ideas of various sages who have supposedly helped to construct the 'dharmas' in question. I think Gotama (and Jesus for that matter, and maybe a few others) would have been surprised to learn that what he'd launched as 'dharma' was in fact a 'technology' designed 'to sustain cooperation and hold the group together' (p.23). We could (with a little difficulty) ascribe that purpose to those who institutionalised his teaching in religious form after his death, but not to him.

Institutional religion as a social practice has indeed underpinned social cohesion, which usually included legitimating the temporal powers-that-be. But Gotama himself (and Jesu, and others) seems to have focused on how individual human beings might develop the full ethical potential of the human estate and live well. In this way he worked on our interiority, and took social and political arrangements as simple givens. As an ethic, his dharma has social and political ramifications, but I think the emperor Ashoka was the first to start drawing them out two centuries later.

Today's secular-dharma practitioners would have difficulty seeing the dharma they practise as merely a contribution to social cohesion, especially as it rests on the focus on individually experienced interiority (spirituality) in Gotama's own retrieved teachings. Many of them ramify their core dharma practice into social and political activism to express their central ethic of care, including care for the planet that now stands in dire jeopardy. But that's another (if related) story.

In sum, I'd have preferred another central working term for ethical systems that underpin social cohesion, and where appropriate to account for their varying relationships to spiritual traditions ('dharmas'?), both religious and secular.

That said, Gary weaves a compelling argument for the need to construct an ethical framework ('dharma') which would unite many different societies in the urgent task of saving the world from human-made catastrophe. That project would involve displacing today's ruling 'dharma' – one oddly not accounted for in his text – neoliberal capitalism. Here is an ethical system which sanctifies as overriding values the optimal growth of shareholder value, leading to optimal growth of GDP. It accounts for the cohesion of today's economic rulers and is destroying the planet.

So I think we can go ahead and publish this thought-provoking book.

Winton
