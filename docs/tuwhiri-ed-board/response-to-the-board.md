# Response to the Tuwhiri editorial board

- Status: working note, 2026-09-14
- Reviews: `girol-karacaoglu-on-in-search-of-dharma.md`, `jf-jeff-fracher-on-gary-deans-ms.md`, `sk-l-on-in-search-of-dharma-v1.md`, `sk-l-in-search-of-dharma-v2.md`, `wh-winton-higgins-response.md`
- Purpose: gather the board's criticisms in one place, say where the manuscript already stands on each, and propose what to change. Proposals are proposals; nothing here is applied until agreed.

## The verdict

Every reviewer who gave one said publish. Girol Karacaoğlu strongly, as a self-described outsider. Winton Higgins plainly, with a dispute about the central term. Starr Kelton-Locke with revisions, softened between her v1 and v2. Jeff Fracher gave no explicit verdict but closed with "otherwise, I enjoyed it". Nobody disputes the thesis, the sourcing, or the writing. The criticisms are about order, balance, tone and one missing case.

## The criticisms, and how the book might respond

### 1. The preface buries the lede

*Starr v1 point 2, v2 point 1; Jeff on the AI disclosure.*

**What they said.** The preface opens with the author's biases and the story of how the book came about. The thesis arrives late. Jeff adds that the AI disclosure, while admirable, may put some readers off.

**Where the book stands.** The preface runs in this order: "A note on two words", "The impetus", "The shape of the lens", "The one idea", "Show the workings", "What this is, and isn't". The one idea is already stated in a single sentence at the top of its section; it is simply in fourth place.

**Proposed response.** Reorder, do not rewrite. Move "The one idea" to directly after "A note on two words", then "The impetus", "The shape of the lens", "Show the workings". Every disclosure stays in full: Jeff calls the transparency a virtue, and the project's methodology depends on it. The reader meets the claim first and the disclosures as a warranty on it. The AI section stays where the workings are shown, which now falls after the reader has a reason to care.

### 2. The science comes from a narrow, agreeing handful

*Jeff.*

**What they said.** Sapolsky, Haidt and "others who mostly agree with each other". Risk of confirmation bias. Might dissenting scientists be included?

**Where the book stands.** Parts 2 and 5 already cite Norenzayan, Henrich, Greene, Bloom and Wrangham beside Sapolsky and Haidt, but they sit on the same broad side of the cooperation-and-culture debate. The Stage 1 research notes each carry a "Tensions, gaps & corpus bias" section that names exactly this lean, and the preface flags it once ("secular, evolutionary, broadly Western, and a bit academic"). The disclosure exists; the argument with the dissenters does not appear on the page.

**Proposed response.** Surface the notes' apparatus in the Parts. One paragraph in Part 2 and one in Part 5 that name who disputes the frame and answer them: the group-selection critics (Pinker's "false allure" argument) against Boehm and Haidt; the Whitehouse and Seshat side of the big-gods dispute, which the book already meets via Lightner, Bendixen and Purzycki; Kurt Gray's critique of moral foundations against Haidt. This turns a disclosed bias into a visible argument, which is what Jeff is asking for. Sources for the dissenters go through the usual harvest into `appliedanthropology/workshops/DD/`.

### 3. Uneven attention to the cases; the Abrahamic section is defensive

*Jeff.*

**What they said.** Sumarah gets long, loving treatment. The Christian, Jewish and Muslim traditions are squeezed into a shorter, more defensive section.

**Where the book stands.** The wong sikep have a Part of their own (Part 3), and Part 4's Sumarah section is its longest at roughly 1,200 words against roughly 950 for the Covenant. The imbalance in length is modest. The preface says why the Javanese material dominates: it is the author's lived material, three decades of it, and the book is written from inside it. That is a strength and stays. The tone point is the real one. The Covenant section is written as a rebuttal of the objection that religions cannot be dharmas, while Ubuntu, the Stoics and the Dreaming are written as admiring case studies.

**Proposed response.** Two changes. Rewrite "The Covenant: looking at your own" in the same register as the other four: a case study that shows what the Covenant holds and how, with the objection answered in passing rather than as the frame. Second, Islam. The author lives in the largest Muslim country on earth and the book scarcely treats Islam as a dharma. Of all the possible additions this is the one that earns its space: a Part 4 section on a lived Islamic dharma, most naturally the Javanese *santri* world the author has watched for thirty years, which would also answer the Sumarah imbalance from the inside rather than by cutting Sumarah.

### 4. Repetition, and "essentially contested concept" as an escape hatch

*Jeff.*

**What they said.** Built from separate notes, the book explains the same key points over and over. And invoking Gallie's "essentially contested concept" lets the author avoid defending his own definition as rigorously as he critiques others'.

**Where the book stands.** The repetition is structural and real. The cooperation-and-free-rider paragraph appears in the preface, Part 1 and Part 2; the placeholder point about the word appears in the preface, Part 1 and the coda. Some of that is deliberate (the coda restates by design). Some is residue from note-to-essay assembly. On Gallie: the book uses the phrase once, in Part 1, about the *word*. Its own stipulation is a four-feature checklist, defended in Part 1, tested in the notes (1.3), and revised there when it fails (most recently against Campbell). The book does hold itself to its definition; it does not say so where Gallie appears.

**Proposed response.** A cross-Part repetition audit: for each key point, one home, pointers elsewhere. This is the same treatment the hand-off echoes received in the de-slop pass. On Gallie, one sentence at the point of use: contestedness belongs to the word; my own definition is a stipulation, and a stipulation is not exempt from criticism, so here is what would count against it. The coda's two tests are already falsifiable in that sense and can be pointed at.

### 5. He doth protest too much about neutrality

*Jeff.*

**What they said.** The author says repeatedly he will not rank one way of life above another, but his warmth toward anarchism and Javanese mysticism, and coolness toward organised religion, show plainly. Either admit the bias more openly or hide it better.

**Where the book stands.** The project's settled position is the first option: the anti-authoritarian lean is constitutive, disclosed, and not to be balanced away. The preface does disclose it, but across several paragraphs, and the phrase "refusal to rank the dharmas in advance" can be misread as a claim to have no preferences. The book also ranks, after the fact, by the two tests in Part 8 and the appendix. A reader who has absorbed only the refusal will hear the later verdicts as smuggled.

**Proposed response.** Say it bluntly once, in the preface, in a single sentence: I am warmer to anarchists and Javanese mystics than to churches, and here is why. Then draw the distinction the book already relies on but never states as a pair: no ranking *in advance*, but judgement *afterward* by two tests that any dharma can be held to, the author's favourites included. Ideally show the tests biting on a favourite: the wong sikep's revisability is not obviously better than a synagogue's. Cut the phrase "will not rank" wherever it appears without the qualifier.

### 6. "Dharma" is the wrong central term; neoliberal capitalism is missing

*Winton.*

**What they said.** Gotama, Jesus and other founders worked on interiority and took social arrangements as given. To call what they launched a technology "to sustain cooperation and hold the group together" misdescribes them; the social function belongs to those who institutionalised the teaching afterwards, and to Ashoka. Today's secular-dharma practitioners would not recognise their practice as "merely a contribution to social cohesion". Winton would have preferred another central term for the cohesion systems, reserving "dharma" for the spiritual traditions. Separately: the ruling dharma of the present day, neoliberal capitalism, goes unaccounted for.

**Where the book stands.** This is the deepest criticism and the book has most of an answer without stating it. The book studies dharmas at the moment they hold a community, not at the moment a sage launches them. Winton concedes that moment himself: Ashoka "drawing out" the social ramifications is the institutional stage the book is about. The four features include "identity-constituting: binds from the inside", and Part 5 is entirely about interiority, so the charge of "merely social cohesion" is answerable from the text. But the distinction between the founder's project and the community's technology is nowhere made explicit, and a reader as careful as Winton had to infer it and did not. The Campbell material added to notes 1.3, 6.1 and 8.3 last week carries the same objection in stronger form: the numinous function that the checklist omits, the "mythogenetic zone" being the individual heart. One reply can serve both critics.

On capitalism: Part 6 contains one passing "capitalistic" and nothing else. The gap is simply true.

**Proposed response.** Two additions. First, in Part 1 or Part 2, a short passage that names the two moments, founder and community, and says which one the book studies and why, with the interiority feature pointed at so that "merely social" cannot stick. The 1.3 note's Campbell paragraph is the sourced basis. Second, a Part 6 section that runs neoliberal market ideology through the checklist and the two tests. It is comprehensive, identity-constituting, held by its carriers as the good, and self-aware enough to have a canon and a clergy; it holds, and it crushes, and it fails the revisability test for almost everyone bound by it. The corpus already holds the material (Graeber, Bregman, and the Polanyi line in the notes). This gives Part 6 its most contemporary example and answers Winton with the book's own instruments rather than a concession.

On the central term itself: the book keeps "dharma", for the reason Part 1 and the coda give (the root says most plainly what the thing does: hold), and because the two-tier alternative Winton sketches, cohesion systems on one hand and spiritual traditions on the other, is the very split the book argues against. That is a disagreement to state respectfully, not to resolve. Winton's own secular dharma is one of the book's instances, and the argument for plurality is best made from that shared ground.

## What no reviewer criticised, and should worry us

Girol, the friendliest reader, took away that "the function that dharma serves is identical across humanity although it comes in numerous manifestations". That is the functional-perennialism reading the 8.3 note flagged last week as the project's soft spot: "one job, many tools" is a hair's breadth from "one truth, many masks", and the coda's "made, plural, revisable" is meant to keep them apart. If the most sympathetic reader lands on the first, the coda is not yet doing that work. Worth one sentence in the coda, and one in Part 4's "What rhymes", saying plainly that a shared job does not make a shared essence.
