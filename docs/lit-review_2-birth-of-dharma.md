# Literature review — Part 2, *The Genesis of Dharmas*

**Date:** 2026-07-28 · **Scope:** downloadable full-text sources bearing on `2-birth-of-dharma.md`
and its underlying notes (2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, 2.5), plus the borrowed material the essay leans on
from notes owned elsewhere (1.2, 5.2, 5.5).

## Why this review exists

The five notes behind Part 2 carry **92 numbered sources** between them (2.1 → 6, 2.2 → 7,
2.3 → 15, 2.4 → 33, 2.5 → 31), and the essay's "Key works" list names thirteen works. The notes
are honest about their own weaknesses — 2.1 flags that "Jaspers, Eisenstadt, Bellah, Armstrong,
Graeber, Assmann, and Baumard are cited via secondary/encyclopedic sources"; 2.4 flags that
"Weber, Ashoka's edicts and Vedic transmission (Staal) remain secondary or encyclopedic". This
review takes those flags at their word and finds what can actually be read.

▲ **Provenance warning.** As in Part 1, a large share of the book-length items below are
*community uploads of in-copyright works* on archive.org and personal course sites — live today,
removable tomorrow, and not publisher-authorised OA. Under this project's source policy these are
**reading copies only**: read them, turn them into **attributed summary notes** at
`$VECTORDBS/appliedanthropology/workshops/DD/sources/`, and never redistribute or fold the PDFs
into the corpus. Items marked **OA** are genuinely open-licensed.

▲ **Framing note (project policy).** Nothing below bears on whether *dharma* is a real
cross-cultural category. The Axial Age is a **specimen**, not the concept: Mullins et al. can
dissolve the 800–200 BCE window entirely and Part 2's thesis — that ethical codes are made things,
forged under scale pressure — stands, because that thesis does not require the codes to have been
made *simultaneously*. What the demolition costs the essay is a rhetorical hook, not an argument.
Likewise, the corpus's anti-authoritarian lean is disclosed in notes 2.3, 2.4 and 2.5 by design;
the counterweights listed here are for *first-hand engagement*, not ballast.

## Tier 0 — the items that change the most, soonest

1. **The moralizing-gods retraction dossier — Whitehouse et al. (2019) *Nature*, retracted 2021.**
   Retraction Note: <https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03656-3.pdf> `verified` · **OA**
   Beheim et al. critique, author preprint (LSE): <https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/110927/1/Beheimetal_Preprint_20Jan2020_1_.pdf> `verified`
   (the published *Nature* Matters Arising, doi:10.1038/s41586-021-03655-4, is `walled`).
   **This is the single most urgent item.** Note 2.2 currently says Whitehouse et al. 2019 found
   complex societies precede moralizing gods, with the parenthetical "(Letter later retracted on a
   coding critique, main finding largely upheld)" — and note 2.1 leans on the same result as
   corroboration for Baumard. That parenthetical is a *contested* characterisation, not a neutral
   one. Beheim et al. show that 61% of the Seshat moralizing-gods data points were missing values
   which the authors **re-coded as known absences**, and that under any standard imputation "the
   reported finding is reversed: moralizing gods precede increases in social complexity." The
   authors retracted rather than concede; the reworked analysis (item 1b) then shifted the
   explanation to *warfare*, not to the original claim. Any sentence in DD that says "complex
   societies precede moralizing gods" must now name which version of the literature it rests on.

   1b. **Turchin, Whitehouse, Larson et al. (2022), "Explaining the rise of moralizing religions",
   *Religion, Brain & Behavior*** — the post-retraction reworking. 2019 OSF preprint:
   <https://osf.io/2v59j/download> `verified`; published version at
   <https://doi.org/10.1080/2153599x.2022.2065345> `browser` (T&F bot-blocks). The finding now:
   moralizing beliefs "did not drive the rise of social complexity", but **intergroup warfare
   supported by agricultural productivity** drove *both* — i.e. shared drivers, not a causal
   relation. That is a materially different claim from the one the notes carry.

2. **Mullins, Hoyer, Collins et al. (2018), "A Systematic Assessment of 'Axial Age' Proposals
   Using Global Comparative Historical Evidence", *American Sociological Review* 83(3).**
   OSF preprint (full text): <https://osf.io/r9czx/download> `verified`
   Published version, Exeter ORE: <https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/36086> `browser`
   The empirical demolition of the Axial Age window, using the same Seshat databank. Results
   "undercut the notion of a specific 'age' of axiality limited to a specific geo-temporal
   localization"; the traits proponents offer as evidence "appeared across Afro-Eurasia hundreds,
   and in some cases thousands, of years prior to the proposed Axial Age." Note 2.1's contested-
   status section currently cites only Assmann and Boy & Torpey — conceptual critiques. This is the
   quantitative one, and it is the strongest thing anyone has published against the essay's opening
   puzzle. Citation trajectory is steady (8 in 2025), so it is not going away.

3. **Karl Jaspers, *The Origin and Goal of History* (1953) — full text, 294 pp.**
   <https://archive.org/download/jaspers-karl-the-origin-and-goal-of-history-2014/Jaspers%2C%20Karl%20-%20The%20Origin%20and%20Goal%20of%20History%20%282014%29.pdf>
   `verified` · grey upload (archive.org `opensource`, unrestricted).
   Also `lending` copies at `origingoalofhist0000jasp_q8t5`, `origingoalofhist0000karl_t8v5`.
   The essay names Jaspers in its second paragraph and note 2.1 concedes he is "cited via
   secondary/encyclopedic sources", including "a generic, AI-authored Jaspers summary". This
   retires that. It also lets the essay's Eurocentrism caution be stated from Jaspers's own text
   rather than from Assmann's report of it.

4. **Eugene Halton (2014), *From the Axial Age to the Moral Revolution: John Stuart-Glennie,
   Karl Jaspers, and a New Understanding* (Palgrave) — full text.**
   <https://archive.org/download/eugene-halton-from-the-axial-age-to-the-moral-revolution-john-stuart-glennie-kar/Eugene%20Halton%20%20-%20From%20the%20Axial%20Age%20to%20the%20Moral%20Revolution_%20John%20Stuart-Glennie%2C%20Karl%20Jaspers%2C%20and%20a%20New%20Understanding%20%282014%29pdf.pdf>
   `verified` · grey upload (folkscanomy).
   ▲ **A substantive challenge to the essay's own sentence.** Halton's case is that the Scottish
   sociologist John Stuart Stuart-Glennie described the same phenomenon as "the Moral Revolution"
   in **1873** — three-quarters of a century before Jaspers — and that Jaspers's framing carries
   metaphysical baggage Stuart-Glennie's did not. The essay says flatly "The German philosopher
   Karl Jaspers gave this window a name." That is the received story and it is disputed in print by
   a full-length monograph. One clause of hedging costs nothing and buys accuracy.

5. **Baumard, Hyafil, Morris & Boyer (2015), "Increased Affluence Explains the Emergence of
   Ascetic Wisdoms and Moralizing Religions", *Current Biology* 25(1):10–15.**
   <https://www.cell.com/article/S0960982214013724/pdf> `browser` (Cell Press free-archive OA; 403s
   to scripted fetch, opens in a browser). DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2014.10.063>
   **Companion, and free:** Baumard, Hyafil & Boyer (2015), "What changed during the axial age:
   Cognitive styles or reward systems?", *Communicative & Integrative Biology* 8(5).
   <https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC4802742?pdf=render> `verified` · **OA**
   The affluence thesis is the essay's second causal pillar ("A second pressure, less obviously,
   was prosperity") and note 2.1 carries it entirely through a prosocial.world blog post. The
   addendum matters independently: it is where Baumard and Boyer specify the *mechanism* (life-
   history reward systems, not cognitive style), which is precisely the part the essay paraphrases
   as "human motivation tends to shift from grabbing what you can today toward patience".

6. **Sosis & Bressler (2003), "Cooperation and Commune Longevity: A Test of the Costly Signaling
   Theory of Religion", *Cross-Cultural Research* 37(2):211–239 — full text.**
   <https://www.cognitionandculture.net/wp-content/uploads/Sosis_2003_CommuneLongevity.pdf> `verified` · author-hosted
   **Plus the current reconceptualisation:** Maciej Potz (2022), "Costly Commitments 'Under His
   Eye': Reconceptualizing the Costly Signaling Theory of Religion", *JAAR* 90(3):599–626.
   <https://academic.oup.com/jaar/article/90/3/599/7049418> `browser` (OUP 403s to scripts).
   The essay's entire "price of belonging" section rests on Sosis, and its sting-in-the-tail —
   that secular communes "could not earn the same return on strictness" because religious claims
   "can never be disproved" — is stated as Sosis's argument but appears in **no 2.x note at all**.
   Potz is the reason to hurry: he argues the mechanism is *not* commitment-broadcasting but
   supernatural-watching, and that the secular shortfall is explained by the absence of that
   mechanism rather than by unfalsifiability. If Potz is right, the essay's forward-looking claim
   about secular dharma "giving up one of the most powerful binding mechanisms" is right in
   conclusion but wrong in reason.

7. **Robert N. Bellah, *Religion in Human Evolution: From the Paleolithic to the Axial Age*
   (Harvard UP, 2011) — full text.**
   <https://archive.org/download/edureligion/Religion%20in%20Human%20Evolution%20by%20Robert%20N.Bellah%202011.pdf>
   `verified` · grey upload. (Lending copy: `religioninhumane0000bell`.)
   Note 2.1 cites Bellah's "theoretic culture" at metadata level; note 2.5 cites "nothing is ever
   lost" by ISBN alone. Both are load-bearing — 2.5's whole "accretions of ages" section turns on
   that phrase — and both are one download away from being first-hand.

### ▲ Substantive challenge found here
**The Axial Age window may not exist.** Mullins et al. (Tier 0 #2) is not a nuance; it is a
finding that the traits used to define axiality predate the window by centuries or millennia. The
essay already carries a caution ("it is a pattern we have drawn on the past, not a switch that
flipped") — that caution is now *empirically backed* and can be stated much more strongly, with a
citation, rather than as authorial modesty. Note 2.1's own "self-undermining exclusion" point
about Egypt and Mesopotamia is exactly what Mullins et al. quantify.

### ▲ Substantive challenge found here
**Cesaretti et al. and McMahon both weaken the tidy "cities needed codes" causal story from the
archaeological side**, and Graeber & Wengrow weaken it from the anthropological side. Note 2.2
already lands on the right verdict ("scale made dharmic codes powerfully *advantageous*, not
mechanically necessary"), but the essay's prose is more deterministic than the note: "the city
faced a stark choice: find a new way to make strangers trustworthy, or fall apart." That sentence
is stronger than the evidence supports.

## 6 · Bonding and bordering: the fence at the end of the essay

The essay's penultimate paragraph — "the bonding and the bordering tend to evolve together: the
warmth turned inward and the suspicion turned outward are two faces of one adaptation" — is stated
as settled evolutionary science and cited to Choi & Bowles (2007) in the Key Works only. It is not
settled.

- **Choi & Bowles (2007), "The Coevolution of Parochial Altruism and War", *Science* 318.**
  <https://sites.santafe.edu/~bowles/2007ScienceParochialAltruismWar.pdf> `verified` · author-hosted
  (mirror: <https://pdodds.w3.uvm.edu/files/papers/others/everything/choi2007a.pdf> `verified`)
- ▲ **Mark Dyble (2021), "The evolution of altruism through war is highly sensitive to population
  structure and to civilian and fighter mortality", *PNAS* 118(11).**
  <https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC7980410?pdf=render> `verified`
  **This is a direct re-analysis of the Choi & Bowles model and it fails.** Dyble finds the result
  "relies on a degree of genetic differentiation between groups that exceeds that seen among
  hunter-gatherers", and that "when the model produces a more realistic population structure,
  altruism does not evolve, casting doubt on the plausibility of the parochial altruism hypothesis."
- ▲ **Columbus, Thielmann, Zettler & Böhm (2023), "Parochial reciprocity", *EHB* 44.**
  <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2023.02.001> `walled`
  Experimental (N=973): individuals who valued their in-group more were **no more likely** to harm
  the out-group. What predicted harm was *expected* out-group aggression. In-group love and
  out-group hate are dissociable, not two faces of one adaptation.
- **Corr, Hargreaves Heap, Seger & Tsutsui (2015), "An experiment on individual 'parochial altruism'
  revealing no connection between individual 'altruism' and individual 'parochialism'",
  *Frontiers in Psychology* 6.** **OA**
  <https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01261/pdf> `verified`
  Same dissociation, independently found.
- **Puurtinen & Mappes (2009)** — already in the corpus and cited by 2.3 [S13]; the *pro* side.
- **Efferson, Lalive & Fehr (2008), "The Coevolution of Cultural Groups and Ingroup Favoritism",
  *Science* 321** — <https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1155805> `browser`. Shows the marker-based
  mechanism the essay describes ("turns a stranger into one of us") actually working in the lab.
- **Hruschka & Henrich (2013), "Economic and evolutionary hypotheses for cross-population variation
  in parochialism", *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 7.** **OA**
  <https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2013.00559/pdf> `verified`
- **Lang et al. (2019)** — see §2; finds moralising gods reduced *local* favouritism and, at some
  sites, extended generosity to religious out-groups, "suggesting that in the absence of intergroup
  hostility, moralizing gods may be implicated in cooperative behaviour toward outgroups." The
  fence is not automatic.

## Claims that should be softened or footnoted regardless of acquisition

- **"Complex societies precede moralizing gods"** (note 2.2's parenthetical) — the paper carrying
  this was **retracted in 2021**, the critique reversed the sign of the result under standard
  imputation, and the authors' own reworked analysis now attributes both variables to warfare and
  agricultural productivity. The phrase "main finding largely upheld" is the retracted authors'
  characterisation, and should be attributed to them rather than stated.
- **"The German philosopher Karl Jaspers gave this window a name"** — Halton's monograph argues
  Stuart-Glennie named the same phenomenon in 1873. One clause.
- **"A remarkable number of them erupted in a single narrow window"** — Mullins et al. find the
  defining traits appearing across Afro-Eurasia centuries to millennia earlier. The essay's own
  caution paragraph should carry this citation and could be moved earlier.
- **"The city faced a stark choice: find a new way to make strangers trustworthy, or fall apart"** —
  stronger than note 2.2's own verdict ("advantageous, not mechanically necessary"), and stronger
  than the archaeology (McMahon; Cesaretti et al.).
- **"The more painful the ordeal a person underwent, the more generously they later gave"** —
  Kavanagh et al. (2018) found no pain–cohesion correlation in a larger sample, and the 2025
  pre-registered Thaipusam study concludes it is estimated *benefits*, not costs, that matter.
- **"Those same forager societies frequently have no moralising high god at all"** — Lightner et al.
  argue the SCCS coding manufactures this result, and Purzycki et al. (2022) find morally-interested
  deities among the Hadza. "Frequently" is doing a lot of work; keep it, but footnote the dispute.
- **"The bonding and the bordering tend to evolve together … two faces of one adaptation"** — Dyble
  (2021) shows the model does not produce this under realistic population structure, and Columbus et
  al. (2023) find in-group love and out-group harm dissociable in experiment.
- **Secular communes "could not earn the same return on strictness" because religious claims cannot
  be disproved** — this is Sosis's stated reason, but Potz (2022) argues the operative variable is
  supernatural monitoring, not unfalsifiability. The essay's conclusion survives; its explanation
  may not.
- **The Vedic "twenty centuries" fidelity figure** — currently traceable only to a faculty news
  page; Staal is `walled`.
- **The Shaker membership figures and the Manichaean extinction** — Britannica-sourced, and note 2.5
  already records that a corpus transcript gave different Shaker numbers.
