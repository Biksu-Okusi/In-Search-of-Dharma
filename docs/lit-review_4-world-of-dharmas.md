### ▲ Substantive challenge found here
**Gade tightens the essay's own shadow into a dating problem.** The essay says the warm picture is
"in part, a story told later" — correct, and Gade is the source. But Gade's specific finding is
sharper: written definitions of ubuntu as a *philosophy/worldview* appear only in the second half
of the twentieth century, and the proverb *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu* is first used **to describe
what ubuntu is** only in **1993–1995** — i.e. contemporaneous with the constitutional moment. The
essay's arc ("a village proverb became a principle of constitutional law") is true of the proverb
but risks implying the *proverb-as-ubuntu-doctrine* is ancient; on the written record, proverb and
constitutional principle arrived together. One clause would fix it.

### ▲ Substantive challenge found here
**"Almost no metaphysical luggage" is contested by the essay's own best sources.** Long's book
argues at length that Epictetus's ethics is grounded in his theology — the dichotomy of control is
underwritten by a providential God who *gave* us prohairesis; SEP's entries say the same for the
school generally ("living according to nature" = according to providential *logos*). Becker's *A
New Stoicism* exists precisely because secularising the system is a substantial reconstruction,
not a shrug. The essay already concedes the metaphysics "sits lightly on the practice", which is
defensible (and Hadot supports practice-first readings) — but the structure is *exactly* the
Sumarah God-floor problem the essay analyses two sections later: a practice whose guardians
insisted the theism was load-bearing, secularised in transit by its modern Western adopters. A
sentence drawing that parallel would turn a vulnerability into one of the essay's best moves.

### ▲ Substantive challenge found here
**The construct-critique is real scholarship, not hedging.** Wolfe (walled) and Swain (lending)
both argue that "the Dreamtime/Dreaming" as a pan-Australian *category* is substantially an
anthropological artefact with a colonial career — a harder version of the essay's own aside that
English "clumsily calls" it the Dreaming. Note 4.3 currently contains no trace of this literature.
The essay survives it (its claims are about *Tjukurrpa*-type orders, not the English label), but
the note should register the critique, and the essay's "the song is the map and the law" sentence
should not be sourced to Bruce Chatwin-derived popularisations anywhere downstream.
