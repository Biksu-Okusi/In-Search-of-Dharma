# 4.1 Samin of Java

**Question:** Explain the origins, beliefs, and dharma of the Samin of Java. How does it
differ from traditional Javanese culture?

## Synthesis

The Samin movement (Saminism; today **Sedulur Sikep**), founded by **Samin Surosentiko** (born
Raden Kohar, 1859–1914) in the teak-forest villages of Blora around 1890, is a near-ideal case
of a **folk dharma**: a culturally-evolved, non-scriptural ethical path — its adherents call it
**Agama Adam**, "the religion of Adam" — that coordinated a peasant community's whole life
around honesty, simplicity, self-sufficiency, non-violence, and the conviction that *land, water
and forest are common property* [S1][S6]. It crystallised as **non-violent resistance** to Dutch
colonial taxation and the enclosure of communal teak forests, but it was never merely a tax
revolt: it was a comprehensive ethical reorientation transmitted *orally* by an illiterate
founder and followers [S2]. It satisfies this project's working definition of a dharma
([[1.3-boundary-definition]]) — a named, identity-constituting, practice-transmitted account of
right living, raised by *reflective, oppositional self-awareness* above the level of inherited
custom [A]. Crucially, it differs from "traditional Javanese culture" **not as an alien import
but as a radical reform from within the peasant (abangan) world**: against the hierarchy Geertz
documented, the Samin deliberately spoke blunt low Javanese (*ngoko*) to everyone — including
officials — refusing the *krama* deference and the *halus* refinement ideal; they rejected both
orthodox Islam and the *priyayi* court order in favour of a "pure Java"; and they replaced
ceremonial elaboration with austerity [S1][S4]. That austerity is sharpest at death: the Sikep
word for dying is *salin sandhangan*, "changing clothes"; the rite is one meal after burial,
with no modin, no qibla and no grave-visiting; and the dead are held to return, by conduct, into
the living [S8][S27][S28]. Bali supplies the counterpart: the Bali Aga villages that refuse the
Majapahit-era cremation — Trunyan exposing its dead under the *taru menyan*, Tenganan and the
north-coast villages burying them the same day — likewise send the dead back into their own
line, but keep the second rite the Sikep discarded [S40][S44][S47]. The same dharma operates
today in the Sedulur Sikep's environmental resistance to limestone mining in the Kendeng
mountains [S6][S17]. Two qualifications now stand on the record from the primary literature: the
pacifism was a majority practice, not an absolute — Korver notes followers "not averse from
using violence against the Government" after 1914 [S3]; and the contemporary movement is a
coalition (JMPPK) in which the Sikep are the moral centre but not the whole body, and in which
some Sikep hold that demonstrating itself contradicts their teaching of *ora pamer suara, ora
pamer tindak* [S17][S18].

## Findings

### Origins: a peasant movement against colonial enclosure
Samin Surosentiko (Raden Kohar) was a Blora farmer, son of **Raden Surowijoyo (Samin Sepuh)** — by
tradition a nobleman from Ponorogo who renounced aristocratic status to live among commoners in
opposition to Dutch rule [S1]. Samin Surosentiko himself was born, on the canonical study's own hedge, "probably in 1859" — the date
is given flat in the Indonesian source and hedged in Benda & Castles [S1][S2]. From around 1890 Samin propagated his teachings in Klopoduwur and
neighbouring villages; the movement spread across the Blora–Rembang–Pati–Kudus–Grobogan–Bojonegoro
arc straddling Central and East Java, reaching perhaps 3,000–5,000 followers by 1907 [S1][S2]. Its
triggers were concrete and material: the Dutch forestry service (Boswezen) declared the Randublatung
teak forests restricted in 1897, cutting off customary access, and colonial taxes (land-rent,
poll-tax, produce-tax) pressed on subsistence farmers [S1][S2]. Samin was arrested in 1907 and banished
with eight followers "to three separate places in the Outer Provinces"; Benda & Castles record
that he "died in exile at Padang, Sumatra, in 1914" [S2] — ▲ the Indonesian video source gives
the place as Sawahlunto [S1]; both are West Sumatra, and the discrepancy is left standing. Followers
held that he had not died but attained *moksha* and entered the spiritual realm [S1][S2]. The
canonical study opens with a colonial official's own admission that reforms could not touch the
movement's "vital core" if it "actually rests on a peculiar mental attitude of its adherents"
[S2] — the colonial file conceding, in its own idiom, that this was an ethic and not a grievance. This is the **moral economy of the
peasant** in James C Scott's sense: a value-system "irrevocably linked to subsistence requirements,"
defending customary rights of access against market-colonial enclosure [S5]. The scholarly
argument over *why people joined* is itself instructive. Victor King's 1973 reply to Benda &
Castles observed that "these economic burdens fail to explain satisfactorily why many of the
poorer peasantry did not join the Samin movement" and proposed Aberle's *relative deprivation* —
"a discrepancy between an anticipated state of affairs, and a less agreeable actuality," covering
"deprivation of status, worth, and behaviour, as well as of possessions" — as the frame [S16];
Korver in turn warned that King's move "may all too easily lead one to suppose that economic
factors such as exploitation and serious poverty" were secondary [S3]. The dispute matters for
this note because a dharma recruits on more than hunger: the Sikep code answered a loss of
standing and self-respect as much as of land [S16][A].

### Beliefs: the Sikep ethical code and "Agama Adam"
Saminist ethics are a compact, this-worldly code rather than a theology. The recurring principles:
**honesty and plain-speaking** (*jujur*); **non-violence**; **self-sufficiency** through one's own
farm labour; **simplicity** and freedom from material excess; not disturbing or harming others;
avoiding envy, greed and quarrels; and the rejection of dishonest gain, especially in trade and
commerce [S1][S8]. Underpinning these is a cosmology in which **land, water and forest are common
property to be used for the common good** — the doctrinal root of both the colonial-era tax/forest
resistance and the present-day ecological struggle [S1][S6]. Their faith, *Agama Adam*, is treated
as humanity's "original religion," with simple prayer at dawn and dusk and no elaborate ritual
apparatus [S1][S2]. What "Adam" means is itself part of the doctrine: Sukolilo informants gloss
it as *pangucap*, utterance — "*Agama niku ageman/gaman/ugeman; Adam pangucap*" — so that the
religion of Adam is "how the utterance is to be practiced in the real life," a faith of keeping
one's word (*ngugemi ucap*) and bettering one's conduct (*mbecikna laku*) [S22]; Rosyid's Kudus
study confirms transmission "secara regenerasi dengan bahasa tutur" (by spoken word across
generations) and notes that the state now files Agama Adam as *penghayat kepercayaan* [S26]. Transmission is **oral**: Samin and his followers were illiterate, and although a few
texts (the *Serat Jamus Kalimosodo*) are attributed to the tradition, the ethic was carried by
recitation, example, and embodied practices such as *laku jejek* — walking on foot for days to
community gatherings as a discipline of continuing Samin's teaching [S1][S2]. Adherents now prefer
**Sedulur Sikep / Wong Sikep** ("honest, responsible kin") to "Samin," which had acquired
connotations of backwardness in mainstream society [S1].

### Why it counts as a *dharma* (not merely custom)
*(This section is the author's own application of the project's criteria, not a claim sourced from
the literature [A].)* Against the criteria of [[1.3-boundary-definition]], Saminism is dharmic rather than mere folkway on
several counts: it offers a *comprehensive* account of how to live (work, speech, property, authority,
death), not a grab-bag of customs; it is *identity-constituting* ("we are the honest people"); it is
*telos-oriented* (an autonomous, truthful, harmonious life on the land); and — decisively — it is
**reflectively and oppositionally self-aware**. Ordinary Javanese village *adat* is inherited custom;
the Samin *self-consciously elevated* a chosen subset of values into a named, principled, defended
path *against* the dominant order. That deliberate elevation, transmitted as identity, is exactly
what converts folkways into a dharma. It also functions as **identity scaffolding and an interface
with power** in the sense developed in [[2.4-transmission-institutionalization]] and
[[7.3-dharma-and-power]] — a dharma defined largely *by* its refusal of the state.

### How it differs from traditional Javanese culture
The contrast is sharpest against the standard anthropological map of Javanese culture — Clifford
Geertz's trichotomy of **abangan** (syncretic peasant), **santri** (observant Muslim), and
**priyayi** (aristocratic-court) streams, and the hierarchical etiquette encoded in the three speech
levels *ngoko* / *madya* / *krama* [S4]. The Samin invert or refuse much of it:
- **Speech-level egalitarianism.** Where status is performed by addressing superiors in deferential
  *krama*, the Samin deliberately used blunt *ngoko* to everyone, officials included — a linguistic
  refusal of the entire hierarchy of deference [S1][S4][S15].
- **Against *halus*, for honesty.** Mainstream Javanese culture prizes the *halus* ideal — refined,
  indirect, emotionally controlled conduct — and ranks blunt directness (*kasar*) as low. The Samin
  made plain, literal honesty an explicit *virtue*, inverting the prestige order [S1][S4].
- **Refusing Islam and the syncretic mainstream.** The nominally Muslim abangan peasantry blends
  Islam with kejawèn mysticism; the Samin rejected orthodox/santri Islam *and* the formal
  religious-administrative order, identifying with a pre-/a-Islamic "pure Java" and leaving the
  religion field blank on their identity cards [S1]. (The nuance: their mystical substrate is
  continuous with heterodox Javanese currents — teachings linked to Sheikh Siti Jenar — so the break
  is with *orthodoxy and institution*, not with all Javanese spirituality [S1].)
- **Simplicity vs. ceremony.** They minimised the elaborate *slametan*/ceremonial life typical of
  Javanese religion in favour of austerity and daily ethics [S4][S8]; the death rite (below) is
  the sharpest case [S27][S28].
- **Refusing the priyayi/state order.** Samin Sepuh's renunciation of nobility, and the movement's
  refusal of tax, corvée, marriage registration and census, reject the aristocratic-bureaucratic
  hierarchy at the heart of traditional Javanese statecraft [S1][S2].

Yet the difference is a **gradient, not a binary**: the Samin are agrarian, mystical, and rooted in
the same abangan peasant world they reform. Saminism is best read as an *internal heresy* of Javanese
culture — using its own materials against its hierarchy — rather than an outside system [A].

### Death and death rites: *salin sandhangan*

The Sikep answer to death is the sharpest specimen of the ceremonial minimalism just described,
and the place where the "not secular" caution in the Tensions section bites hardest [A].

**The word.** The community's own term for dying is *salin sandhangan* (Bojonegoro) or *ganti
sandangan* (Blora, Kudus, Pati): "changing clothes." The 2015 BPNB ethnography of Dusun Jepang
(Margomulyo, Bojonegoro) records its principal informant, the elder Mbah Hardjo Kardi, giving the
term and its gloss: for the Samin the dead person is "mahluk yang sudah tidak bisa komunikasi
lagi," and a death is "peristiwa biasa," handled "dengan tatacara yang sangat sederhana" and
without the display of loss usual outside the community [S28]. Gunretno, at his father's funeral
in Kudus in February 2022: "hidup itu tidak mati, mati itu langgeng cuma ganti pakaiannya"; what
wears out is the body — "Sandangan itu rusak, bukan sandangan ini, melainkan wujudnya raga"
[S33][S34]. The pun runs deeper than a euphemism: *agama* is read as *ageman*, clothing, so a
religion is itself a garment worn in order to become good and taken off at death [S29][S27].

**What survives.** The oldest record is Dangir's interrogation of 1928. Asked the purpose of
becoming *orang sikep*, he gives two: "goodness [kebaikan] when one is alive and goodness after
death." Then: if an *orang sikep* keeps his pledge, "his soul [njawanja] will come to life again
in the form of man"; if he "does not understand the Religion of Sikep well, his soul will enter
into the form of animal or plants" [S8]. Asked about paradise and hell: "An orang sikep does not
believe in the existence of Allah and also the existence of paradise and hell. [He] only believes
forms [woedjoed]" [S8]. Shiraishi's gloss, drawn from the 1955 Gadjah Mada field report, supplies
the concept underneath: *urip* (life), which the *wong sikep* also call *suksma* or *nyawa*, is
"the essence of all the forms of life," and *wong* its highest form — so what changes at death is
the form, not the life [S8].

**Three versions of where it goes.** The sources disagree, and Kristiantoro (citing Samiyono)
says outright that there is no fixed cosmology, "hanya ajaran lisan yang bisa berbeda-beda" [S31]:

- *Transmigration by conduct.* Munawaroh et al: the soul of the good "menitis kembali menjadi
  manusia," the bad "menjadi hewan atau tumbuh-tumbuhan," through seven cycles ("tujuh kali")
  until it becomes "manusia yang sempurna" — and this, the ethnography says, is what the community
  itself calls *salin sandangan* [S28]. Rosyid's Kudus fasting-prayer puts it in verse: "Asale
  wong, bali wong, seng becek kelakuane… Ojo dadi kebo, dadio wong neh"; a fast well kept earns
  "esok surut, sore nanggal" — dead in the morning, reborn in one's own line by evening [S27].
- *Lodging in one's descendants.* Rosyid (Kudus, 2012 and 2023): "Orang yang telah mati, arwahnya
  akan menitis pada generasinya"; good conduct "menempel pada diri generasinya," which is why the
  grave gets no attention [S27][S26]. The death *brokohan* prays that the living stay safe and
  that the dead "menjadi asal manusia yang menitis pada anak cucu menjadi orang yang baik" [S27].
- *Gathering with the living.* Suharyo et al, quoting the Bojonegoro elder Mbah Sampan via
  Mardikantoro: "Wong enom mati uripe titip sing urip… Dadi ora mati nanging kumpul sing urip.
  Apik wong salawase sepisan dadi wong, salawase dadi wong" — a young person's life is entrusted
  to the living; not dead but gathered with the living; once good, a person for ever [S29].
  Gunretno's version: "kalau baik kelakuannya bener ucape asale uwong balek dari uwong bener"
  [S34]. Hardjo Kardi, interviewed in December 2015: "pada dasarnya manusia itu tidak ada yang
  mati. Jiwa masih ada di bumi; yang dibawa orang mati adalah suara, hati, dan amalnya saja" [S30].

Benda & Castles left exactly this open in 1969 — is the conduct-sanction "the Hindu-Buddhist law
of Karma… or is this a prijaji intellectualization?" and "what was the eschatological element in
the 'Religion of Adam'?" [S2]. Lestari et al read the whole complex as a contest of jurisdiction:
Saminism "contested the claims of Islamic authorities to exclusive jurisdiction over matters such
as marriage, birth, and death by affirming the community's right to regulate its own lifecycle
rituals" [S23].

**The founder exception.** The one place the death-talk turns millenarian is Samin himself. In
1914 followers "were expecting Samin's return from exile with a Dutch wife and an army, and would
not believe the report of his death" [S2]; Pa Karsyah in Pati prophesied his return, "whereupon
the world would be divided in two" [S3]; Dangir in 1928 believed Samin had "come back and is now
staying in Batavia close to the Resident's house" [S8]. Benda & Castles suspected that the Dutch
writers "were deceived by their informants, their apprehensions, and even by their own erudition"
[S2]; the later *moksha* gloss is noted in the Origins section above [S1].

**The rite.**

- *No modin.* From the 1950s provincial volumes: "Their birth and circumcision customs were like
  those of their neighbours, but their marriage and burial customs were different… The modin
  (village religious functionary) was given no part in the Saminists' marriages or funerals" [S2].
  Geertz's Modjokuto supplies the abangan parallel of the same decade — Permai members refusing
  the modin at funerals, and the pull back toward "a traditional funeral" under grief, since the
  modin is also the village "undertaker" [S4].
- *Kudus form.* Rosyid's three stages of *merti jenazah*: (i) washed (*diadusi*) "sebagai simbol
  membersihkan pakaian/sandangan (jasad)," or deliberately not washed if the dead "dianggap teguh
  prinsip kesaminan"; (ii) wrapped (*diulesi*) in white cloth or, for some, a *jarit*; (iii)
  buried without Islamic procedure — "mayat tak ditalkin dan tak dihadapkan kiblat" — and without
  Christian dress either [S27]. In summary: "tidak dikafani dengan kain mori, tidak selalu
  dimandikan, tidak disalati, tidak diadzani (talqin) ketika di liang lahat, tidak diberi batu
  nisan, dan tidak diziarahi makamnya"; and "Ajaran Samin tak mengenal ziarah kubur atau khaul
  (ulang tahun wafat) atau slametan pascakematian. Hanya slametan di saat kematian (dino geblak)"
  [S27]. By 2023 "ada pula yang mengadopsi budaya muslim dalam pemulasaraan jenazah, seperti
  dikafani dan diberi kayu nisan" [S26]. Dress for a funeral is the formal Sikep black: *iket*,
  *sruwal*, *tokong* [S26].
- *Bojonegoro form.* Munawaroh et al: one *selametan* or *brokohan* only, "sekali di saat kematian
  (dino geblak) yakni setelah penguburan jenazah," and none of the Javanese seven-, forty- and
  hundred-day cycle; formerly "tanpa dimandikan, tidak disholatkan, dan hanya dikafani dengan kain
  kafan putih 3 lapis, kalau tidak ada dengan kain jarit seadanya," and "tidak memiliki arah
  kiblat"; no *ziarah kubur* — in Ruwah, when the neighbours clean graves and hold *nyadran*,
  Dusun Jepang holds *bersih dusun* instead [S28]. The contrast case is Geertz's abangan cycle:
  slametans "three, seven, forty, and one hundred days after death, on the first and second
  anniversaries of the death, and on the thousandth day" [S4].
- *Body position.* Gunretno: the body lies face up and level, not turned to the west as in Islam;
  in traditional clothes rather than a shroud; in black, "symbolising kelanggengan" [S33][S34].
- *In kind, and together.* Contributions to a death, as to any *hajatan*, are rice, sugar and
  cigarettes, not money; money is handed back [S28][S30]. "Ketika ada khitanan, kawin, kematian
  semua dilakukan dengan bersama-sama" [S32].
- *Home burial.* Mbah Wargono (Mbah Gono), sesepuh of Kaliyoso, Karangrowo, Undaan, Kudus, died on
  25 February 2022 and was buried the next day in the central room of his house [S34][S37]. Kudus
  officials cited a Perda forbidding burial inside houses; the standoff drew the village head,
  Kesbangpol, local NU figures, police and army; Gunretno offered to move the body to his own
  house in Pati; the governor's staff intervened and the burial went ahead [S36][S35]. Suara
  Karya presents home burial as a Samin "keyakinan" [S35]; detik, Jurnal Pantura and Gunretno
  himself present it as his father's *wasiat*: "Pemakaman di rumah ini merupakan permintaan
  terakhir atau wasiat dari Mbah Wargono" [S34][S37]. No ethnography consulted here mentions home
  burial (see Tensions).

**The drift.** The vocabulary survives while the rite converges on Islam. In Dusun Jepang, families
who have taken Islam "menyerahkan sepenuhnya kepada modin": washed, shrouded, *talqin*, faced to
the qibla, and the graves now carry ceramic or cement markers [S28]. Oktafiya, interviewing Hardjo
Kardi in December 2015: "Upacara kematian pada masyarakat Samin sama dengan masyarakat pada
umumnya" [S30]. Mbah Lasiyo, sesepuh of Karangpace, Klopoduwur (Blora), died on 20 November 2025
and was prayed over at the village mosque before burial at Situs Samin Suro Engkrek after Friday
prayers — "Pemakaman memakai tradisi Islam," in his pupil's words, with the Regent among the
mourners [S38]. Mbah Pramugi Prawiro Widjojo, sesepuh of Sambongrejo (Blora), died on 21 February
2026, aged 68, and was buried in the village public cemetery, the mourners in black with *iket*;
the headline still reads *Ganti Sandangan* [S39]. Kudus is the holdout for the non-Islamic form;
Blora's tourism village carries the most Islamised one [A].

For the dharma question this cuts two ways [A]. It is the strongest evidence for the caution
(here and in Part 3) that the *wong sikep* are not secular: the sanction on conduct is
karma-shaped. But the sanction points at the living and the lineage — the good return as people,
into their own descendants, or "gather with the living" — not at another world; Dangir's "only
forms" is as this-worldly as an afterlife doctrine gets.

### The Balinese counterpart: the Bali Aga and the refusal of fire

Java's nearest counterpart to the *wong sikep* sits across the strait. The highland and old
north-coast villages of Bali that outsiders call **Bali Aga**, "mountain Balinese" — a label the
villagers of Trunyan take as a slight, preferring *Bali Mula*, "original Balinese," or their own
*Bali Turunan*, those who "came down" from the sky to the Batur shore [S48] — stand to
Majapahit-derived Hindu Bali much as the Sikep stand to the *priyayi*-Islamic order: an older,
flatter, priestless village world under a cosmopolitan overlay. Colonial scholarship defined
them by refusals. Brandes characterised the Bali Aga "as those who acknowledge neither a
Brahmana priest's authority nor the power of *toya tirta* (holy water)"; Sembiran "is famous for
its (former) megaliths; its (former) practice of exposing the corpses and covering them only
with leaves instead of burying or cremating them; its egalitarian, non-caste social structure;
and its language, which is considered uniquely antique" [S41]. Their village assembly, the *ulu
apad*, is a ladder of married couples ranked by the date of marriage, up which a full member
"rises one step upwards each time a position higher than his own (on his side) is vacated by the
death or retirement of a higher ranking member"; membership ends when the last child marries or
a spouse dies — "if either of the couple die, the survivor must also retire (*balu*, 'to be
widowed')" — and the head elder at the top turns round to face the sea, "in a liminal state at
the boundary between life and afterlife, a boundary which for the old is death as for the young
it is birth" [S43]. Where the Sikep answer to the modin is *no modin*, the Bali Aga answer to
the Brahmana *pedanda* is *no pedanda* — and at the grave, no fire [A].

**What is refused.** The rite they decline is the Balinese cremation, *ngaben*, which
Covarrubias described in 1937 as "the accomplishment of their most sacred duty: the ceremonial
burning of the corpses of the dead to liberate their souls so that they can thus attain the
higher worlds and be free for reincarnation into better beings"; the soul "lingers near the body
when death comes… until liberated by the obliteration of the corpse by the elements." He then
makes the historical judgement the Bali Aga make of themselves: "Cremation rites were probably
not introduced into Bali until the time of Madjapahit, about the thirteenth century, but the
ancient Balinese animists already believed that their life-fluid was immortal and that after
death it returned to animate other beings. They practised the obliteration of the corpse by
burial or, as is still done in the primitive village of Sembiran, simply by abandoning the
bodies in the forest at the edge of a ravine to be eaten by wild animals" [S40]. "Primitive" is
his word, and his book's frame; the observation stands [A].

**Trunyan: exposure, then water.** The ethnography is James Danandjaja's 1980 *Kebudayaan Petani
Desa Trunyan di Bali* [S56], reached here only through what later Indonesian writers relay from
it [S47][S48][S52]. Danandjaja called the practice "exposure"; the name *mepasah* is what
Hindu-Balinese outsiders call it, and he "tidak menemukan istilah lokal di Trunyan" for it
[S47]. The village has three grounds. *Sema Wayah*, reached by boat, holds the exposed dead: on
Danandjaja's account those who died in the ordinary way and were married, or still *teruna* or
*debunga*, or children past *mekutus* (the loss of the milk teeth); the damaged dead — smallpox,
leprosy, an unhealed wound, murder, suicide — are buried in *Sema Bantas*, and infants below
*mekutus* in *Sema Nguda* [S47][S52]. The recent ethnographic study by Jero and colleagues
narrows eligibility to the married: "Only married individuals who die of natural causes are
eligible," those dead by accident (*salah pati*) or suicide (*ulah pati*) being "out of
alignment with the cosmic order" [S44]. The ground has seven plots on the older accounts, two
for village heads and five for commoners [S47][S45], eleven on the newer ones, three for priests
and eight for the rest [S44][S53]; when a plot is needed, the family prays leave of the previous
occupant and the bones are moved aside outside the plot [S47]. The body is laid in a hollow
twenty centimetres deep, open from the chest up, and fenced with a bamboo cone, the *ancak saji*
[S45]; women accompany it only to the lakeshore, and the men do the rest [S44]. The *taru
menyan* — "fragrant tree," the village's name — is credited with taking the smell of decay, and
an elder gave Jero the sentence that carries the whole contrast: "Once the body has fulfilled
its physical function, there is no need for fire, the earth and the tree will take it back
peacefully" [S44].

Exposure does not finish the matter. On the relayed Danandjaja account the soul of a married
person "belum dapat dibebaskan sepenuhnya dari ikatan badan kasarnya" and "belum bisa menuju ke
Dalem (dunia orang mati)"; it must "gentayangan terlebih dulu di sekitar desa" until the family
performs the second rite, the *Pengabenan*, after which it enters Dalem and "bisa menitis
kembali sebagai manusia melalui tubuh anak keturunan mereka yang dalam satu garis kerabat
(dadia)." The unmarried and the children are "dianggap masih suci"; they "bisa serta merta
kembali ke kawah (surga)" and "tidak perlu mengalami reinkarnasi lagi ke bumi (Trunyan)." The
bad dead need a further purification, failing which they haunt "tempat-tempat kotor seperti di
lokasi buang hajat" as *hantu* [S47]. The Trunyan *ngaben* is done without fire. It may be held
only in *Sasih Jiyestha*, the eleventh month, and only by men; the dead are represented by
*prerai*, puppets of sandalwood and of lontar leaf, carried in a two-tiered *wadah* with a
*boma* head; the sandalwood puppet is "exposed" at Sema Wayah as a second burial, the lontar one
taken home to the *amben tengah* and prayed over for a year, and the *wadah* is not burned but
"ditenggelamkan ke Danau Batur" — in Danandjaja's informants' phrase, "Pengabenan dengan Tirta
(air)" [S47][S45]. The end of the road is the lineage: "setiap roh seseorang setelah mati akan
selalu menitis (lahir kembali) kembali ke badan kasar keketurunannya," which Aridiantari and
colleagues give as the reason for *dadia* endogamy [S52]. Danandjaja held *mepasah* to be
pre-Hindu and once shared with Sembiran and Tenganan [S48].

**The burying villages.** Trunyan is the exception that tourism made famous; the Bali Aga norm
is burial, fast and plain. At Sembiran, van der Tuuk "was the first… who noted that the Bali Aga
of Sembiran do not bury their dead but expose them on a plank and leave them to predators"
[S41]; Covarrubias in the 1930s saw the corpse washed, wrapped in new cloth and set on a bamboo
platform at the edge of the ravine with offerings and the dead person's belongings, left three
days, then "thrown unceremoniously into the ravine to be eaten by wild beasts" if the spirits
had not taken it [S40]; Wirz in the 1920s heard the bodies were "just thrown in a gorge to be
eaten by tigers." Hauser-Schäublin corrects the travel-book image: women were laid on their
backs, "identified with the earth," men on their bellies, "identified with the sky"; "The
corpses, though not buried, were carefully disposed of. The practice came to an end in 1963"
[S42]. Sembiran now buries, "together with a few objects, such as clothing, an umbrella, etc.,
that might be useful to the deceased person on her way to the upper world" [S42]. At Pacung,
next door, Drawatik records that "traditionally, cremation was not carried out"; the dead of the
day are buried before sunset, no special day is sought, a body may not wait more than three
days; on the bamboo litter the *mepegat* rite cuts white cotton strings with burning resin "so
that they do not object to letting him or her go"; eleven Chinese coins pay "for the piece of
ground in which the corpse will rest"; personal belongings go in "as a token of their affection
(*tresna asik*)"; "A male corpse is arranged with the face downward and a female corpse with the
face upward," sky and earth; and the soul is later called into a manikin, the *jejeneng*,
purified, and sent on to *embang*, the eternal world, in the *metuun* rite [S42]. Tenganan, the
walled village in the east, keeps "Hindu Dharma Sekte Indra" and buries: "di Desa Tenganan itu
ngabennya dikubur, kalau biasanya kan dibakar"; the burial is on the day of death, "di atas jam
12 siang atau sebelum matahari terbenam," no auspicious day sought, in one of nine graveyards
sorted by the kind of dead — infants, adolescents, the disabled, warriors, the *sanghyang* class
[S49]. The body goes in naked, "sebagai simbol kita lahir tidak membawa apa-apa, dan
meninggalpun tidak bawa apa-apa," and the ground between village and forest carries no
gravestones [S50]; the one covering is a *geringsing* hip sash over the genitals, and the
soul-purification rite, *muhun*, carries an effigy of the dead in a *geringsing* shoulder cloth
[S55]. Julah practises *mekelin*, burial unclothed with the face covered in leaves under a
bamboo *wadah* [S51]. Tigawasa, in the Buleleng hills, has a graveyard of some twelve hectares
that no one may enter except to bury; there is no Pura Dalem or Pura Prajapati beside it, the
grave is dug only when the body arrives, the bamboo and leaves are cut on the spot, and by
village law a body "tidak boleh didiamkan di rumah duka lebih dari 24 jam" — so no one looks for
a *dewasa ayu*; the whole burial uses three offerings where lowland Bali uses dozens [S46].

**Set against the Sikep.** The likenesses are close enough to be structural [A]. Both refuse the
dominant society's rite and its cleric — modin there, *pedanda* here. Both bury fast and without
divination: the Sikep hold one *brokohan* at *dino geblak* [S28], Tigawasa and Tenganan bury the
same day [S46][S49]. Both leave the grave alone: no *ziarah* in Kudus [S27], no entry at
Tigawasa [S46], no stone at Tenganan [S50], the old bones swept aside at Trunyan [S47]. Both
send the dead back into the living line — *arwah menitis pada generasinya* [S27] and *menitis…
ke badan kasar keketurunannya* [S52] are the same sentence in two languages. The differences are
as instructive. The Bali Aga keep what the Sikep stripped out: a second rite that finishes the
soul's passage — the water-*ngaben*, the *metuun*, the *muhun* — and a populated cosmos of
ancestor-gods, Ratu Sakti Pancering Jagat and Ratu Ayu Pingit Dalam Dasar at Trunyan [S47][S48].
Dangir's "only forms" [S8] has no Balinese equivalent; the Bali Aga are not a boundary case for
the secular but a plainly religious people who happen to refuse the religion of the lowlands.
And they have no founder and no doctrine of refusal: Trunyan's origin story explains exposure as
a king's order to mask the tree's scent from outsiders [S47], Tenganan's as the smell of a dead
horse marking the land [S55]; the Bali Aga inherited their difference, the *wong sikep* chose
theirs [A]. Both now receive the same attention from the state and the tourist — a thousand
visitors a day at Trunyan in the 1970s [S48] — that the Kendeng and Klopoduwur sections above
describe for the Sikep.

### The resistance as a dharma in action (the comparative lens)
The Samin's signature tactics — refusing taxes, declining to register marriages, and above all
answering officials with deadpan literalism ("Where are you from?" "From the forest." "Where are you
going?" "To the forest." [S1]; and, in the 1907 interrogation, "My body is my home" [S15]) — are a
paradigm of what Scott calls the **weapons of the weak**:
foot-dragging, feigned ignorance, false compliance, and dissimulation, the "infra-politics" of those
who cannot win open confrontation [S1][S5]. Read this way, the Samin dharma *is* a codified,
moralised, identity-bearing version of everyday peasant resistance — ethics and tactics fused [A].
The non-violence should not be over-stated: Korver records that "whereas the majority of the
Saminists favoured passive resistance methods, there were also some, especially in a later period
after 1914, who were not averse from using violence against the Government," and on that basis
rejects Benda, Castles and King's view that pacifism places the movement outside the millenarian
tradition [S3]. The primary record also has the colonial side reading the 1907 arrests through
"a state of rising messianic expectation which was liable to erupt in violence" — a new era to
begin in the month of Suro, 14 February 1907, when "no taxes would have to be paid and teak wood
could be taken from the forest" [S2] — which is exactly the spy-mediated material Shiraishi later
discounts [S8].

### The modern scholarship: Dangir's voice, the state's labels, and the New Order

The 1969/1976 canon is no longer the end of the scholarship, and the later work changes the
picture in three ways.

**A Samin voice, at last — and a renaming of the whole object.** Takashi Shiraishi unearthed
and translated the minutes of the interrogation of **Dangir**, a ~25-year-old *wong sikep*
peasant of Genengmulyo (Pati), arrested 26 November 1928 for *lijdelijk verzet* (passive
resistance) and examined over the following days by the patih of Pati, Raden Tohar — "the
most informative testimony by a Samin villager" on record [S8]. Dangir describes his own
initiation: taught five walking aphorisms by a local *pitua* (head), beginning "*Mlakoe wahe
sing panteng*" ("Just walk straight on"); made *orang sikep* when he could recite the
self-identification formula ("*Koelo wong, djeneng lanang…*"); required to hold a simple
*slametan* paid **only** with money from his own farm labour, because "traders usually get
profit by cheating, while an *orang sikep* may not cheat"; and bound to a code of no
stealing, cheating, coveting, trading, illicit sex, lying — work by one's own sweat, keep
promises [S8]. Crucially, **Dangir denied being a "Samin" or "Samat" at all**: the words
were the *government's* labels, "meaningless for him"; his self-understanding was *wong
sikep* ("man who embraces"), religion Adam, learned not from Samin Surosentiko (whom he
never met) but from a neighbouring teacher [S8]. Shiraishi's reconsideration follows: much
of "the Samin movement" as an organised, subversive entity is **an artifact of the colonial
file** — officials joining scattered, teacher-centred *wong sikep* circles into a single
remembered conspiracy ("Once Dangir appeared as a Samin in the world of documents, he evoked
all the official memories about Saminism"), with no formal hierarchy, no written texts, and
wide local variation (some circles paid taxes but renamed them *zakat fitrah*, corvée as
*sambatan*, mutual help) [S8]. And the **millenarian material is compromised at the root**:
the prophecies of coming twin kings (Ratu Kembar) that feed the salvation-expectation
reading [S3] reached the record substantially through **spies**, whose reports were "a
fantastic mixture of what spies thought they learned from the Samins they visited and what
they concocted outright to please their masters" [S8]. Shiraishi also recovers a Samin
**doctrine of language**: the words that matter are those about work, marriage and honest
conduct; nearly every other word can be hollowed or repurposed by punning (*agama* = *gaman*,
"weapon"; *pajek*, tax, may mean nothing at all) — "It is man who utters the word, dictates
its meaning, or pronounces its emptiness" [S8]. The famous deadpan literalism is thereby
upgraded from tactic to doctrine: semantic sovereignty as ethics. On mediation, Shiraishi is
exact: the testimony is "doubly alienated from Dangir, his voice overlaid by that of the
patih… But Dangir's voice is still there, however muted" [S8]. He also draws on the 1955
Soehernowo (UGM) field reports — early *Indonesian* scholarship on "wong-sikep-isme,"
itself to be read with the same scepticism [S8].

**The New Order bridge.** Amrih Widodo — the scholar with the longest fieldwork engagement
with the community — documents the middle of the century the older canon leaves blank (▲ his
chapter is consulted here at summary/metadata level, not read in full): under
Suharto's New Order the Sedulur Sikep were pressed to fill the religion column on their
identity cards (Agama Adam being unrecognised), to register their marriages through
mass ceremonies under the religious-affairs apparatus, and were threatened with
transmigration off Java when they refused — the "politics of encounter and isolation"
[S9]. The refusal, in other words, continued essentially unchanged against the
*independent* state, which matters for the note's thesis: the dharma was never merely
anti-colonial; it was a standing refusal of the state's power to name, number and register
its people [S9][A]. The same refusal is visible in the present administrative order:
Sukirno's legal study of local-religion adherents (Sunda Wiwitan and the Sedulur Sikep's Agama
Adam in Java, Parmalim in North Sumatra) finds that even after Constitutional Court decision
97/PUU-XIV/2016 their identity cards carry a "kepercayaan" line rather than a religion, and that
marriage and birth certificates remain hard to obtain because the parents' customary marriage
is unregistered [S25]; the Al-Ihkam family-law study records the community's own view that Samin
law "had existed before Indonesian independence" while "politically, the state does not recognize
the existence of the Samin law" [S14]. Lestari et al's 2026 archival re-reading adds the discursive
layer: colonial authorities "systematically portrayed the Samin community as backward, irrational,
and resistant to progress" — the label the Sedulur Sikep renaming was designed to shed [S23][S1].

**The forestry state, longue durée.** Nancy Lee Peluso's political-economic history of
Javanese state forestry supplies the structural frame the movement-focused canon lacks:
three centuries of escalating state control over the teak forests, colonial "scientific
forestry," and the criminalisation of customary access ("forest crime") that made ordinary
subsistence illegal — the enclosure world within which the 1897 Randublatung restriction
and the Samin response sit [S10]. And the scholarship is now a living, substantially
Indonesian literature: contemporary studies analyse the Kendeng anti-cement movement's
environmental reinterpretation of Sikep teaching from within Indonesian anthropology
[S11], and an active Indonesian-language literature (e.g. Moh Rosyid's studies of Agama
Adam) continues to document the community directly [S12][S26]. Since the 2026-08 corpus
expansion a dozen of these studies are readable in full (see [S16]–[S26]); the picture they
give of the Kendeng years is set out below.

### The hard edges: schooling and gender (the floor-test inputs)

Two features of the Sikep order press directly on this project's "holds without crushing"
floor ([[3.4-relativism-universalism]]), and both are documented rather than speculative.

**Schooling and literacy.** For most of a century the community refused formal schooling for
its children. The refusal was principled and contextual: schools were read as instruments of
the colonial state — "a political tool" imposing an alien culture — and elders forbade formal
education for fear it "would fade their identity and reduce the purity of Sedulur Sikep
teachings" [S13]. The consequence, however contextualised, was that children were denied
letters, in a dharma founded by an illiterate teacher and carried orally [S2]. The arc since
matters as much as the refusal: after independence (from roughly 1945) acceptance came
gradually; from the 2000s the majority of Sikep children complete formal primary and
secondary schooling; and some communities that still decline state schools have built their
**own** "indigenous ecological curriculum" — an owned alternative rather than a bare refusal
[S13]. ▲ Both education studies are consulted at abstract/metadata level, so this whole arc —
the quoted phrases and the "majority since the 2000s" claim alike — rests on abstracts rather
than on the fieldwork itself. Read against the project's floor, this is the rare documented
case of a dharma *adjusting its own hardest edge* without losing its identity [A].

**Gender.** The Sikep cosmology is, at its root, gendered. Dangir's initiation formula
defines husband and wife by reciprocal sexual-marital duty ("my work is *sikep rabi*"; of the
wife, "her work is man"), and the 1955 Soehernowo material renders the "way of man" (*tatane
wong*) as procreation, with the male as planter and the female as bearer, woman assimilated
to earth (*bumi* = *bu* = *ibu*, mother) — the *wong sikep* is "the man who embraces his wife
and land," wife and land in grammatical parallel [S8]. Mukodi and Afid Burhanuddin's fieldwork
among the *wong sikep* of **Kelopo Dhuwur, Blora** reports a correspondingly patriarchal domestic
order, with women "still placed as a sub-ordinate of men" and the community *dalam tataran
tertentu* — "at a certain level" — still displaying a strong patriarchal culture, especially in
matchmaking and marriage [S14]. ▲ That is one village study carrying its own qualifier, not a
finding of Indonesian scholarship generally. The counter-currents are also real: strict lifetime monogamy pledged in the
marriage rite ("*bojo siji kangge saklawase*," one spouse forever) [S14]; and the Kartini
Kendeng, whose leadership of the community's defining modern struggle is female agency
exercised *from within* the tradition's own maternal idiom — enabled and constrained by it
at once [S6][A]. The honest statement: the dharma made room for heroines while prescribing
wives, and no account that celebrates the first may omit the second [A].

### Contemporary register: the Kendeng ecological turn
The dharma persists. Since the 2000s the Sedulur Sikep of the **Kendeng** karst have led non-violent
opposition to limestone quarrying and a **PT Semen Indonesia** cement plant across Rembang, Pati and
Grobogan, on the doctrine that "land, water and forests are common property" and that farming and
protecting the water-bearing karst are sacred duty [S6]. The **"Kartini Kendeng"** — nine women
farmers led by figures such as Sukinah — travelled to Jakarta in **April 2016** and again in **March
2017** and sat outside the Presidential Palace with their **feet encased in cement**, a visceral
emblem of *wong cilik* ("little people") immobilised by the industry; one protester, **Patmi (48)**,
died soon after the 2017 action [S6]. Indonesia's Supreme Court ruled for the farmers in October 2016,
but the governor issued a replacement permit within days and the plant proceeded [S6]. The episode
shows the colonial-era dharma — common-property cosmology, non-violence, refusal of extractive
authority — recoded for an environmental age (→ [[8.1-planetary-dharma]]).

▲ **Coalition context (added 2026-07-31; sourced in full 2026-08-27).** The vehicle of the
Kendeng resistance is **JMPPK** (*Jaringan Masyarakat Peduli Pegunungan Kendeng*), a network of
Kendeng farmers in which the Sedulur Sikep are the symbolically central but **not sole** component.
Zarkasi et al describe the Sikep strategy as one of rallying "allies and supporters with other
parties in order to realize the interests of the JMPPK," naming LBH Semarang, LBH Jakarta, WALHI,
KRuHA, Desantara, KONTRAS and others, with WALHI and LBH Jakarta providing "legal assistance in
court and outside the courts" [S17]; WITNESS's 2023 profile places Sukinah inside JM-PPK, which
"encompasses various groups in Kendeng mountains, mainly Pati and Rembang" [S6]. The English
coverage behind [S6] foregrounds the Sikep. Stage 2 should credit the coalition and treat the
Sikep as its moral centre, not its whole body.

**What the Indonesian scholarship adds.** Five findings from the full-text studies now in the
corpus sharpen the Kendeng picture:
- *The demonstrating self is contested from inside.* A media study of the two documentaries
  *Samin vs Semen* and *Sikep Samin Semen* records that the Sikep interviewed in the second film
  hold that "demo bukanlah ajaran komunitas mereka" — demonstration is not their teaching — since
  the community's rule is *ora pamer suara, ora pamer tindak* (do not show off one's voice or
  one's deeds), "sangat bertentangan" with the protests the first film shows [S18]. The
  feet-in-cement image is therefore a departure for some Sikep as well as an emblem for others.
- *Leadership has changed its basis.* Asrawijaya & Hudayana argue that the open, organised
  opposition depends on the agency of one man, Gunretno, and that where "Samin Surosentiko used a
  power based on charisma and ideology," Gunretno "developed his influence by using social
  capital" — networks, allies, media [S20]. A dharma that once refused organisation now runs on it
  [A].
- *Neither class nor identity politics.* Adam & Bagir's four months with Wong Sikep households in
  Pati conclude that the struggle "can be best understood by inseparability of the politics of
  redistribution and recognition": "the relationship between the political-economic and
  cultural/spiritual dimensions cannot be separated in the peasant identity of Wong Sikep" [S19] —
  the same fusion of ethics and tactics this note reads in the colonial period [A].
- *The maternal idiom is doctrine, not rhetoric.* Putri's narrative study reports that "a woman
  (mother) is a manifestation of earth, the giver, nurture, and life protector," so that "Sedulur
  Sikep believes that women are relatively more affected by any form of environmental degradation"
  [S21]; Candraningrum reads the same cosmology as a "revival of neo-Saminism" in which "humans are
  the earth, and the earth is humans," and records Gunretno's argument that Kendeng's ecology must
  be defended "extraterritorially" because the threats come from outside [S24]. This is the
  source of the Kartini Kendeng's standing — and, as the gender section above notes, of the
  prescribed role that standing rides on [A].
- *The environmental turn is scriptural in the Sikep sense.* Supratikno & Adi's Sukolilo study
  presents Agama Adam — the religion of "keeping the utterance" — as the resource the community
  draws on "to resist the material-based spirit today," specifically the cement industry [S22].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **Doubly-mediated sources (the central caution — now qualified, not removed).** Benda &
  Castles's foundational finding is that Samin and his followers were *illiterate*, so
  "historians and social scientists must work with accounts written about the movement by
  others" — colonial officials and later ethnographers [S2]. The "doctrine" we summarise is
  therefore reconstructed from outsiders, often hostile ones. Dangir's 1928 testimony [S8]
  *thins* this caution without removing it: it is the nearest thing to a Samin first-person
  source, but by Shiraishi's own description it reaches us "doubly alienated," through the
  patih's pen and the file's frame. On top of that, the **corpus's own Samin documents (`The-Samin_sonnet.md`, `The-Samin_gpt45.md`) are
  AI-generated essays written inside *this very project's* dharma frame** (their frontmatter literally
  reads "…Dharma, Human Behavioural Evolution") — so they already assert that Samin "is a dharma."
  Citing them as independent confirmation of that thesis would be circular — and under the
  project's source policy they are now **barred** (`barred:self`: LLM prose may never carry an
  `[S#]`), so they carry no citations here at all. The ethnographic particulars they once framed
  are grounded directly in a genuine translated Indonesian source [S1] and in Benda & Castles,
  read in full text [S2]; the dharma-fit argument is made on this project's own criteria
  ([[1.3-boundary-definition]]), not borrowed from the corpus essays.
- **Contested classification.** Scholars do not agree what the Samin "were": Benda & Castles framed an
  ethical-religious passive-resistance movement [S2]; Korver read it through **millenarism** (salvation
  expectation, the moksha/return-of-Samin motif) [S3]; a Scott-style reading makes it rational
  subsistence-defending peasant resistance [S5]. These lenses pull the "dharma" toward religion,
  prophecy, or class struggle respectively. (Scott never studied the Samin; his frame is applied here
  as analysis, not as his claim.) Shiraishi's archival reading now cuts across all three: "Samin"
  was the state's label for self-named *wong sikep* circles, the "movement" is partly an artifact
  of the colonial file, and the millenarian evidence is partly spy-mediated — which weakens the
  strong Korver reading at its evidentiary root and should temper any confident summary of "Samin
  doctrine," this note's included [S8]. The canon also disagrees about *recruitment*: King's
  relative-deprivation reading against Benda & Castles's economic one, and Korver's warning that
  King discounts poverty too easily [S16][S3]. This note takes King's point (a dharma recruits on
  standing as well as on subsistence) without adjudicating the weights [A].
- **Non-violence is a majority practice, not an absolute.** Korver's record of post-1914 followers
  "not averse from using violence" [S3], and the colonial file's own fear of a violent Suro
  uprising in 1907 [S2], mean that "non-violent" describes the Sikep norm and its dominant
  practice, not every act ever done in the movement's name. The contemporary parallel is the
  Sikep who regard demonstrating itself as contrary to *ora pamer suara* [S18]: the dharma's
  quietism and its public activism are in tension inside the community, not only in the
  scholarship.
- **The bibliography was thin, and has been thickened.** Until July 2026 this note rested on the
  1969/1976 canon plus Geertz — a weakness flagged by the project's own critical review (the
  keystone chapter "resting on a fifty-seven-year-old article"). The modern layer added in
  response [S8]–[S12] is web-sourced; Shiraishi and Benda & Castles [S2] were read in full text, Widodo and Peluso at
  summary/metadata level (flagged per entry), and the Indonesian-language literature is cited
  representatively, not surveyed. **Second pass, 2026-08-27:** the canon (Benda & Castles, King
  1973, Korver, Shiraishi), Geertz, Scott's three books, and eleven Indonesian and international
  studies of the Kendeng years are now in the corpus and cited by chunk [S2]–[S5][S8][S14]
  [S16]–[S26]; Widodo (1997), Peluso (1992), Setiadi et al (2017), Rosyid (2014), Rohman (2010)
  and Fadhilah et al (2024) remain at summary or abstract level. Scott's *Weapons of the Weak*
  is a partial OCR scan (title pages and a handful of pages), so the "weapons of the weak"
  vocabulary is anchored to the book's title page only and its argument to the printed edition.
- **Romanticisation risk.** It is tempting to enlist the Samin retroactively as proto-environmentalist,
  proto-nationalist, or anarchist heroes. Mainstream Indonesian society long stigmatised "Samin" as
  foolish/backward — the very reason for the Sedulur Sikep renaming [S1] — and the movement's own aims
  were local and customary, not programmatic. Reading modern ecological or rights values *into* it
  should be done as interpretation, flagged as such.
- **The gender question was previously unasked.** Earlier versions of this note (and the Part 3
  essay) celebrated the Kartini Kendeng without asking what the Sikep dharma prescribes *for*
  women — a "sympathetic witness" failure flagged by the project's critical review. The
  hard-edges section above now carries the documented answer (a gendered cosmology and a
  patriarchal domestic order, alongside real female agency and strict monogamy), sourced from
  Dangir's testimony at full-text level [S8] and from Indonesian scholarship [S14] — two items of
  which have since been read in full rather than at metadata level, confirming the "subordinate to
  men" wording in its original fieldwork context.
  ▲ **A misattribution lived in this note until 2026-07-28**: the marriage-practices article was
  credited to Amrih Widodo when it is by Arif Rohman, and it was described as sitting "on the same
  terrain" as the gender findings when it is in fact a refutation of sexual rumours about the
  community. Both errors are recorded at [S14]. The residual gap is unchanged in shape but smaller:
  Rohman himself and the 2024 Kudus monogamy study remain unread, and both are browser-only.
- **"Secular?" — a deliberate boundary case.** Saminism is *not* secular: it has *Agama Adam*, an
  eternal soul, and *moksha* [S1]. Its value to a secular-dharma project is precisely as a
  **boundary case** — a *non-scriptural, non-institutional, this-worldly, ethics-first folk dharma*
  showing that "dharma" need be neither a world religion nor a secular philosophy. This honesty
  matters for the project's non-privileging stance.
- **Geertz himself is contested.** The abangan/santri/priyayi trichotomy used as the "traditional
  Javanese culture" baseline has been widely criticised (priyayi is arguably a class not a religious
  type; most Javanese sit in a grey zone) [S7]. The contrast drawn above is therefore against an
  ideal-type, not a uniform reality.
- **Death doctrine: variant, thinly witnessed, and partly seen through Islamic eyes.** Three
  versions sit side by side (transmigration by conduct; lodging in descendants; gathering with the
  living); Kristiantoro says there is no fixed cosmology [S31], and Benda & Castles left the
  eschatology open in 1969 [S2]. The witnesses are few and each has a lens to name: Dangir speaks
  through the patih's pen [S8]; Rosyid is an IAIN Kudus scholar whose frame is a comparison with
  Islam, and who grades Agama Adam "not modern" by Manaf's typology because it "tidak meyakini
  adanya kehidupan kedua" [S26]; the BPNB ethnography is a state cultural-heritage product with one
  principal informant [S28]; the 2022–2026 funerals are journalism, with Gunretno as the main
  voice [S33]–[S39]. Oktafiya's informant also speaks of "jaminan surga" — Islamic idiom absorbed,
  or the interviewer's — a slippage to note [S30]. No Western-academic treatment of Sikep death
  rites was found; King and Korver say nothing about the rite [A].
- **Home burial: custom, or one man's wish.** Only Suara Karya calls burial inside the house a
  Samin belief [S35]; detik and Jurnal Pantura report it as Mbah Wargono's *wasiat* [S34][S37],
  and neither ethnography consulted mentions the practice [S27][S28]. Treat it as uncorroborated
  as a norm.
- **A loose attribution to flag.** Supratikno & Adi attribute to "Castles 1969" the claim that the
  Samin built "a funeral site or graveyard separate with others" [S22]; the Benda & Castles text,
  read in full, says only that the modin had no part in Samin funerals [S2]. Do not repeat the
  separate-graveyard claim on that authority.
- **Serat Jati Sawit.** Kejawen web sources describe a Samin text of that name as teaching
  "kemuliaan hidup sesudah mati"; no scholarly source consulted names it (Rosyid names *Uri-uri
