# 4.1 Samin of Java

**Question:** Explain the origins, beliefs, and dharma of the Samin of Java. How does it
differ from traditional Javanese culture?

## Synthesis

The Samin movement (Saminism; today **Sedulur Sikep**), founded by **Samin Surosentiko**
(born Raden Kohar, 1859–1914) in the teak-forest villages of Blora around 1890, is a near-ideal
case of a **folk dharma**: a culturally-evolved, non-scriptural ethical path — its adherents call
it **Agama Adam**, "the religion of Adam" — that coordinated a peasant community's whole life
around honesty, simplicity, self-sufficiency, non-violence, and the conviction that *land, water
and forest are common property* [S1][S6]. It crystallised as **non-violent resistance** to
Dutch colonial taxation and the enclosure of communal teak forests, but it was never merely a tax
revolt: it was a comprehensive ethical reorientation transmitted *orally* by an illiterate founder
and followers [S2]. It satisfies this project's working definition of a dharma
([[1.3-boundary-definition]]) — a named, identity-constituting, practice-transmitted account of
right living, raised by *reflective, oppositional self-awareness* above the level of inherited
custom [A]. Crucially, it differs from "traditional Javanese culture" **not as an alien import but as a
radical reform from within the peasant (abangan) world**: against the hierarchy Geertz documented,
the Samin deliberately spoke blunt low Javanese (*ngoko*) to everyone — including officials —
refusing the *krama* deference and the *halus* refinement ideal; they rejected both orthodox Islam
and the *priyayi* court order in favour of a "pure Java"; and they replaced ceremonial elaboration
with austerity [S1][S4]. The same dharma operates today in the Sedulur Sikep's environmental
resistance to limestone mining in the Kendeng mountains [S6].

## Findings

### Origins: a peasant movement against colonial enclosure
Samin Surosentiko (Raden Kohar) was a Blora farmer, son of **Raden Surowijoyo (Samin Sepuh)** — by
tradition a nobleman from Ponorogo who renounced aristocratic status to live among commoners in
opposition to Dutch rule [S1]. Samin Surosentiko himself was born, on the canonical study's own hedge, "probably in 1859" — the date
is given flat in the Indonesian source and hedged in Benda & Castles [S1][S2]. From around 1890 Samin propagated his teachings in Klopoduwur and
neighbouring villages; the movement spread across the Blora–Rembang–Pati–Kudus–Grobogan–Bojonegoro
arc straddling Central and East Java, reaching perhaps 3,000–5,000 followers by 1907 [S1][S2]. Its
triggers were concrete and material: the Dutch forestry service (Boswezen) declared the Randublatung
teak forests restricted in 1897, cutting off customary access, and colonial taxes (land-rent,
poll-tax, produce-tax) pressed on subsistence farmers [S1][S2]. Samin was arrested in 1907 and exiled to
Sawahlunto (West Sumatra), where he died in 1914 — though followers held that he had not died but
attained *moksha* and entered the spiritual realm [S1][S2]. This is the **moral economy of the
peasant** in James C Scott's sense: a value-system "irrevocably linked to subsistence requirements,"
defending customary rights of access against market-colonial enclosure [S5].

### Beliefs: the Sikep ethical code and "Agama Adam"
Saminist ethics are a compact, this-worldly code rather than a theology. The recurring principles:
**honesty and plain-speaking** (*jujur*); **non-violence**; **self-sufficiency** through one's own
farm labour; **simplicity** and freedom from material excess; not disturbing or harming others;
avoiding envy, greed and quarrels; and the rejection of dishonest gain, especially in trade and
commerce [S1][S8]. Underpinning these is a cosmology in which **land, water and forest are common
property to be used for the common good** — the doctrinal root of both the colonial-era tax/forest
resistance and the present-day ecological struggle [S1][S6]. Their faith, *Agama Adam*, is treated
as humanity's "original religion," with simple prayer at dawn and dusk and no elaborate ritual
apparatus [S1][S2]. Transmission is **oral**: Samin and his followers were illiterate, and although a few
texts (the *Serat Jamus Kalimosodo*) are attributed to the tradition, the ethic was carried by
recitation, example, and embodied practices such as *laku jejek* — walking on foot for days to
community gatherings as a discipline of continuing Samin's teaching [S1][S2]. Adherents now prefer
**Sedulur Sikep / Wong Sikep** ("honest, responsible kin") to "Samin," which had acquired
connotations of backwardness in mainstream society [S1].

### Why it counts as a *dharma* (not merely custom)
*(This section is the author's own application of the project's criteria, not a claim sourced from
the literature [A].)* Against the criteria of [[1.3-boundary-definition]], Saminism is dharmic rather than mere folkway on
several counts: it offers a *comprehensive* account of how to live (work, speech, property, authority,
death), not a grab-bag of customs; it is *identity-constituting* ("we are the honest people"); it is
*telos-oriented* (an autonomous, truthful, harmonious life on the land); and — decisively — it is
**reflectively and oppositionally self-aware**. Ordinary Javanese village *adat* is inherited custom;
the Samin *self-consciously elevated* a chosen subset of values into a named, principled, defended
path *against* the dominant order. That deliberate elevation, transmitted as identity, is exactly
what converts folkways into a dharma. It also functions as **identity scaffolding and an interface
with power** in the sense developed in [[2.4-transmission-institutionalization]] and
[[7.3-dharma-and-power]] — a dharma defined largely *by* its refusal of the state.

### How it differs from traditional Javanese culture
The contrast is sharpest against the standard anthropological map of Javanese culture — Clifford
Geertz's trichotomy of **abangan** (syncretic peasant), **santri** (observant Muslim), and
**priyayi** (aristocratic-court) streams, and the hierarchical etiquette encoded in the three speech
levels *ngoko* / *madya* / *krama* [S4]. The Samin invert or refuse much of it:
- **Speech-level egalitarianism.** Where status is performed by addressing superiors in deferential
  *krama*, the Samin deliberately used blunt *ngoko* to everyone, officials included — a linguistic
  refusal of the entire hierarchy of deference [S1][S4][S15].
- **Against *halus*, for honesty.** Mainstream Javanese culture prizes the *halus* ideal — refined,
  indirect, emotionally controlled conduct — and ranks blunt directness (*kasar*) as low. The Samin
  made plain, literal honesty an explicit *virtue*, inverting the prestige order [S1][S4].
- **Refusing Islam and the syncretic mainstream.** The nominally Muslim abangan peasantry blends
  Islam with kejawèn mysticism; the Samin rejected orthodox/santri Islam *and* the formal
  religious-administrative order, identifying with a pre-/a-Islamic "pure Java" and leaving the
  religion field blank on their identity cards [S1]. (The nuance: their mystical substrate is
  continuous with heterodox Javanese currents — teachings linked to Sheikh Siti Jenar — so the break
  is with *orthodoxy and institution*, not with all Javanese spirituality [S1].)
- **Simplicity vs. ceremony.** They minimised the elaborate *slametan*/ceremonial life typical of
  Javanese religion in favour of austerity and daily ethics [S4][S8].
- **Refusing the priyayi/state order.** Samin Sepuh's renunciation of nobility, and the movement's
  refusal of tax, corvée, marriage registration and census, reject the aristocratic-bureaucratic
  hierarchy at the heart of traditional Javanese statecraft [S1][S2].

Yet the difference is a **gradient, not a binary**: the Samin are agrarian, mystical, and rooted in
the same abangan peasant world they reform. Saminism is best read as an *internal heresy* of Javanese
culture — using its own materials against its hierarchy — rather than an outside system [A].

### The resistance as a dharma in action (the comparative lens)
The Samin's signature tactics — refusing taxes, declining to register marriages, and above all
answering officials with deadpan literalism ("Where are you from?" "From the forest." "Where are you
going?" "To the forest." [S1]; and, in the 1907 interrogation, "My body is my home" [S15]) — are a
paradigm of what Scott calls the **weapons of the weak**:
foot-dragging, feigned ignorance, false compliance, and dissimulation, the "infra-politics" of those
who cannot win open confrontation [S1][S5]. Read this way, the Samin dharma *is* a codified,
moralised, identity-bearing version of everyday peasant resistance — ethics and tactics fused [A].

### The modern scholarship: Dangir's voice, the state's labels, and the New Order

The 1969/1976 canon is no longer the end of the scholarship, and the later work changes the
picture in three ways.

**A Samin voice, at last — and a renaming of the whole object.** Takashi Shiraishi unearthed
and translated the minutes of the interrogation of **Dangir**, a ~25-year-old *wong sikep*
peasant of Genengmulyo (Pati), arrested 26 November 1928 for *lijdelijk verzet* (passive
resistance) and examined over the following days by the patih of Pati, Raden Tohar — "the
most informative testimony by a Samin villager" on record [S8]. Dangir describes his own
initiation: taught five walking aphorisms by a local *pitua* (head), beginning "*Mlakoe wahe
sing panteng*" ("Just walk straight on"); made *orang sikep* when he could recite the
self-identification formula ("*Koelo wong, djeneng lanang…*"); required to hold a simple
*slametan* paid **only** with money from his own farm labour, because "traders usually get
profit by cheating, while an *orang sikep* may not cheat"; and bound to a code of no
stealing, cheating, coveting, trading, illicit sex, lying — work by one's own sweat, keep
promises [S8]. Crucially, **Dangir denied being a "Samin" or "Samat" at all**: the words
were the *government's* labels, "meaningless for him"; his self-understanding was *wong
sikep* ("man who embraces"), religion Adam, learned not from Samin Surosentiko (whom he
never met) but from a neighbouring teacher [S8]. Shiraishi's reconsideration follows: much
of "the Samin movement" as an organised, subversive entity is **an artifact of the colonial
file** — officials joining scattered, teacher-centred *wong sikep* circles into a single
remembered conspiracy ("Once Dangir appeared as a Samin in the world of documents, he evoked
all the official memories about Saminism"), with no formal hierarchy, no written texts, and
wide local variation (some circles paid taxes but renamed them *zakat fitrah*, corvée as
*sambatan*, mutual help) [S8]. And the **millenarian material is compromised at the root**:
the prophecies of coming twin kings (Ratu Kembar) that feed the salvation-expectation
reading [S3] reached the record substantially through **spies**, whose reports were "a
fantastic mixture of what spies thought they learned from the Samins they visited and what
they concocted outright to please their masters" [S8]. Shiraishi also recovers a Samin
**doctrine of language**: the words that matter are those about work, marriage and honest
conduct; nearly every other word can be hollowed or repurposed by punning (*agama* = *gaman*,
"weapon"; *pajek*, tax, may mean nothing at all) — "It is man who utters the word, dictates
its meaning, or pronounces its emptiness" [S8]. The famous deadpan literalism is thereby
upgraded from tactic to doctrine: semantic sovereignty as ethics. On mediation, Shiraishi is
exact: the testimony is "doubly alienated from Dangir, his voice overlaid by that of the
patih… But Dangir's voice is still there, however muted" [S8]. He also draws on the 1955
Soehernowo (UGM) field reports — early *Indonesian* scholarship on "wong-sikep-isme,"
itself to be read with the same scepticism [S8].

**The New Order bridge.** Amrih Widodo — the scholar with the longest fieldwork engagement
with the community — documents the middle of the century the older canon leaves blank (▲ his
chapter is consulted here at summary/metadata level, not read in full): under
Suharto's New Order the Sedulur Sikep were pressed to fill the religion column on their
identity cards (Agama Adam being unrecognised), to register their marriages through
mass ceremonies under the religious-affairs apparatus, and were threatened with
transmigration off Java when they refused — the "politics of encounter and isolation"
[S9]. The refusal, in other words, continued essentially unchanged against the
*independent* state, which matters for the note's thesis: the dharma was never merely
anti-colonial; it was a standing refusal of the state's power to name, number and register
its people [S9][A].

**The forestry state, longue durée.** Nancy Lee Peluso's political-economic history of
Javanese state forestry supplies the structural frame the movement-focused canon lacks:
three centuries of escalating state control over the teak forests, colonial "scientific
forestry," and the criminalisation of customary access ("forest crime") that made ordinary
subsistence illegal — the enclosure world within which the 1897 Randublatung restriction
and the Samin response sit [S10]. And the scholarship is now a living, substantially
Indonesian literature: contemporary studies analyse the Kendeng anti-cement movement's
environmental reinterpretation of Sikep teaching from within Indonesian anthropology
[S11], and an active Indonesian-language literature (e.g. Moh Rosyid's studies of Agama
Adam) continues to document the community directly [S12].

### The hard edges: schooling and gender (the floor-test inputs)

Two features of the Sikep order press directly on this project's "holds without crushing"
floor ([[3.4-relativism-universalism]]), and both are documented rather than speculative.

**Schooling and literacy.** For most of a century the community refused formal schooling for
its children. The refusal was principled and contextual: schools were read as instruments of
the colonial state — "a political tool" imposing an alien culture — and elders forbade formal
education for fear it "would fade their identity and reduce the purity of Sedulur Sikep
teachings" [S13]. The consequence, however contextualised, was that children were denied
letters, in a dharma founded by an illiterate teacher and carried orally [S2]. The arc since
matters as much as the refusal: after independence (from roughly 1945) acceptance came
gradually; from the 2000s the majority of Sikep children complete formal primary and
secondary schooling; and some communities that still decline state schools have built their
**own** "indigenous ecological curriculum" — an owned alternative rather than a bare refusal
[S13]. ▲ Both education studies are consulted at abstract/metadata level, so this whole arc —
the quoted phrases and the "majority since the 2000s" claim alike — rests on abstracts rather
than on the fieldwork itself. Read against the project's floor, this is the rare documented
case of a dharma *adjusting its own hardest edge* without losing its identity [A].

**Gender.** The Sikep cosmology is, at its root, gendered. Dangir's initiation formula
defines husband and wife by reciprocal sexual-marital duty ("my work is *sikep rabi*"; of the
wife, "her work is man"), and the 1955 Soehernowo material renders the "way of man" (*tatane
wong*) as procreation, with the male as planter and the female as bearer, woman assimilated
to earth (*bumi* = *bu* = *ibu*, mother) — the *wong sikep* is "the man who embraces his wife
and land," wife and land in grammatical parallel [S8]. Mukodi and Afid Burhanuddin's fieldwork
among the *wong sikep* of **Kelopo Dhuwur, Blora** reports a correspondingly patriarchal domestic
order, with women "still placed as a sub-ordinate of men" and the community *dalam tataran
tertentu* — "at a certain level" — still displaying a strong patriarchal culture, especially in
matchmaking and marriage [S14]. ▲ That is one village study carrying its own qualifier, not a
finding of Indonesian scholarship generally. The counter-currents are also real: strict lifetime monogamy pledged in the
marriage rite ("*bojo siji kangge saklawase*," one spouse forever) [S14]; and the Kartini
Kendeng, whose leadership of the community's defining modern struggle is female agency
exercised *from within* the tradition's own maternal idiom — enabled and constrained by it
at once [S6][A]. The honest statement: the dharma made room for heroines while prescribing
wives, and no account that celebrates the first may omit the second [A].

### Contemporary register: the Kendeng ecological turn
The dharma persists. Since the 2000s the Sedulur Sikep of the **Kendeng** karst have led non-violent
opposition to limestone quarrying and a **PT Semen Indonesia** cement plant across Rembang, Pati and
Grobogan, on the doctrine that "land, water and forests are common property" and that farming and
protecting the water-bearing karst are sacred duty [S6]. The **"Kartini Kendeng"** — nine women
farmers led by figures such as Sukinah — travelled to Jakarta in **April 2016** and again in **March
2017** and sat outside the Presidential Palace with their **feet encased in cement**, a visceral
emblem of *wong cilik* ("little people") immobilised by the industry; one protester, **Patmi (48)**,
died soon after the 2017 action [S6]. Indonesia's Supreme Court ruled for the farmers in October 2016,
but the governor issued a replacement permit within days and the plant proceeded [S6]. The episode
shows the colonial-era dharma — common-property cosmology, non-violence, refusal of extractive
authority — recoded for an environmental age (→ [[8.1-planetary-dharma]]).

▲ **Coalition context (added 2026-07-31).** The vehicle of the Kendeng resistance is **JMPPK**
(*Jaringan Masyarakat Peduli Pegunungan Kendeng*), a network of Kendeng farmers in which the
Sedulur Sikep are the symbolically central but **not sole** component, allied with LBH
Semarang/Jakarta, WALHI, KRuHA, KONTRAS and speleological groups (Zarkasi et al. 2021, harvested
at `workshops/DD/sources/3-kendeng/`); a documentary-reception study in the same folder notes that
most demonstrators in the filmed actions were not Samin, and Sukinah is documented as JMPPK. The
English coverage behind [S6] foregrounds the Sikep. Stage 2 should credit the coalition and treat
the Sikep as its moral centre, not its whole body.

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **Doubly-mediated sources (the central caution — now qualified, not removed).** Benda &
  Castles's foundational finding is that Samin and his followers were *illiterate*, so
  "historians and social scientists must work with accounts written about the movement by
  others" — colonial officials and later ethnographers [S2]. The "doctrine" we summarise is
  therefore reconstructed from outsiders, often hostile ones. Dangir's 1928 testimony [S8]
  *thins* this caution without removing it: it is the nearest thing to a Samin first-person
  source, but by Shiraishi's own description it reaches us "doubly alienated," through the
  patih's pen and the file's frame. On top of that, the **corpus's own Samin documents (`The-Samin_sonnet.md`, `The-Samin_gpt45.md`) are
  AI-generated essays written inside *this very project's* dharma frame** (their frontmatter literally
  reads "…Dharma, Human Behavioural Evolution") — so they already assert that Samin "is a dharma."
  Citing them as independent confirmation of that thesis would be circular — and under the
  project's source policy they are now **barred** (`barred:self`: LLM prose may never carry an
  `[S#]`), so they carry no citations here at all. The ethnographic particulars they once framed
  are grounded directly in a genuine translated Indonesian source [S1] and in Benda & Castles,
  read in full text [S2]; the dharma-fit argument is made on this project's own criteria
  ([[1.3-boundary-definition]]), not borrowed from the corpus essays.
- **Contested classification.** Scholars do not agree what the Samin "were": Benda & Castles framed an
  ethical-religious passive-resistance movement [S2]; Korver read it through **millenarism** (salvation
  expectation, the moksha/return-of-Samin motif) [S3]; a Scott-style reading makes it rational
  subsistence-defending peasant resistance [S5]. These lenses pull the "dharma" toward religion,
  prophecy, or class struggle respectively. (Scott never studied the Samin; his frame is applied here
  as analysis, not as his claim.) Shiraishi's archival reading now cuts across all three: "Samin"
  was the state's label for self-named *wong sikep* circles, the "movement" is partly an artifact
  of the colonial file, and the millenarian evidence is partly spy-mediated — which weakens the
  strong Korver reading at its evidentiary root and should temper any confident summary of "Samin
  doctrine," this note's included [S8].
- **The bibliography was thin, and has been thickened.** Until July 2026 this note rested on the
  1969/1976 canon plus Geertz — a weakness flagged by the project's own critical review (the
  keystone chapter "resting on a fifty-seven-year-old article"). The modern layer added in
  response [S8]–[S12] is web-sourced; Shiraishi and Benda & Castles [S2] were read in full text, Widodo and Peluso at
  summary/metadata level (flagged per entry), and the Indonesian-language literature is cited
  representatively, not surveyed. A reader with library access to Widodo (1997) and Peluso (1992)
  in full could still deepen this note.
- **Romanticisation risk.** It is tempting to enlist the Samin retroactively as proto-environmentalist,
  proto-nationalist, or anarchist heroes. Mainstream Indonesian society long stigmatised "Samin" as
  foolish/backward — the very reason for the Sedulur Sikep renaming [S1] — and the movement's own aims
  were local and customary, not programmatic. Reading modern ecological or rights values *into* it
  should be done as interpretation, flagged as such.
- **The gender question was previously unasked.** Earlier versions of this note (and the Part 3
  essay) celebrated the Kartini Kendeng without asking what the Sikep dharma prescribes *for*
  women — a "sympathetic witness" failure flagged by the project's critical review. The
  hard-edges section above now carries the documented answer (a gendered cosmology and a
  patriarchal domestic order, alongside real female agency and strict monogamy), sourced from
  Dangir's testimony at full-text level [S8] and from Indonesian scholarship [S14] — two items of
  which have since been read in full rather than at metadata level, confirming the "subordinate to
  men" wording in its original fieldwork context.
  ▲ **A misattribution lived in this note until 2026-07-28**: the marriage-practices article was
  credited to Amrih Widodo when it is by Arif Rohman, and it was described as sitting "on the same
  terrain" as the gender findings when it is in fact a refutation of sexual rumours about the
  community. Both errors are recorded at [S14]. The residual gap is unchanged in shape but smaller:
  Rohman himself and the 2024 Kudus monogamy study remain unread, and both are browser-only.
- **"Secular?" — a deliberate boundary case.** Saminism is *not* secular: it has *Agama Adam*, an
  eternal soul, and *moksha* [S1]. Its value to a secular-dharma project is precisely as a
  **boundary case** — a *non-scriptural, non-institutional, this-worldly, ethics-first folk dharma*
  showing that "dharma" need be neither a world religion nor a secular philosophy. This honesty
  matters for the project's non-privileging stance.
- **Geertz himself is contested.** The abangan/santri/priyayi trichotomy used as the "traditional
  Javanese culture" baseline has been widely criticised (priyayi is arguably a class not a religious
  type; most Javanese sit in a grey zone) [S7]. The contrast drawn above is therefore against an
  ideal-type, not a uniform reality.
