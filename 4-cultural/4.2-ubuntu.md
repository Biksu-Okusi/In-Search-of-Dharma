# 4.2 Ubuntu

**Question:** Analyze Ubuntu ('I am because we are') as a dharmic framework. How does it differ
from individualistic dharmas while serving similar social functions?

## Synthesis

Ubuntu — from the Nguni maxim *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*, "a person is a person through other
persons" (Sotho-Tswana *motho ke motho ka batho*) — is a Southern African relational ethic best
read as a dharma whose distinctive core is an **ontology of personhood**: one is not born a finished
individual who then enters relationships, but *becomes* a person through community and right conduct
[S1][S4][S5]. As a dharma it does exactly the work every dharma does — coordinating prosocial life,
forming character, grounding dignity, managing the individual/collective tension — but on an
**inverted metaphysics**: where individualistic dharmas begin from a prior, self-contained self and
derive obligations to others, Ubuntu begins from the web of relations and derives the self [S2][S3]
(the "dissolve the dichotomy" strategy of [[3.1-tension-resolution]]). It is not mere sentiment:
Thaddeus Metz has formalised it into a rigorous moral theory — *an action is right insofar as it
respects harmonious relationships in which people identify with, and show solidarity toward, one
another* — capable of grounding human rights and public morality, and built expressly to answer the three
standing objections that Ubuntu is too vague, hostile to individual freedom, and fitted only to
small-scale life [S6]. Its most visible institutional
expression was post-apartheid restorative justice (Tutu's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, whose
founding Act called for "a need for ubuntu but not for victimisation") and
its enshrinement as a constitutional value in *S v Makwanyane* [S1][S8][S12][S13]. But Ubuntu is also heavily
**romanticised**: critics charge that the celebratory "I am because we are" masks a "narrative of
return" to an idealised pre-colonial past, can enforce conformity, and coexists in practice with the
very xenophobia it rhetorically forbids — the dissolution-into-collective failure mode of
[[3.1-tension-resolution]] [S9][S15]. The written record sharpens the charge: the term is attested
in print since 1846 but only as a human quality, and the proverb *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu* was first
used to *define* Ubuntu in 1993–95, the transition years [S10]. The most useful recent move is Sule's
split between *ontological* Ubuntu (the "authentic mode of being African", a pre-modern communal
condition xenophobia has already falsified) and *axiological* Ubuntu (a prescriptive ideal "to which
all humanity ought to aspire") — the dharma survives as the second, not the first [S15].

## Findings

### What Ubuntu is
Ubuntu originates in the Nguni Bantu languages of Southern Africa (notably Zulu and Xhosa), where it
denotes humanness, and is captured in the maxim *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu* — "a person is a person
through other persons" [S1][S7]. The same maxim recurs beyond Nguni — Sotho-Tswana renders it *motho ke motho ka batho* — so the relational-personhood claim is at least not a single-language idiom [S7]. Its popular English gloss, "I am because we are" (often "…and since
we are, therefore I am"), is the formulation crystallised in John Mbiti's account of African
personhood [S11], and has since travelled worldwide [S1][S2]. Gade's survey of the written sources finds 31 texts
using the word before 1950, the oldest a Xhosa Bible portion of 1846, all of them treating *ubuntu*
as a human quality; the broader senses (African humanism, a philosophy, an ethic, a worldview) appear
only in the second half of the twentieth century [S10]. Substantively it names a cluster: interdependence, compassion,
hospitality, mutual recognition, dignity, and collective responsibility for the common good [S1]. One
corpus exposition is careful to say what it is *not*: Ubuntu "is not a political programme" and "not a
religious expression," but a *social awareness* — a consciousness of others (including the non-human
world, via a principle of "co-agency") and of one's responsibilities to them [S2].

### The distinctive core: relational, *achieved* personhood
What most sharply marks Ubuntu off is its claim that personhood is **attained, not given**. Ifeanyi
Menkiti's foundational statement: "personhood is the sort of thing which has to be attained, and is
attained in direct proportion as one participates in communal life through the discharge of the
various obligations defined by one's stations" [S4] — and, in his own paper, that where Western
conceptions "go for what might be described as a minimal definition of the person" (soul, rationality,
will, memory), the African view "reaches instead for what might be described as a maximal definition,"
so that "personhood is something at which individuals could fail, at which they could be competent or
ineffective, better or worse"; the self-identity an individual comes to possess "cannot be made sense
of except by reference to these collective facts" [S5]. The Akan *onnye onipa* and Yoruba *Ki i se
eniyan* ("he or she is not a person") say the same: "an individual can be a human being without being
a person" [S4]. African ethics on this account is "Social, Not Individualistic" — "a *social* morality which is
enjoined by social life itself" — an ethics "of duty, not of rights," and it centres on **character
formed through habituation** from an original moral neutrality rather than on rights asserted by an
autonomous agent [S4]. Tutu gives the lay form: "It is not, 'I think therefore I am.' It says rather:
'I am human because I belong. I participate, I share'" [S12]. Mogobe Ramose develops Ubuntu beyond an ethic into a full ontology — the
ground of an African philosophy in which being is relational and processual [S7]. This is the engine
of the maxim: the self is downstream of the "we."

The web does not end at death. In Mbiti's account the recently dead are the "living-dead," still
persons in the community for as long as someone who knew them remembers them by name, and only
afterwards do they pass into the collective, nameless past [S11]. ▲ Consulted at metadata level
like the rest of S11: the term and its outline are Mbiti's (chapters 3 and 8 of the 1969 book);
no wording-level claim is made here, and the book's Part 4 states it at the same level [A].

### Ubuntu as a dharma (fit to the project's definition)
Against the criteria of [[1.3-boundary-definition]], Ubuntu is dharmic on every count: it offers a
*comprehensive* account of how to live; it is *identity-constituting* (to act with Ubuntu is to *be*
a proper human); it is *character/telos-oriented* (harmony and communal flourishing — for Metz the
*summum bonum* [S6]); it is *transmitted across generations* as a lived, largely oral tradition; and
it is *this-worldly*. Like the Samin ([[4.1-samin-of-java]]) it is a folk dharma carried by practice
and proverb rather than by scripture or church — which is precisely why it qualifies without being
either a world religion or a secular philosophy [A].

### How it differs from individualistic dharmas
The contrast is cleanest against the atomistic self of liberal modernity and the sage-centred
individualism of some Stoic/secular ethics:
- **Ontology — relations-first vs self-first.** Descartes' "I think therefore I am" posits the self
  prior to all relation; Ubuntu's "I am because we are" makes the self *constituted by* relation
  [S2][S3]. Western despotic states, on the Anarkata reading in the corpus, manufactured an "atomized
  individual" whose good is set *against* the collective's; Ubuntu "eschews this false dichotomy"
  [S3].
- **Ethics — harmony vs rights/utility.** The right act *honours* harmonious, friendly relationships
  (identity + solidarity), not the maximal autonomy or aggregate utility of separate agents [S6]. Metz
  is explicit that the theory must be deontological rather than a bare instruction to *promote*
  community — a promotion principle "would permit a doctor to kill an innocent, relatively healthy
  individual and distribute her harvested organs to three others," so "certain ways of treating
  individuals are considered wrong… 'in themselves'"; the opaque maxim then unpacks as "One becomes a
  moral person insofar as one honours communal relationships" [S6].
- **Personhood — achieved vs inherent.** Dignity is earned and relational, not a pre-loaded property
  of the lone individual [S4][S5].
- **The individuality/individualism cut.** Ubuntu does *not* simply erase the individual: a corpus
  source distinguishes *individuality* (independence, personal character — affirmed) from
  *individualism* (self-referential ego — moderated by community) [S2], echoing Gyekye's defence
  (below).

### …while serving the same social functions
For all the metaphysical inversion, Ubuntu performs the universal dharmic functions: it **secures
cooperation** (and, tellingly, even critics grant that its pro-social features are "neither unique nor
indigenous to Africa" — i.e. a culturally specific instance of the universal cooperative substrate of
[[1.2-universal-patterns]], not a sui generis essence) [S9]; it **forms moral character**; it
**confers belonging and identity**; it **manages the individual–collective tension** (the relational
strategy of [[3.1-tension-resolution]]); and it **grounds a public, juridical morality**. The last is
concrete: Ubuntu underwrote the TRC's restorative-justice approach — confession and reintegration
over retribution [S1][S2] — and South Africa's Constitutional Court invoked Ubuntu as a foundational
value in *S v Makwanyane* (1995), the judgment abolishing the death penalty in the name of life and
dignity [S8]. Metz argues Ubuntu can therefore ground human rights *communally*: "typical human beings have a
dignity by virtue of their capacity for community," and the derivation "did not appeal to the Kantian
notion of autonomy; the invocation of our communal nature did the work" — a different route to the
same protective floor that individualist liberalism reaches via the autonomous individual [S6]
(cf. [[3.4-relativism-universalism]]). The Commission's own report describes the mechanism from the
inside: restorative justice "does not seek revenge, nor does it seek impunity"; to see "both the deed
and the doer and sever them from each other" is "part of the spirit of ubuntu," and dignity "cannot be
bestowed on someone… It can only be acknowledged" [S14]. Tutu adds the admission the celebratory
literature omits — "Where was ubuntu then?" of Rwanda and the Congo; it "clearly is not a mechanical,
automatic, and inevitable process" [S12].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The corpus was celebratory; the critique is now in it.** At the first pass (2026-07) the
  retrieved corpus material was mostly admiring — transcripts of talks, an NGO profile, even an Ubuntu
  *song* ("Tell all the people near and far: I am because we are") — and the philosophical spine and
  *all* the sceptical literature had to be imported. Since the 2026-08 expansion the `1-ubuntu/` shelf
  holds Menkiti, Metz 2011, Gade, the SEP entry, Tutu, both TRC volumes and Sule's xenophobia paper in
  full, and the note now cites them by chunk [S4][S5][S6][S10][S12][S13][S14][S15]. Still external:
  Ramose, Mbiti, Gyekye's reply, Matolino & Kwindingwi 2013 and Metz 2014, and the *Makwanyane*
  judgment itself (in corpus only as Metz's footnote to Mokgoro J, paras 309–311 [S6]). The one
  internal counter-voice of the first pass — an Anarkata text quoting **Nkrumah's caution** against
  romanticising pre-colonial communalism [S3] — is now joined by Sule, and by Tutu's own "Where was
  ubuntu then?" [S12]. Tutu and Menkiti are OCR scans; quotations were checked against the chunks.
- **Romanticisation / "the End of Ubuntu."** Matolino & Kwindingwi argue Ubuntu functions as a
  political-elite "narrative of return" to a harmonious pre-colonial past, an anachronism against
  modern, urbanised, unequal African societies that can *mask structural inequality* and *silence
  dissent in the name of harmony* [S9]. Christian Gade's conceptual history sharpens this: the *term* is old (1846) but the *doctrine* is
  not — the broad definitions are post-1950 and the proverb was first used to define Ubuntu only in
  1993–95, and "most authors today refer to the proverb" [S10]. That the definition arrived with the
  democratic transition is consistent with the elite "narrative of return" reading, though Gade does not
  draw that inference and neither should Stage 2 without saying so [A]. (Metz replies that
  Ubuntu-as-moral-theory survives even if the historical golden-age story is myth [S9]; Sule reaches
  the same place by a different cut — reject *ontological* Ubuntu, keep *axiological* [S15].)
- **The dark side — conformity, gerontocracy, xenophobia.** Critics note Ubuntu communities can be
  "notorious for disliking outsiders… intolerant of divergent ideas," privileging blood relations
  [S9]; the "older = more a person" logic [S5] can license gerontocracy and patriarchy; and the
  bitterest irony is documented **xenophobic violence** in Ubuntu-professing South Africa against
  other African migrants — for Sule the fact that "greatly contradict[s] the principal canons" of
  ontological Ubuntu and shows the communal/individual divide to be "reflective of the stage of
  development on each side," not of a distinctive African essence [S15]. ▲ Sule's paper (Acta
  Academica, 2022) is *not* by Matolino, as an earlier draft of this note and the register implied; it
  agrees with Matolino & Kwindingwi that Ubuntu's features are "neither unique nor indigenous to
  Africa" but calls their bankruptcy verdict "quite unconvincing" [S15]. This is the in-group/out-group
  failure mode of [[3.1-tension-resolution]] and seeds [[7.1-failure-modes]] and [[7.3-dharma-and-power]].
- **Radical vs moderate communitarianism (does it erase the individual?).** Gyekye rejects Menkiti's
  *radical* communitarianism — that the community wholly defines the person — as having "insurmountable
  problems," defending a *moderate* communitarianism in which rationality, virtue, moral judgement and
  **individual choice/rights** also constitute personhood [S5][S6]. Which Ubuntu one means decides
  whether it protects or swallows the dissenter.
- **Appropriation/flattening.** Ubuntu has been globally marketed (leadership consultancy, even a
  Linux distribution), a translation risk to be examined in [[7.2-appropriation]].
