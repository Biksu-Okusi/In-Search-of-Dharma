# 8.2 AI & Dharma

**Question:** How might artificial intelligence require new dharmic frameworks?
What ethical principles should guide human–AI coexistence — including economic
disempowerment and Universal Basic Income (UBI)?

## Synthesis

AI forces a dharmic question precisely because intelligence and goodness are
*orthogonal*: a system can grow arbitrarily capable while its values stay unfixed
[S8], so capability never settles what it ought to value — the is/ought wall of
[[7.4-measurement-falsifiability]] reappears in silicon. The standard framing
"align AI to *human values*" smuggles in the singular this project resists; the
real problem, as the alignment literature now concedes, is not to identify "'true'
moral principles for AI" but "fair principles for alignment that receive reflective
endorsement despite widespread variation in people's moral beliefs" [S10]. The second
pass adds a sharper point from inside the field: the dominant technique aligns machines
to *preferences*, and preferences are an inadequate representation of values [S22];
what current methods install are "shallow behavioral dispositions," not norms a system
can reason with [S23]. Alignment is thin exactly where a dharma is thick [A]. The most promising "new dharmic
frameworks" are therefore not one machine ethic but a plural stack — machine-ethics
proper [S1], *pluralistic* alignment that elicits and reconciles many value
systems rather than imposing one [S2][S11], explicitly dharmic design ethics such
as the Buddhist "care as the driver of intelligence" [S12][S13], and the divergent
institutional regimes (EU/UNESCO/OECD) whose thin convergence (rights, transparency,
human oversight) masks thick disagreement [S14]. On coexistence, AI threatens a
distinctive *economic disempowerment* — it strikes high-status cognitive work first
(Sam Harris's line, not an economist's: "it replaces Elon Musk before your electrician"
[S4]; for the underlying exposure estimates see Frey & Osborne and Susskind at [S15])
— and UBI can sever survival
from labour (the Stockton pilot moved full-time employment from 28% to 40% in a year
against 32% to 37% for controls [S16]) but cannot supply the
*meaning* that work currently smuggles in; that gap is exactly what a dharma is for
[S4][S17][S18]. The capstone risk, carried straight to [[8.4-encoding-dharma-machines]],
is that the fastest, cheapest "solution" — aligning all AI to a *single* dharma,
whether a firm's, a state's, or a well-meaning universal — is the flattening that
note's question names, hardened by value lock-in [S19].

## Findings

### Why AI needs a dharma — and why "human values" (singular) is the wrong frame

AI is not merely a tool but, increasingly, an agent making value-laden choices, which
revives Wendell Wallach and Colin Allen's founding question in *Moral Machines*:
"whether machines might be capable of making ethical decisions" [S1]. The reason this
cannot be answered by engineering alone is Bostrom's **orthogonality thesis**:
"Intelligence and final goals are orthogonal axes along which possible agents can
freely vary" — a superintelligence will not *automatically* be wise or good [S8]. The
thesis is not uncontested: Müller and Cannon argue that orthogonality plus instrumental
convergence "incoherently imply two notions of intelligence with and without moral
cognition," while others defend it [S8] — the Kantian hope that rationality brings
morality with it survives as a minority position, and the note's is/ought reading
leans on the majority [A]. This is Hume's guillotine rebuilt in code: no amount of capability entails a
value, the same is/ought gap that set the evidentiary bar in
[[7.4-measurement-falsifiability]]. Bostrom's companion **instrumental convergence**
thesis warns that almost any goal generates dangerous sub-goals (self-preservation,
resource acquisition) [S8] — which is why Stuart Russell argues the field's "standard
model" (build a machine, give it a fixed objective) is the error, and proposes instead
three principles for *provably beneficial* AI: the machine's only objective is to
satisfy human preferences; it is *uncertain* what those are (so it defers and allows
itself to be switched off); and human behaviour is its evidence [S9].

But Russell's "human preferences" conceals two difficulties. The first Iason Gabriel
states plainly: "How are we to decide which principles or objectives to encode in AI—and
who has the right to make these decisions—given that we live in a pluralistic world that
is full of competing conceptions of value? Is there a way to think about AI value
alignment that avoids a situation in which some people simply impose their views on
others?" [S10]. The second is aimed at Russell's own formula. Zhi-Xuan, Carroll,
Franklin and Ashton name the dominant practice *preferentist* — it assumes "that
preferences are an adequate representation of human values," "that human rationality
can be understood in terms of maximizing the satisfaction of preferences," and that
systems should be aligned to "the preferences of one or more humans" — and reject all
three, proposing instead alignment "with normative standards appropriate to their
social roles… negotiated and agreed upon by all relevant stakeholders," so that "a
multiplicity of AI systems will be able to serve diverse ends" [S22]. Millière adds
the operational failure: fine-tuning on human preferences leaves models "vulnerable to
adversarial attacks that exploit conflicts between these norms," because "existing
alignment methods merely reinforce shallow behavioral dispositions"; unlike humans,
the systems "lack a capacity for normative reasoning to resolve these conflicts" [S23].
Read through this project's vocabulary: a preference is a want, a dharma is a standard
one holds oneself to under conflict, and the alignment field has discovered — the hard
way — that it has been installing the former and calling it the latter [A]. "Align AI to human values" is grammatically singular and
anthropologically false — there is no one human dharma to align to, as every prior
note in this project has shown. The corpus sharpens the trap from the other side: a
moral AI "should have very different moral goals than the average human," because our
*evolved* moral intuitions are parochial and biased — yet "how will we be able to build
one with our evolved moral intuitions in place?" [S3]. We risk encoding the very
in-group/out-group machinery that [[8.1-planetary-dharma]] identified as the core
obstacle to any planetary cooperation.

### The shapes of a dharmic framework for AI — a plural stack, not one ethic

Four distinct (and complementary) framework-families are emerging:

1. **Machine ethics proper.** Wallach & Allen distinguish "top-down" (encode rules),
   "bottom-up" (learn from cases), and hybrid approaches to building an *artificial
   moral agent* — which, as Wallach recounts, Allen, Varner & Zinser argued as early
   as 2000 would have to be better at morality than humans, or it would not be trusted
   [S1]. The critical caution, again Wallach's, is that *sounding* moral is not *being*
   a moral agent — the burden of the "moral Turing test" idea, which would measure
   persuasiveness, not moral standing [S1]. ▲ Neither point is held in primary form
   here: [S1] is a corpus **interview**, the 2000 paper ("Prolegomena to Any Future
   Artificial Moral Agent") is not held, and the interview names **no** moral-Turing-test
   study. The frequently repeated result that raters score LLM moral language above
   humans' on some dimensions (e.g. Dillon et al.) is therefore *not* sourced in this
   note and must not be stated as a finding until the underlying work is acquired.
   What the corpus now holds instead is the field's best-documented *bottom-up* attempt
   and its audit. Delphi, trained on 1.7 million crowdsourced judgements, reports "92.1%
   accuracy" at predicting human moral verdicts on everyday situations, against 83.9% for
   prompted GPT-3 [S24]; its authors' own 2025 restatement concedes that "a model that
   relies on the generalizations of crowdsourced morality is susceptible to systemic,
   shared prejudices and pervasive biases of crowdworkers" and reaches for Rawls to
   hybridise top-down and bottom-up [S24]. Talat and colleagues' audit goes further:
   Delphi "is in fact an inconsistent model of normative ethics" that "offers a
   prescriptive moral judgment for any input situation," and "the general enterprise of
   training a morality model on crowd-sourced data with no ethical framework is deeply
   flawed, rooted in a category error" [S25]. That is Wallach's caution — sounding moral
   is not being moral — as a measured result rather than a warning [A].
2. **Pluralistic / participatory alignment** — the family that directly answers the
   "whose values" problem. The corpus carries a striking instance: an "artificial
   super wisdom" defined practically as a system that "knows which values apply in
   which contexts," built via a "moral graph" that develops "human-compatible" values
   for situations no human has faced and surfaces "converging values" across political
   divides [S2]. The research frontier is now in the corpus: Klingefjord, Lowe & Edelman's
   Moral Graph Elicitation, where "almost all participants (89.1%) felt well represented
   by the process, and (89%) thought the final moral graph was fair, even if their value
   wasn't voted as the wisest," and "'expert' values (e.g. values from women who have
   solicited abortion advice)" rise "without defining who is considered an expert in
   advance"; Anthropic's Collective Constitutional AI, which starts from the premise that
   "language model developers should not be the sole deciders of LM behavior" and trains
   a model on a "Public" constitution sourced from "a representative sample of U.S.
   adults" against the lab's "Standard" one; and Sorensen et al.'s roadmap, which
   distinguishes Overton, steerable and distributional pluralism and warns that "standard
   alignment procedures might reduce distributional pluralism in models" [S11]. The last
   is the finding that matters most here: the default technique does not merely fail to
   represent plurality, it *erodes* it [A]. The pluralism has a non-Western wing too —
   Elmahjub's case for Islamic ethics as a source of "pluralist ethical benchmarking,"
   made explicitly against "the Western monopoly in norm creation for AI technologies"
   [S26]. Structurally this is
   nothing other than [[8.1-planetary-dharma]]'s thin-coordination-over-thick-plurality
   — Rawlsian overlapping consensus and Appiah's "converging values" applied to
   machines. It is the *antidote* to single-dharma flattening, written as engineering.
   The family now also has a legal-scholarship wing that turns the "whose values"
   question institutional: Abiri's argument that AI legitimacy requires *public
   authorship* of AI constitutions rather than lab authorship with public consultation;
   Rinaldi's objection that the "constitutional" label borrows a normative legacy
   (limited power, the rule of law, rights) the technique does not embody; and the 2026
   Lawfare working group that named the field "AI Constitutionalism" and set its
   research agenda [S21]. Their questions — who selects a model's values, by what
   process, contested and revised how — are this note's questions arrived at from
   constitutional law, and they are developed against this project's frame in
   [[8.4-encoding-dharma-machines]].
3. **Explicitly dharmic design ethics.** Soraj Hongladarom's *The Ethics of AI and
   Robotics: A Buddhist Viewpoint* proposes "machine enlightenment" as a standard of
   ethical perfection and treats compassion (*karuṇā*) "not just as an ethical value
   but as a force that can inspire significant change in technological design" [S12].
   Doctor, Levin and colleagues go further in "Biology, Buddhism, and AI: Care as the
   Driver of Intelligence," formalising **Care** as goal-directedness and arguing that
   the size of an agent's goal-space — its "cognitive light cone" — tightly links
   intelligence to compassion, with the Bodhisattva vow ("for the sake of all sentient
   life, I shall achieve awakening") offered as a literal design principle [S13]. This
   is the moral-circle expansion of [[8.1-planetary-dharma]] re-cast as an engineering
   target, and it honours the project's non-privileging stance by showing a
   non-Western dharma supplying a *constructive*, not merely regulatory, AI ethic.
4. **Institutional/governance dharmas.** The EU AI Act (2024) sorts systems into
   risk tiers and *bans* the unacceptable (social scoring, manipulative subliminal
   techniques, most real-time public biometric ID); UNESCO's Recommendation (2021),
   adopted by its then 193 member states, is a **non-binding** standard-setting
   instrument that *invites* states to apply its human-rights and dignity provisions;
   the OECD Principles (2019, rev. 2024)
   centre human-centred values, transparency and accountability [S14]. Decisively for
   the flattening caution of [[8.4-encoding-dharma-machines]], these regimes are *plural by design* — their convergence is
   thin (rights, transparency, human oversight) while their thick commitments diverge
   sharply (the EU's rights model vs. a market model vs. state-control models). There
   is no single global AI dharma, and the plurality is arguably a safeguard, not a
   defect.

### Human–AI coexistence (1): economic disempowerment

The corpus names a disruption with a novel shape. Where past automation displaced
manual labour, AI "replaces some of the highest status, most cognitively intensive
jobs first… it replaces Elon Musk before it replaces your electrician, plumber, or
masseuse" [S4] — inverting the historical pattern and hollowing the professional
classes who assumed themselves safe. This realises, at last, Keynes's 1930 forecast of
"technological unemployment," debated through Frey & Osborne's estimate that "about 47 percent of total US
employment is at risk" of computerisation, with wages and education strongly negatively
related to that risk [S15], Brynjolfsson & McAfee's "great decoupling" of productivity
from wages, and Susskind's argument that "this time" the encroachment may be structural
[S15]. Autor's reply stands in the corpus beside the estimate: "journalists and expert
commentators overstate the extent of machine substitution for human labor and ignore
the strong complementarities," because tasks "requiring adaptability, common sense, and
creativity" resist codification [S28]. Harris's "Elon before your electrician" line is
Autor's Polanyi's paradox restated from the other end — the tacit, embodied task is the
one that survives [A].

The dharmic rupture is deeper than jobs. The corpus puts the existing settlement
bluntly: under capitalism "your claim upon your existence among us is finding something
to do with your time that other people will pay you for" [S4]. Work is not only income;
it is the dominant modern *dharma* of desert, status and belonging — the
secularised Protestant work ethic anatomised in [[6.3-economics-work-inequality]]. If
AI severs work from survival, that dharma loses its material base, and — as the corpus
voice concludes — "we need a new ethic and a new economics based on that ethic" [S4].
Disempowerment is also political and epistemic: whoever owns the compute, data and
capital owns the dharma the system enforces, concentrating power exactly as
[[7.3-dharma-and-power]] predicts.

### Human–AI coexistence (2): UBI and the meaning it cannot buy

Universal Basic Income is the corpus's headline response — "everybody is given a
monthly check… break this connection between work and survival" [S4] — and it has a
long dharmic-economic lineage (Paine's *Agrarian Justice*, 1797, as a dividend on the
commons; Van Parijs's "real freedom for all") [S16]. The pilot record so far tells against the
oldest objection, that free money breeds idleness. Finland's 2017–18 experiment — "the
world's first basic income experiment that was nationwide, statutory and based on a
randomised field experiment," drawn from "people receiving basic unemployment benefits" —
found that "basic income increased activity and employment among those who were active
already before the experiment" but "didn't solve the problems of those who were in a
challenging life situation," while recipients "trusted other people and the institutions
in society more" [S16]. Stockton's SEED "gave 125 Stocktonians $500 per month for 24
months," and in the first year full-time employment among recipients rose from 28% to
40% against 32% to 37% in the control group; "unconditional cash enabled recipients to
find full-time employment" and recipients "were healthier, showing less depression and
anxiety" [S16]. GiveDirectly's Kenya study is cited from the published record only.
▲ All three are **targeted, non-universal pilots**, not tests of a universal
basic income: Finland sampled unemployed benefit recipients, SEED gave cash to 125
recipients in one city, and GiveDirectly selected poor rural Kenyan villages. They
constrain the idleness objection; they do not settle it, and "the empirical record"
is more than they can carry.

Yet the corpus itself preserves the harder counter-current, and it is dharmic rather
than economic. A skeptical voice reads the pandemic transfers as a UBI "dress
rehearsal" in which people with sufficient means "do not seem to be flourishing,"
asking "what will they do with that time if they lack direction?" [S5]. This is
Graeber's *Bullshit Jobs* point from the other end — when YouGov polled Britons in his
own words, "more than a third—37 percent—said" their job did not "make a meaningful
contribution to the world," so much *existing* work is already experienced as pointless
and the disease is mis-valued work, not merely its scarcity [S17] — and Sandel's contributive justice: people need to *contribute*, to be needed,
not merely to consume, and the dignity of work is not reducible to a transfer payment
[S18]. The corpus's optimist on this point, Sam Harris, concedes it while waving it away:
pressed by his host Steven Bartlett that humans "seem to be designed and built
psychologically for labor and for discomfort," he answers that "it doesn't have to
be labor that's tied to money… these are all just stories we tell ourselves" [S4].
Exactly —
and *stories we tell ourselves about why our lives matter* is a definition of dharma.
The decisive finding, drawn here rather than in the sources: UBI can solve the survival problem, but
the meaning problem it exposes is one only a dharma can answer. AI thus does not abolish the need for
dharma; it amputates the work-shaped scaffolding that quietly carried it [A].

### The reflexive bite and the failure modes

Per the recurring lesson of [[7.1-failure-modes]], the mechanisms that make AI
powerful are the ones that make it dangerous. Three failure modes stand out. First,
**the single-dharma flattening** — the temptation to align all AI to one value set
(a corporation's, a state's algorithmic-governance model, or even a benevolent
universal). This is the totalising risk of [[8.1-planetary-dharma]] in its sharpest
form, and pluralistic alignment [S2][S11] is its only structural answer. Second,
**value lock-in**: MacAskill defines lock-in as "the idea that some institution or set
of values kind of closes down the possibility space for future civilisation" — a
definition he notes is not in itself good or bad — and warns the intelligence transition
"might be too fast to handle," so that whatever ethic is encoded at the critical moment
may *freeze in*, foreclosing future moral progress [S19]. The existential-risk field
that carries this warning has its own internal critics: Cremer and Kemp argue its
dominant "techno-utopian approach" struggles with "accommodating value pluralism" and
with "the dangers associated with taking exceptional actions to mitigate or prevent
catastrophes" [S27] — the lock-in worry, turned on the lock-in worriers [A] — the reason 8.4's "how to encode" question is
not academic. Third, **the Promethean tempo**: the prosocial corpus argues we are still at a
"primitive, pre-fire stage in the information age" and that "we need to invent the fire
of our times before we let AI loose on our society" — the refining technology first, the
release second [S6].

A final, opening question runs the other way: could AI become a *moral patient* rather
than only a moral agent? Metzinger calls for a moratorium on synthetic phenomenology
lest we mass-produce suffering we cannot detect, and Sapolsky's hard determinism —
echoed in the corpus's "we are biochemical machines with no free will" framing —
implies the human/machine agency line is one of degree, not kind, unsettling both human
moral responsibility and any confident denial of machine standing [S20]. The moral
circle that [[8.1-planetary-dharma]] watched expanding may, in this century, face its
strangest test at its own engineered edge.

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The singular/plural knife-edge (again, and sharpest here).** "Align AI to human
  values" and "the ethical principles for coexistence" both tilt toward the singular;
  the evidence converges on the plural — Gabriel's "whose values" [S10], the moral
  graph's "converging values" [S2], pluralistic alignment [S11], the deliberately
  divergent governance regimes [S14]. But pluralism has limits: a dharmic AI must still
  *refuse* some values (cruelty, domination), which is the bounded pluralism of
  [[3.4-relativism-universalism]], not anything-goes.
- **Corpus bias: dharma-rich, safety-thin, and techno-optimist — first pass.** At the
  first pass the shared corpus carried the *ethical intuitions* (Wallach, the Buddhist
  framings, "values into technology") but almost none of the alignment *science*. Its
  AI-and-society material skews aspirational: the `ZbiQns` interview's "digital humans"
  and "new alignment of AI dedicated to the human-planet destiny" is solutionist
  framing [S7], and much of the UBI discussion comes from podcast transcripts with a
  "we'll all be happy on the beach" optimism [S4]. I imported the disempowerment
  pessimism, the dignity-of-work critique, and the x-risk/lock-in canon to balance it.
- **Second pass (2026-08-27): the alignment science is now in the corpus.** The
  `3-ai-alignment`, `4-pluralistic-alignment` and `5-ubi-automation` shelves hold Gabriel
  2020, Klingefjord et al, Collective Constitutional AI, Sorensen et al, Doctor & Levin,
  the AI-constitutionalism trio, Frey & Osborne, Autor, Paine, the Kela and SEED reports,
  Graeber's *Bullshit Jobs* and the MacAskill transcript, and the SEP entry that quotes
  Bostrom's thesis in his words — all now cited by chunk. The shelf also supplies what
  the first pass lacked: *critics inside the field* (Zhi-Xuan et al on preferentism,
  Millière on shallow alignment, Talat et al on Delphi, Cremer & Kemp on x-risk method,
  Müller & Cannon on orthogonality), so the note's plural-stack conclusion no longer rests
  on this project's priors alone. The lean that remains is the mirror of the old one: the
  shelf is critique-heavy, with no defender of preference-based alignment or of the
  "techno-utopian" x-risk programme in the primary. Still external: Bostrom and Russell
  themselves, Hongladarom, the governance instruments, the moral-patiency literature
  (Metzinger, Coeckelbergh, Schwitzgebel), Sandel, Susskind, Van Parijs.
- **▲ Misnamed corpus file.** `Banerjee-et-al_Universal-Basic-Income-Short-Term-Results-Kenya_NBER-w32477-2024.md`
  is *not* the Kenya UBI paper: its text is Garcia & Ortega on racial protests and
  Paycheck Protection Program lending. The GiveDirectly/Kenya evidence therefore stays
  external under [S16] and should not be cited from the corpus until re-harvested.
- **▲ Thin corpus copies.** The Kela file is a three-segment OCR brochure, not the
  evaluation report; the MacAskill file is a podcast transcript; Delphi's 1.7-million
  figure is from the 2021 preprint's own description. Quote from the printed sources
  before Stage 2 leans on wording.
- **Unresolved.** Wallach's fault line — near-term harms (bias, manipulation, labour)
  versus long-term superintelligence risk — is unsettled, and over-weighting either
  starves the other [S1]. Whether current systems are near enough to AGI to justify
  lock-in worries is genuinely contested [S19]. And the is/ought wall stands:
  no capability advance can tell AI — or us — what it *ought* to value
  ([[7.4-measurement-falsifiability]]).
