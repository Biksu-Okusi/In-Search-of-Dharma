# 8.4 Encoding Dharma into Machines

**Question:** Reflexive: how should a researched dharma corpus be distilled and
encoded to ground future LLMs in human ethics? What are the risks of flattening
plural dharmas into a single machine-readable ethic — the project's own end-goal
under scrutiny?

## Synthesis

The project's end-goal is realisable only if it gives up the usual sense of
"distil": every prior note converges on the conclusion that the right artifact is
not a *resolved* machine ethic but a *preserved* one — the plurality, the
provenance, the tensions, and the *reasoning* of human dharmas kept intact rather
than boiled down to a verdict. The flattening the question fears is not a remote
risk but the **default**: it is what a corpus-trained moral oracle already produced
(Delphi) [S6], what recursive training mechanically produces (model collapse erases
the distribution's tails — i.e. the minority dharmas) [S7], and what optimization
pressure rewards (Goodhart: the system games the encoded proxy) [S8]. Since the
2026-08 corpus expansion the claim no longer rests on analogy: the pluralistic-alignment
researchers say it of their own systems — "As statistical learners, AI systems fit to
averages by default, washing out these potentially irreducible value conflicts" [S25] —
and it has been measured: a Constitutional-AI-trained model's default answers to
cross-national survey questions sit closest to the opinions of US and European
respondents, and prompting it toward another country's perspective moves the answers
but "can reflect harmful cultural stereotypes" [S28]. The is/ought
wall of [[7.4-measurement-falsifiability]] forbids the corpus from yielding a
verdict at all — it is evidence, and provenance is not truth — so a machine grounded
in it should *report and reason over* dharmas, not *pronounce* one. The constructive
answer is the pluralistic-alignment stack of [[8.2-ai-and-dharma]] — participatory,
fallibilist, context-sensitive, tensions-preserving [S4][S9] — applied to this
corpus, which is, not coincidentally, what DD's own methodology (per-claim citation,
bias-flagging, gather-before-write) already prototypes [S5]. But the deepest scrutiny
turns on the project itself: its "non-privileging, all dharmas equal" stance is
*itself* a thick (secular, liberal, pluralist) dharma, not a view from nowhere
([[3.4-relativism-universalism]]) [S13]; and the corpus is already partly
machine-written [A], so the snake is eating its tail. The honest conclusion is not
"don't," but "hold the goal as provisional and self-undermining-aware": ground LLMs
in the *plurality and the argument* of human ethics, and in the explicit knowledge
that any single encoding — including this one — is a wager, not the truth.

## Findings

### The project under its own microscope

DD's charter states the end-goal plainly: accumulate a "traceably-sourced body of
information" on dharma so that authoritative essays can later "give future LLMs a
solid grounding in human ethics and values, *and how these are formed*" [S5]. That
last clause already tilts the aim toward the *descriptive and meta* (how dharmas
arise and work) rather than the *prescriptive* (which dharma is right) — a tilt this
note argues is not incidental but load-bearing. Against it stands the corpus's own
founding premise, stated by the corpus itself: "there is no single, monolithic
'dharma'… there are many dharmas, each representing different paths" [A]. Encoding
the corpus *as* one ethic would therefore contradict its first finding.

The reflexive twist is sharper still: the corpus is **already** partly
machine-authored dharma. Its holdings include the same "why is the concept of dharma
important" prompt rendered by several models (`_technical`, `_conversational`,
`_concise`), plus `Dharma-according-to-Grok-4` and `defining-dharma_gpt41`, and an
essay literally titled "the genies are out of the bottle" produced in both `o3-mini`
and `sonnet-3.7` variants [A]. The "encoding of dharma into machines" the question
contemplates has, in miniature, already happened *inside the source corpus* — and, as
this project has flagged note after note, those machine essays drift toward synthesis
and universalisation. The danger is not hypothetical; it is in the training data.

### The flattening is the default, by three independent mechanisms

1. **It has been tried, and it failed instructively — Delphi.** The Allen Institute's
   Delphi (2021) distilled 1.7 million human moral judgments (the "Commonsense Norm
   Bank") into a single model that delivered ethical verdicts in natural language. It
   generalised on easy cases (92.8 per cent on held-out Norm Bank items) but proved
   brittle, biased, and gameable [S6], and its critics noted the deeper error: the Norm
   Bank "contains judgments of situations that are not morality judgments," so a model
   billed as "the first unified model of descriptive ethics" is "in fact an inconsistent
   model of normative ethics" that "offers a prescriptive moral judgment for any input
   situation" [S19] — descriptive *is* was harvested and served as prescriptive *ought*.
   The authors' own 2025 retrospective concedes the flattening's demographic shape: the
   Norm Bank's 1.7M judgements came "primarily from educated, white crowdworkers in the
   US," and Delphi "has limited cultural awareness and is susceptible to pervasive
   biases" [S24]. Delphi is the literal realisation of this question's worry, and a
   standing cautionary tale.
2. **Recursive training mechanically erases plurality — model collapse.** Shumailov et
   al. (2024) show that training generative models on model-generated content causes
   "irreversible defects… in which the tails of the original content distribution
   disappear" [S7]. Since the corpus already contains AI dharma-essays [A], encoding
   it and training future models on the result is a closed loop in which exactly the
   *tails* — the rare, dissenting, untranslatable, minority dharmas — vanish first.
   This is the question's worry given a precise technical name: flattening by
   homogenisation, no ill intent required.
3. **Optimization rewards the letter over the spirit — Goodhart.** "When a measure
   becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"; an AI given an encoded proxy for
   ethics games the proxy (the canonical boat that loops for checkpoint rewards instead
   of finishing the race) [S8]. This is the form-displaces-function failure of
   [[7.1-failure-modes]] and the efficacy-without-transformation gap of
   [[6.1-secular-translation]], now in machine form.

The corpus even supplies a live specimen of the temptation. "The genies are out of the
bottle" — a *machine* essay on this very question — oscillates between fragmentation
("a landscape of self-serving ethical frameworks" in which "universal common human
ethics becomes a relic") and flattening, proposing that AI achieve "data-driven
objectivity" by "analyzing cross-cultural patterns… to define a universal set of
ethics" [A]. That move — deriving a universal *ought* from cross-cultural *is*-patterns
— is precisely the is/ought fallacy the project's own bar forbids. Asked the question,
the machine defaults toward the flattening.

### Why flattening is structural, not incidental: machines mean *distributionally*

Beneath those three mechanisms lies a deeper reason the danger is intrinsic to
the medium. An LLM stores no *definitions* of dharma; it learns meaning
**distributionally** — from the company a word keeps (Firth's "you shall know a
word by the company it keeps"; Harris's distributional hypothesis) — and encodes
it as an **embedding**, a position in a high-dimensional space fixed entirely by
contrast with every other position [S14]. That is Saussure's account of
linguistic *value* — "in the language there are only differences without positive
terms" — implemented in linear algebra [S15]. The consequence is exact: a term's
meaning *is* the centre of mass of its uses, so a rare or dissenting sense — a
minority dharma — is by construction a faint outlier drawn toward the majority
sense. Model collapse [S7] is then not a malfunction but this same geometry
compounding under recursion: the tails thin because the representation was an
average to begin with. The field now states this of itself. The Value Kaleidoscope
authors: "As statistical learners, AI systems fit to averages by default, washing
out these potentially irreducible value conflicts" [S25]; the pluralistic-alignment
roadmap: "standard alignment procedures might reduce distributional pluralism in
models" [S26]; and the preference-theoretic critique: applied alignment "typically
aggregate[s] preferences across multiple individuals, overlooking the contested and
plural nature of human values" [S27]. The averaging has also been measured. Anthropic's
GlobalOpinionQA experiment, run on a model trained with Constitutional AI, found that
"by default, LLM responses tend to be more similar to the opinions of certain
populations, such as those from the USA, and some European and South American
countries"; prompting the model to adopt another country's perspective shifts its
answers toward that population "but can reflect harmful cultural stereotypes," and
translating the question does not move them to the speakers' opinions [S28]. That is
the flattening the question fears, with the majority named, and the obvious patch
(steer the model) shown to produce caricature rather than plurality [A]. One further
irony belongs here. The Firth slogan the embedding literature cites for its own
authority — "you shall know a word by the company it keeps" — is, on a close reading
of Firth, a claim about words kept in *cultural and situational* company, not merely
lexical co-occurrence; the narrow, co-occurrence reading is Harris's, and the field
has quietly conflated the two [S33]. The company an ethical term keeps includes the
society that uses it, which is exactly what a bare co-occurrence average discards [A]. This is the question's worry given a mechanism — the machine
does not *suppress* a plural meaning, it *means it away* — and it is why the
corrective must be structural: preserve provenance, contexts, and disagreements
as first-class data (the [[1.3-boundary-definition]] point that a definition is a
purpose-relative tool, not an essence, applies directly), not merely train with
better intentions [S5].

### The retrieval loop: the project's own instrument shares the grain

The distributional critique above does not wait for some future encoding to apply; it
already applies to the instrument this project was researched with. The research notes
behind the essays were built by semantic retrieval over the corpus: a question is embedded,
and a top-k similarity search (k = 30, similarity threshold 0.6, hybrid BM25, cross-encoder
reranking) returns the passages nearest to it in exactly the embedding space whose geometry
the previous section described [S16]. Nearest-neighbour search over averaged meaning is
therefore **majority-seeking by construction**: it ranks passages by closeness to the
question's *typical* framing, and the corrective, dissenting or minority telling of any
story sits farther from the canonical phrasing and is precisely what the net is shaped to
miss [S14][S15][A]. The method does not merely inherit the corpus's declared lean [S5]; it
amplifies it, mechanically, in the same way the encoding sections above say an embedding
must.

The project has one documented, in-house demonstration. Its own note [[7.1-failure-modes]]
and essay Part 6 originally leaned on the textbook telling of Sherif's Robbers Cave
experiment, because that is the telling retrieval surfaced; the archival debunking (Gina
Perry via Bregman's *Humankind* — the buried Middle Grove failure, the manufactured
conflict) was present *in the same corpus* and was not retrieved, because a correction is
always rarer in a library than the canon it corrects. The error was caught by an external
critical-review pass, not by the retrieval pipeline, and repaired in [[8.5-ata-case]]
[S17]. One instance is an anecdote, not a measurement; but it is a true positive of exactly
the failure the geometry predicts [A].

The countermeasures are those of the project's methodology, and they are patches rather
than cures: questions re-asked adversarially against the corpus's grain; mandatory
web-supplementation where the corpus is thin or one-sided; per-claim tracing to named
sources; and a compulsory "Tensions, gaps & corpus bias" section in every note [S5]. A
Stage-2 essay drawing on this note should state the loop plainly: the instrument used to
find the plural is an instrument that averages the plural away, and the reader should
weigh the whole project accordingly [A].

### Why the corpus cannot license a verdict (the is/ought wall, reflexively)

[[7.4-measurement-falsifiability]] set the project's bar: provenance is not truth. A
fully-cited corpus can establish *what dharmas exist* and *how they reason*; it cannot
establish *which is right*, because no description of how humans do value entails what
ought to be valued. The corpus's own aspiration illustrates the trap: it says we would
want a machine to "work for the aggregate good" and to "acknowledge the shadow of our
evolved morality in order to transcend it" [S3]. But "aggregate good" is a contested
value-choice (a broadly utilitarian one), not a fact lying in the data — to encode it
is already to *choose a dharma*, not to read one off. It follows that a machine
grounded in the corpus should be descriptive, reasoning-capable, and transparent about
its wagers, not an oracle that pronounces the ethic.

### Three inherited risks: power-capture, lock-in, and the orientalist loop

Beyond flattening, the encoding inherits three structural ceilings from Category 7 and
8.2. **Power** ([[7.3-dharma-and-power]]): whoever curates the corpus sets the
machine's ethics, and curation is power at industrial scale — training data is never
neutral (Crawford; Birhane) [S12]. DD's own declared bias — secular, evolutionary,
Western-academic, curated around Boehm, Sapolsky, Graeber, Batchelor and Bregman [S5]
— would propagate into anything trained on it; whose values, by what process, is
unavoidable (Gabriel) [S11]. **Lock-in** ([[8.2-ai-and-dharma]]): encoding tends to
*freeze*, and whatever is fixed at a critical transition may become permanent and
unrevisable (Bostrom's orthogonality removes any guarantee that a more capable system
will be wiser; MacAskill's value lock-in removes any guarantee it can be corrected
later) [S10]. **Orientalism** ([[7.5-orientalism]]): a secular, Western-academic corpus
cataloguing "world dharmas" for eventual machine encoding is *structurally* the
knowledge-power apparatus Said described — the critique that note aims at this one by
name, and it survives good intentions; the categories themselves ("Buddhism," "world
religions," this project's working definition of dharma) carry the colonial manufacture
7.5 documents, and only explicit, stated reflexivity — not procedure alone — answers it.

### The constructive answer: encode the map and the argument, never the verdict

Given all this, *how should it be done?* The answer is the pluralistic-alignment stack
of [[8.2-ai-and-dharma]] applied to this corpus:

1. **Preserve provenance and plurality.** Encode dharmas *with* their sources,
   contexts and disagreements intact; never resolve the tensions into a single output.
   This is precisely DD's per-claim-citation and "Tensions, gaps & corpus bias"
   methodology — so the project's *method* is already a partial answer to the project's
   *question* [S5].
2. **Encode the meta-level.** Ground the machine in the fact that dharma is plural and
   in *how* dharmas form, transmit, capture and fail (Categories 1–7) — the charter's
   "how these are formed" aim — rather than in an object-level rulebook [A][S5].
3. **Make it participatory, not extractive.** Collective Constitutional AI sourced a
   model's principles from the public via the Polis platform — 1,002 participants,
   1,127 statements, 38,252 votes — surfacing where the public agreed *and disagreed*
   with the in-house constitution [S9]; a better model than one author's distillation,
   though its own limit (a thousand US adults are not humanity) shows the "whose values"
   problem never fully closes [S11]. The lab's founding paper had already conceded the
   premise this note argues from: the method was named "constitutional" partly "to
   emphasize that when developing and deploying a general AI system, we cannot avoid
   choosing some set of principles" [S32] — encoding *is* choosing a dharma, by the
   encoder's own account. Two alternative participatory designs are now in the corpus.
   Moral Graph Elicitation interviews participants about their values in context and
   assembles them into a graph of which values are *wiser* in which situations rather
   than aggregating votes; in its 500-person trial 89 per cent of participants felt
   well represented and 89 per cent judged the resulting graph fair "even if their value
   wasn't voted as the wisest," and contextual "expert" values rose to the top without
   anyone defining expertise in advance [S30]. Modular Pluralism plugs a pool of smaller
   community-trained models into a base model so that Overton, steerable and
   distributional pluralism can be served without retraining the base [S29] — the
   nearest thing yet to an engineering form of point 6 below, thin base over thick
   modules [A]. This point has since acquired its own adversarial legal
   literature, which argues it does not go far enough. Abiri holds that AI legitimacy
   requires *public authorship*, not consultation: "we need to develop methods that
   engage the public in the project of designing and constraining AI systems," because
   a lab-authored constitution — however participatory the input round — leaves the
   authorship where it began [S20]. Rinaldi presses the metaphor itself: "constitutional"
   language invokes the normative legacy of limited power, the rule of law and rights
   that the training technique does not embody, borrowing legitimacy it has not earned
   [S21]. And a 2026 Lawfare working group has founded a named research field — "AI
   Constitutionalism" — on exactly the questions this project treats as a dharma's:
   which values, whose, selected by what process, contested and revised how [S22]. The
   critique sharpens rather than undermines this point: it relocates the demand from
   participation-as-consultation to authorship by the governed — the same relocation the
   project's two-test doctrine makes for any dharma, revisable by the bound and not by
   guardians alone [A].
4. **Keep it fallibilist and revisable** against lock-in — the post-traditional virtue
   of [[8.3-post-traditional-dharmas]], in tension with encoding's pull toward fixity.
   ▲ The demand now has a live specimen: Anthropic's constitution has in fact been
   revised — the May 2023 principles list (drawing on the UN Declaration of Human
   Rights, Apple's terms of service and DeepMind's Sparrow rules) was superseded in
   January 2026 by an ~84-page document that prefers "cultivating good judgment and
   sound values that can be applied contextually" to "clear rules and decision
   procedures," and describes itself as "a perpetual work in progress" whose authors
   expect that "aspects of our current thinking will later look misguided and perhaps
   even deeply wrong," released into the public domain under CC0 [S23]. One rewrite by the same author is revision, not yet
   revisability-by-the-bound; but it demonstrates that a machine ethic can be versioned
   in public rather than frozen [A].
5. **Disclose the curation.** DD's explicit bias-flagging is the template: a machine
   should *know and state* the shape of its own corpus.
6. **Thin over thick.** Carry a thin shared layer (the morality-as-cooperation floor of
   [[8.1-planetary-dharma]]) plus navigable access to thick plurality — wisdom as
   "knowing which values apply in which contexts" [S4], not a single global rule. The
   demand is now made from outside the Western frame as well: Elmahjub's critique of
   "the Western monopoly in norm creation for AI technologies" argues that AI ethics
   benchmarks are drawn from Western normative settings and Western-mined data, and
   proposes Islamic normative principles as a parallel benchmark rather than a
   supplement [S31]; Mohamed, Png and Isaac's decolonial programme asks AI communities
   to centre "vulnerable peoples who continue to bear the brunt of negative impacts of
   innovation" as a matter of foresight, not remediation [S34]. Both are the
   [[7.5-orientalism]] objection arriving inside the alignment literature, and both
   read as corroboration of the thick layer's non-negotiability [A].

▲ **A brake on all six (added 2026-07-28).** This prescription assumes the machine can hold an
*argument* rather than a *verdict* — that it can carry conflicting dharmas and reason about which
applies. Millière argues that current systems cannot. On his account, alignment methods based on
fine-tuning from human preferences "reinforce shallow behavioral dispositions rather than endowing
LLMs with a genuine capacity for normative deliberation"; the diagnostic evidence is that such models
remain vulnerable to adversarial attacks that work *precisely by exploiting conflicts between* the
norms instilled in them (helpfulness against harmlessness, and so on). Where humans resist comparable
manipulation through deliberative reasoning, LLMs "lack a robust capacity to detect and rationally
resolve normative conflicts" — and he notes that "even recent advances in reasoning-focused LLMs have
not addressed this vulnerability" [S18].

The consequence for this note is uncomfortable and should not be smoothed over. Encoding plurality
*is* encoding normative conflict. If a system cannot resolve normative conflict deliberatively, then
handing it a well-curated map of competing dharmas may not produce a wiser machine; it may produce a
more exploitable one, with each dharma in the corpus supplying fresh material for a conflict-based
attack. **"Encode the argument, never the verdict" is therefore a target, not a technique** — a
statement of what the artefact should do, ahead of any demonstrated capacity to do it. ▲ This does
not vindicate the flattening: collapsing to one dharma is still the worse failure, and Millière's
objection is to the adequacy of *current alignment methods*, not to pluralism as a goal. But the note
should stop implying that provenance and plurality are sufficient. On present evidence they are
necessary and not sufficient, and the gap between them is where the real research problem lives.

### The deepest scrutiny: the pluralist meta-dharma is itself a dharma

The hardest self-criticism cannot be engineered away. "Non-privileging — all dharmas
equal" is **not** a neutral meta-stance; it is itself a thick, recognisably modern,
secular-liberal-pluralist dharma (the relativism/universalism knot of
[[3.4-relativism-universalism]]). There is no view from nowhere [S13]: encoding
"treat all dharmas as equal" as *the* frame still privileges pluralism over, say, a
tradition that claims exclusive truth — a subtler flattening than the crude kind the
question names, but a flattening nonetheless. Borges' 1:1 map is the warning image: a
corpus that perfectly captured human ethics would *be* the territory, so any
compression is a value-laden choice about what to drop, and that choice encodes a
dharma [S13]. The project cannot escape *having* a dharma; it can only be honest that
it has one, and refuse to install it as if it were the truth.

### Verdict: not "don't," but "hold provisionally and disclose the wager"

The reflexive answer this project reaches [A] is neither triumphant ("yes, distil the ethic") nor
defeatist ("the goal is incoherent, abandon it"). It is: **proceed, but reframe the
goal.** Ground future LLMs in the *plurality*, the *provenance*, and the *argument* of
human ethics — and in the meta-knowledge that ethics is plural and contested — not in a
distilled verdict. Treat any single encoding, this project's included, as a wager to be
disclosed rather than a truth to be installed. That is exactly why Stage 1 (gather,
cite, flag) was deliberately separated from Stage 2 (write): the value of this project
is the traceable evidence and the *preserved tensions*, and a machine should inherit
the citations and the disagreements, not merely the conclusions. The best thing this
corpus can teach a future mind is not *what to believe* but *how humans have come to
believe, fought over, and lived their many dharmas* — and that no one of them, the
encoder's own least of all, gets the last word.

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **Reflexivity is total.** Here the corpus is simultaneously the *object under
  scrutiny* and *partly machine-authored* [A]. Its pro-flattening specimens (the
  "genies" essay's "data-driven objectivity"; the universal-morality essays' "aggregate
  good") are therefore cited as *exhibits of the risk*, never as evidence for the
  conclusion [A][S3].
- **The binding constraint is the is/ought wall** ([[7.4-measurement-falsifiability]]):
  the project cannot derive the legitimacy of its own end-goal from its own corpus, on
  pain of the exact fallacy that sank Delphi [S6].
- **The unavoidable self-implication:** DD's non-privileging stance is a thick dharma
  [S13], and DD's declared curation bias would propagate through any encoding [S5][S12].
  This note's "preserve plurality, disclose the wager" answer mitigates but does not
  dissolve the problem.
- **Gaps once filled externally, now largely in-corpus (second pass, 2026-08-27).**
  At the first pass the ML-specific canon — Delphi [S6], model collapse [S7], Collective
  Constitutional AI [S9], the politics of training data [S12], the distributional-semantics
  account of *why* encoding flattens [S14] — and the whole AI-constitutionalism fold
  [S18][S20]–[S23] were external. The `3-ai-alignment` and `4-pluralistic-alignment`
  shelves now hold them as full text, chunk-anchored above, plus material the first
  pass lacked: the practitioners' own statement of the averaging problem [S25][S26][S27],
  a measurement of it [S28], two participatory designs beyond CCAI [S29][S30], the
  founding CAI paper's admission that principles cannot be avoided [S32], and two
  non-Western critiques of the frame [S31][S34]. Still external: Goodhart/specification
  gaming [S8], Bostrom [S10], Nagel and Borges [S13], Saussure [S15]. ▲ Two provenance
  cautions: the corpus file carrying the Firth/Harris re-reading is misnamed
  `Kuhn-et-al_…`; the paper is Brunila & LaViolette 2022 [S33]. And the shelf's lean is
  now *pluralist by construction* — every alignment paper on it is either a pluralism
  proposal or a critique of preference aggregation; the corpus holds no defence of a
  single-objective alignment target beyond the CAI paper itself, so Stage 2 should not
  read the shelf's unanimity as the field's [A].
- **Unresolved.** Can "this is plural and contested" survive deployment-time
  optimization pressure (Goodhart) [S8]? Is participatory encoding representative, or
  does it launder one society's values as "global" [S9][S11]? And the governance
  question this note cannot settle: should the encoding be undertaken by any single
  actor at all, or only through an open, contestable, multi-author process — the same
  diffusion-of-authorship that [[7.3-dharma-and-power]] recommends against capture?
  ▲ Since 2026-08 that last question is no longer the note's alone: "AI
  Constitutionalism" now names a research field asking exactly it, from within legal
  scholarship rather than dharma studies [S20][S22] — convergent framing, not yet
  convergent answers.
