# 8.3 Post-Traditional Dharmas

**Question:** What would a post-traditional dharma look like — one that honours
wisdom traditions while embracing scientific understanding and cultural pluralism?

## Synthesis

A post-traditional dharma is not *anti*-traditional but *post-conventional*: it has
passed through tradition and kept its practices and "ultimate concern" while loosening
its metaphysics, institutional authority, and identity-labels [S1][A]. The question's
honour-versus-embrace framing is softer than it looks, because the "wisdom traditions"
we inherit were *already* remade by modernity — detraditionalized, demythologized,
psychologized — so post-traditional dharmas are the honest continuation of a
150-year-old process, not a clean break, and they multiply in what Charles Taylor calls
the "nova effect" [S9][S11]. Their constructive features are fallibilism,
naturalism-without-scientism, practice and community over doctrine, and critical
reflexivity [S3][S4][S13]. Two opposite failure modes bound them — and both are forms
of the flattening this project guards against ([[8.4-encoding-dharma-machines]]): at one end, collapse into shallow
DIY consumerism ("Sheilaism") [S14]; at the other, the sharper danger of collapsing
distinct traditions into one perennial "deep unity," which the corpus's own
Interspirituality courts [S6] and which Steven Katz's constructivism shows to be false
[S12]. Two further findings cut against easy optimism: a *secular* dharma is not
automatically safer or more rational than a religious one — it can become a "stealth
religion," dogmatic in its own right [S4] — and the community/belonging that traditional
religion delivered remains the unsolved problem, with secular substitutes so far
under-performing [S5] — the one ethnography of a humanist congregation finds it
"sacralize[s] the secular" but, "absent a 'core faith message,'" places "little by way
of demands" and is "unlikely to draw the kind of commitments that religious
congregations are known for" [S24]. Read in the primaries (2026-08-27), the
already-modern thesis is sharper still: McMahan's detraditionalization is by definition
a "shift of orientation from external to internal authority" that makes religion "a
matter of choice — one has the right to choose and even construct one's own religion"
[S9], so the road from post-traditional dharma to Sheilaism is not a swerve but the same
road; and from the classical side the carve looks like "a rupture with Buddhist
tradition," not a continuation [S26]. Which of the plural results deserve to be called
*better* is not this note's question: the project's settled two-test answer lives in
the companion essay `the-better-ones.md` (holds without crushing; stays revisable by
those bound), and a post-traditional dharma's fallibilism is precisely what keeps it
inside the second test [A]. The corpus's anarchist tranche converts one of this note's hopes
into a datum: a non-theistic, voluntarily adopted, practice-centred ethical tradition
has in fact run for a century and a half — with the negative result attached, for it
never achieved durable mass transmission, persisting as a recurrent tendency rather
than an institution [S18][A]. And Colin Ward supplies a third answer to how such a
dharma gets built at all — neither inherited nor constructed but *discovered*, "like a
seed beneath the snow," already operating in everyday mutuality and needing only
extension [S18] — a route that steps around the construction warning of
[[7.7-engineered-dharmas]] by method rather than restraint [A]. There will be many post-traditional dharmas, not one; this note
and the project that contains it ([[8.4-encoding-dharma-machines]]) are themselves an
instance of the genre.

## Findings

### What "post-traditional" means — and what it does not

"Post-traditional" is easily misread as "anti-traditional." It is better understood as
*post-conventional* in Kohlberg's and Habermas's sense (cf [[5.3-moral-psychology-development]]):
a stance reached by internalising a tradition and then moving beyond its unquestioned
authority, not by skipping it — the reading this note adopts [A]. The corpus's central exemplar, Stephen Batchelor's
*After Buddhism*, supplies the structural move. Batchelor distinguishes two senses of
"religious": (1) the wish "to come to terms with… our own birth and death" — Tillich's
"ultimate concern" — and (2) "whatever formal means are employed — adherence to sacred
texts, submission to the authority of monastics and priests, performance of rites" — to
enact that concern [S1]. A post-traditional dharma *keeps the first and loosens the
second*: one can be driven by ultimate concern "without ever engaging in any overtly
religious behaviour," which is why, as Batchelor notes, "those who describe themselves
as 'devout atheists' are not entirely joking" [S1]. The corollary stance — "moving
beyond a Buddhist identity while still engaging with dharmic practices," extracting
insight "without adhering to religious dogma or identity labels" — is the secular-dharma
programme in one sentence [A].

The deepest reframing, though, dissolves the question's premise. David McMahan shows
that the "wisdom traditions" available to us are *already* products of modernity:
Buddhist modernism was produced by three "cultural processes that are largely a product
of modernity": **detraditionalization** — "the shift of orientation from external to
internal authority and the associated reorientation from institutional to privatized
religion," the tendency "to elevate reason, experience, and intuition over tradition and
to assert the freedom to reject, adopt, or reinterpret traditional beliefs and practices
on the basis of individual evaluation"; **demythologization** (the six realms of rebirth
transmuted "from ontological orders to psychological states"); and **psychologization**
(the Buddhism–Western-psychology interface as "one of the most prevalent frameworks of
interpretation by westerners"), with figures such as DT Suzuki having "decontextualized
Zen literature in order to incorporate it into a framework of modern, largely western,
ideas" [S9]. McMahan adds two riders the note needs. The product was "a cocreation of
Asians, Europeans, and Americans," not a Western imposition [S9]; and detraditionalization
carries its own pathology already named — "religion becomes more individualized,
privatized, and a matter of choice — one has the right to choose and even construct
one's own religion," from which "can also emerge the 'consumerization and
instrumentalization' of religion" [S9]. So "honouring wisdom traditions while embracing
science" is not the fusion of two pristine things — the fusion happened generations ago.
"Post-traditional" is simply the honest name for what has been underway since the
nineteenth century, and what Taylor's "immanent frame" and "nova effect" made
inevitable: once "a viable humanist alternative" was posited it "set in train a dynamic,
something like a nova effect, spawning an ever-widening variety of moral/spiritual
options, across the span of the thinkable and perhaps even beyond," and the same
pluralism produces a "mutual fragilization of all the different views in presence, the
undermining sense that others think differently" — so every option, "whether believing
or unbelieving," is fragile in the way a post-traditional dharma must learn to live
with [S11]. Richard King supplies the critic's version of the same genealogy: a
detraditionalised Vedanta "particularly amenable to modern Western interests in
non-institutional and privatized forms of spirituality," and a "mysticism" that, once
decontextualised, becomes "élitist … antisocial … otherworldly … and domesticated" — the
privatisation that makes a dharma post-traditional is also what can make it socially
inert [S27].

### The constructive features

Across the exemplars, five family resemblances recur:

1. **Fallibilism / revisability.** A post-traditional dharma holds its claims
   provisionally and updates them against evidence, rather than treating them as fixed
   revelation — the answer it offers to the grounding problem of
   [[3.3-grounding-problem]] and the evidentiary discipline of
   [[7.4-measurement-falsifiability]]. (The corpus's secular dharma *claims* this
   virtue; Evan Thompson's critique, below, is that it often fails to deliver it.)
2. **Naturalism without scientism.** Religious naturalism (Ursula Goodenough, Loyal
   Rue) treats the sacred as *emergent from* nature — "the mystical dimension as
   emergent from one's mind and heart" rather than access to a separate spiritual
   realm — reverence without the supernatural [S13]. Crucially this is *not* the
   reduction of value to science that sinks Harris's project (cf
   [[7.4-measurement-falsifiability]]); it keeps the is/ought wall intact. The corpus's
   minimalist version is the Dalai Lama's "no need for temples… our own brain, our own
   heart is our temple; the philosophy is kindness" [S3].
3. **Orthopraxy over orthodoxy.** What is preserved is practice — meditation, ethics,
   ritual, community (cf [[5.4-ritual-habituation]], [[6.1-secular-translation]]).
   Ritual matters because, as costly-signalling theory shows (Sosis & Bressler 2003),
   it builds cooperation precisely through non-rational, hard-to-fake commitment [S7].
   ▲ **The same paper carries a result this note has been citing around** (added 2026-07-28).
   Sosis & Bressler's dataset is 83 nineteenth-century American communes, and the headline
   finding is comparative: **religious communes outlasted secular ones by a factor of two to
   four**, with a median lifespan on the order of 25 years against roughly 5 [S23]. Worse for
   the constructive project here, the dose–response held *only* for the religious communes:
   more costly requirements predicted greater longevity among them, and showed **no such
   effect** among the secular ones. So the secular-community problem this note treats as a
   matter of insufficient thickness may be **structural** — the commitment mechanism appears
   to need the unfalsifiable warrant that a post-traditional dharma is precisely renouncing.
   ▲ That is a harder problem than the note previously posed, and it should be carried as such
   rather than softened: it is the sharpest available statement of what
   [[6.1-secular-translation]] is up against. It does not close the question — nineteenth-century
   communes are one setting, and selection into them was not random — but any claim that a
   designed secular community can hold has to answer it.
   But this exposes a signature difficulty: once a ritual is understood to be "just" a
   bonding mechanism, it risks the scrupulosity Sapolsky describes — performing "the
   ritual for its own sake… without thinking of the content," i.e. form displacing
   function ([[7.1-failure-modes]]) [S7].
4. **Critical reflexivity.** A post-traditional dharma is, in principle, aware of its
   own failure modes, power dynamics, and appropriation risks (cf [[7.1-failure-modes]],
   [[7.2-appropriation]], [[7.3-dharma-and-power]]). The corpus's best instance is
   DS Wilson's own warning that secular belief systems must be tested lest they
   "go the way of stealth religions" [S4].
5. **Community without authoritarianism.** The proposal for "forward-looking,
   quasi-religious groups that provide the benefits of traditional religious communities
   while eschewing supernatural beliefs" — fellowship-first, face-to-face,
   intergenerational [S5].

### The exemplars are plural by design

The nova effect is visible in the spread of actual post-traditional dharmas: secular
Buddhism (Batchelor's *Buddhism Without Beliefs*, recasting the four truths as tasks
rather than propositions) [S1][S15]; Sam Harris's secular-meditation *Waking Up* [S17];
religious naturalism [S13]; modern Stoicism (cf [[6.1-secular-translation]]); Alain de
Botton's "religion for atheists" and Comte-Sponville's "atheist spirituality," which
borrow religion's practices, calendars and architecture without its creed [S17] — a
borrowing Terry Eagleton reads as the old elite instrumentalism ("Atheism was fine for
the elite, but might breed dissent among the masses"): a book that "assumes that
religious beliefs are a lot of nonsense, but that they remain indispensible to civilised
existence," finding religion "sporadically useful, interesting and consoling" [S25] — the
consent test of [[7.2-appropriation]] failed from the inside, since the borrowed dharma's
own adherents are treated as useful fools [A]; humanist congregations such as the Sunday
Assembly, whose services "sacralize the secular" through song, silence and readings
"in some sense analogous to religious congregational worship" [S24][S5]; and prosocial /
interspiritual movements [S6]. No single one is "the" post-traditional dharma — which is exactly the
point, and exactly where the dangers live.

### The precedent that already ran the experiment: anarchism — evidence, and negative evidence

The strongest new support for this note's central claim is not a proposal but a track
record. Anarchism has been, since the mid-nineteenth century, very nearly what a
post-traditional dharma is supposed to be: a non-theistic, voluntarily adopted way of
life with an ethics (mutual aid, non-domination), a practice, and no supernatural
metaphysics [A]. Colin Ward's summary makes the family resemblance explicit:
"Anarchists are people who make a social and political philosophy out of the natural
and spontaneous tendency of humans to associate together for their mutual benefit," in
a lineage he traces from Godwin through Proudhon, Bakunin and Kropotkin [S18]. Ticked
against the five constructive features above, the tradition scores on each —
revisable doctrine, evolutionary naturalism (Kropotkin's grounding of the moral sense;
cf [[0.2-politics-and-anarchism]]), orthopraxy, reflexive critique, and community
without authoritarianism as its defining commitment — which upgrades the note's thesis
from "possible in principle" to "attested for roughly 150 years" [S18][A].

The same record supplies the negative finding, which must be stated just as plainly:
anarchism never achieved durable mass transmission. Ward's own history concedes it —
"Everywhere they were defeated, and the historians wrote that anarchism finally died
when Franco's troops entered Barcelona in 1939," before the unforeseen 1968 resurgence
[S18] — and the epigraph he chooses (John Quail) states the structural law: "The
anarchist movement grows in times of popular self-activity, feeds it and feeds off it,
and declines when that self-activity declines" [S18]. A century and a half of
persistence, then, but as a recurrent tendency, never an institution: the transmission
problem of [[2.4-transmission-institutionalization]] unsolved, and the same
under-performance the secular-community campaign admits of its own efforts [S5]. The
uncomfortable suspicion this plants is that the very features that make a dharma
post-traditional — voluntarism, anti-authoritarianism, fallibilism — may be the
features that prevent durable institutionalisation; structural impermanence may be the
price of refusing the boot of [[7.7-engineered-dharmas]] [A]. The tradition's
fissiparity makes the point in miniature: its chief institutional theorist, Bookchin,
grew tired of "defending anarchism against anarchists," publicly broke with anarchism
late in life and founded a new -ism, communalism [S22].

### Discovered, not designed: Ward's third assembly route

As it stood, this note knew two ways a post-traditional dharma comes to exist:
renovation of an inherited tradition (Batchelor's keeping-and-loosening) and deliberate
construction (the conditional licence of [[7.7-engineered-dharmas]]). Ward's *Anarchy
in Action* argues a third: the order is *already there*, and the work is noticing and
extending it. "An anarchist society, a society which organises itself without
authority, is always in existence, like a seed beneath the snow"; anarchism, "far from
being a speculative vision of a future society," is "a description of a mode of human
organisation, rooted in the experience of everyday life," operating alongside and in
spite of the dominant authoritarian trends [S18]. He grounds the stance in Gustav
Landauer — anarchy as "the actualisation and reconstruction of something that has
always been present, which exists alongside the state, albeit buried and laid waste" —
and Paul Goodman: a free society is "the extension of spheres of free action until
they make up most of social life"; the practical upshot being that "the alternatives
are already there, in the interstices of the dominant power structure. If you want to
build a free society, the parts are all at hand" [S18].

For this note the discovery model does two things. First, it dissolves most of Scott's
high-modernist syndrome ([[7.7-engineered-dharmas]]) by method rather than restraint —
no blueprint, no blank-slate premise, nothing to impose — and it chimes with the note's
own naturalism: if the ethics is the evolved default of a cooperative primate, then a
dharma is found in practice before it is ever written down (cf
[[0.2-politics-and-anarchism]]) [A]. Second, it shows the discover-versus-design
question unresolved *inside* the precedent tradition itself. Bookchin occupies the
opposite pole: explicit utopian design — "'Be practical, do the impossible'… to do the
impossible is the most rational and practical thing we can do" [S21] — and libertarian
municipalism as deliberate institution-building, in which "creating free cities is
about developing free citizens" through designed assemblies and confederations [S22].
Ward and Bookchin are two poles within one tradition, which suggests a post-traditional
dharma will need both: a discovered substrate and designed institutions [A]. Bookchin
adds a transmission observation worth keeping: "People change unconsciously before
they change consciously" [S21] — adoption of a new dharma is felt before it is
professed, Ward's point restated at the level of the person [A].

### The fusion specimens: post-traditional dharmas assembling in the wild

The corpus now holds two primary specimens of the anarchist–contemplative fusion
actually being attempted. Their standing must be fixed first: Anna Ronan's "Anarchism
as a Spiritual Practice" is a DSA Libertarian Socialist Caucus pamphlet (2019),
explicitly the author's own opinion; Anna Prouty's "An Anarcho-Taoist Manifesto"
(2017) is a self-published essay circulated via Medium and The Anarchist Library.
Neither carries scholarly or institutional warrant; they are cited here strictly as
evidence that the fusion is live — specimens of post-traditional dharma-making caught
in the act — never as authorities on whether it works [S19][S20][A]. Read as
specimens, they are informative about how such dharmas assemble:

- **Religion-shaped self-description, metaphysics disclaimed.** Ronan opens
  "Anarchism is my religion" — "as personal, emotional, psychological and universal to
  me as any religion or spirituality could be" — while insisting "I'm not getting at
  anything pan-dimensional or explicitly sacred here" [S19]. That is Batchelor's move
  (keep the ultimate concern, drop the institutional form) executed from a political
  rather than a Buddhist starting point [A].
- **A donor structure.** The political tradition supplies the ethics (consent,
  non-domination, mutual aid); the contemplative tradition supplies the technique and
  idiom (Tao, *wu wei*, flow). Ronan's programme is "making the external practices of
  horizontalism, self-determination, spontaneity and mutual aid an internal practice
  too" [S19]; Prouty fuses anarchist consent theory with a needs-and-feelings
  psychology and reaches explicitly for Buddhism — "freeing yourself from desire, in a
  full Buddhist sense" [S20]. Post-traditional dharmas, on this evidence, assemble by
  pairing an ethics-donor with a practice-donor [A].
- **Fallibilism as house style.** "Treat this as practice. Experiment. Hold it
  lightly, and use it as best serves you" [S19]; Prouty refuses to specify the
  end-state at all: "Anarchism is that which emerges naturally without exterior
  direction or control. I cannot tell you what it looks like. That is the entire
  point" [S20].
- **The appropriation pattern, reproduced.** Both independently reach for Taoism —
  plausibly the available tradition with the least theistic and institutional surface
  [A] — and both take it decontextualised: Ronan's cited Taoist authority is *The Tao
  of Pooh*, a Western pop digest, and no lineage, teacher or living Taoist community
  appears in either text [S19][S20]. This reproduces in miniature the
  borrowing-without-relationship flagged in [[7.2-appropriation]], and stands one step
  from Sheilaism [S14] — the step being that both authors write from and answer to a
  real political movement, which supplies the community and accountability Sheila
  lacked [A].
- **No transmission mechanism.** Both are single-author essays; neither describes a
  practice community, a pedagogy, or any way the dharma outlives its author — the
  tradition-level finding (persistent tendency, no institution) recurring at the
  smallest scale [A].

### The two flattening failure modes (the flattening caution, doubled)

A post-traditional dharma can betray the plural in two opposite directions.

- **The shallow end — "Sheilaism."** Robert Bellah's *Habits of the Heart* named the
  archetype: Sheila Larson's private faith, "Sheilaism," following her "own little
  voice," co-opting "strands of multiple religions… without much theological
  consideration" [S14]. This is the buffet/consumerist degeneration — the same
  hollowing diagnosed as McMindfulness in [[6.1-secular-translation]] and as
  decontextualised borrowing in [[7.2-appropriation]]: maximal personalisation, minimal
  depth, no community, no accountability.
- **The deep end — perennialism.** The sharper danger, because it masquerades as
  respect, is the claim that all traditions are surface expressions of one underlying
  truth. The corpus advances exactly this: its Interspirituality literature holds that
  "contemplative practices unearth the mystical roots of the world's religious
  traditions, revealing a deep unity" (Wayne Teasdale, Kurt Johnson, in a Teilhardian
  lineage) [S6]. But this perennialism (Huxley) is precisely the single-dharma
  flattening this project resists — and Steven Katz's constructivism shows it is also
  false: there are "no pure or unmediated mystical experiences"; each tradition
  *constructs* the experiences it produces, so a Christian's union and a Buddhist's
  realisation "are not two encounters with the same reality but two fundamentally
  different experiences," and a forced global synthesis ends up "stripped of much of its
  meaning" [S12]. Honouring traditions means honouring them as genuinely *different*,
  not as interchangeable masks of one essence — the same thin-over-thick logic as
  [[8.1-planetary-dharma]].

Behind both lies a meta-failure: the assumption that a *secular* dharma is automatically
rational and safe. DS Wilson's test exposes this — any belief system, religious or
secular, that "sacrifices factual realism on the altar of practical realism" by
portraying "a world without trade-offs" is functioning as a stealth religion; his
Exhibit A is not a church but Ayn Rand's Objectivism, "rationalist" yet as
trade-off-free as any fundamentalism [S4]. Secular ≠ undogmatic.

### The unsolved problems: community and disenchantment

Two difficulties remain genuinely open. First, **community**: despite long effort,
secular substitutes "have not come close to matching the popularity of traditional
religious communities" [S5], even as the decline of religion correlates with an epidemic
of loneliness whose health toll rivals smoking and obesity [S5]. Smith's fieldwork gives
the mechanism the campaign's admission lacks: congregations "with strong core faith
messages develop stronger congregational adherence," those that "place more demands
(e.g. time commitment, confession of sin, profession of belief) generally elicit stronger
commitments," whereas "absent a 'core faith message' or clear doctrine, Assemblies place
little by way of demands on congregants and are unlikely to draw the kind of commitments
that religious congregations are known for" [S24] — the costly-signalling asymmetry of
[S23] observed in a living secular congregation rather than in nineteenth-century
commune records [A]. The diagnosis is not
new: Bookchin made it in 1978 — "We don't have any villages, we don't have any
communities, we live in a state of atomization" — and anticipated the electronic
pseudo-community that would deepen it: "I may know people in the future for years and
years — play chess games with them, have interesting intellectual conversations with
them — and never touch them once" [S21]. Second,
**disenchantment**: the corpus's *Secondo Natura* mourns a modern "mercantile" order
that has "exiled ritual," replacing the communal and sacred with "the cult of
consumption" [S8] — Taylor's buffered self may be freer but also lonelier and flatter
[S11]. Whether a naturalised dharma can carry the existential weight that transcendence
once bore is the question post-traditional dharmas have not yet answered.

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The singular/plural caution, doubled.** "A post-traditional dharma" is plural in
  reality; the two ways to wrongly singularise it are Sheilaism (atomise) and
  perennialism (homogenise) [S14][S12]. This note treats both as failure modes, not
  resting points.
- **Corpus bias: this is the corpus's home turf — and its advocacy.** The shared corpus
  does not merely *describe* post-traditional dharma; it *is* the secular-dharma /
  secular-Buddhism movement (Batchelor, the P-SAGE programme, the secular-community
  campaign), and the Templeton-funded P-SAGE is advocacy with a funder (cf the same
  flag in [[6.1-secular-translation]]) [A][S6]. So the corpus argues *for* the genre;
  the rigorous internal critique had to be imported — Thompson's charge of "Buddhist
  exceptionalism" (that secular Buddhism cherry-picks and claims a false scientific
  warrant) [S10], McMahan's deflation ("it was all already modernist construction")
  [S9], and Bellah's Sheilaism [S14]. ▲ Second pass (2026-08-27): the traditionalist reply
  is now in the corpus in its own voice. Bhikkhu Bodhi draws the same line McMahan does
  but from the other side — "Classical Buddhism … continues the heritage of Asian
  Buddhism, with minor adaptations made to meet the challenges of modernity," while
  "Secular Buddhism … marks a rupture with Buddhist tradition, a re-visioning of the
  ancient teachings intended to fit the secular culture of the West," the two being "end
  points of a spectrum" rather than fixed categories [S26]. Read together, McMahan and
  Bodhi agree on the facts of the carve and disagree only on its name (continuation vs
  rupture); the note keeps both, because which name is right is a question about the
  *grain* of the tradition that [[2.5-lifecycle-of-a-dharma]] owns [A]. Credit where due: DS Wilson's "stealth religion"
  essay is a genuine *internal* self-critique [S4].
- **Gaps filled externally — now partly in-corpus (2026-08-27).** McMahan [S9], Taylor
  [S11], Pinker [S16], Smith's Sunday Assembly ethnography [S24], Eagleton [S25], Bodhi
  [S26], King [S27] and Sosis & Bressler [S23] are read from full texts in the corpus and
  cited by chunk. Thompson [S10], the Huxley/Katz debate [S12], religious naturalism
  [S13], Bellah's *Habits* [S14], Batchelor's two books [S15] and the de Botton /
  Comte-Sponville / Harris trio [S17] remain external. Pinker is cited with the caveat
  that it under-rates the meaning/disenchantment gap [S16]. McMahan and Taylor are
  born-digital PDFs with ligature artefacts ("fi rst", "signifi cantly"), not OCR; the
  Eagleton review is a scraped *Guardian* page whose article text sits in segments 4–7
  only. Two things the second pass did *not* change: no source-community voice on the
  consent question has entered the corpus, and no defender of Sheilaism-as-legitimate
  (the "spiritual but not religious" apologetic literature) is present — the corpus's
  critics of privatised religion (Bellah, King, Eagleton, Bodhi) all argue from a
  communal or traditional standpoint, so the case *for* pick-and-mix is unmade here.
- **The 2026-07 anarchist tranche deepens the corpus's constitutive anti-authoritarian lean.**
  The new material (Ward, Bookchin, Goldman, Malatesta, Kropotkin, the fusion
  specimens) is overwhelmingly anarchist and libertarian-socialist primary text. For
  this note it supplied the precedent case and the third assembly route — genuine
  gains — but it thickens a lean the corpus already had. That lean is a design property
  of this knowledgebase, not an error awaiting repair, so what it obliges is disclosure
  rather than balance: this note's precedent case is argued by its own partisans, and
  Stage 2 should read it as such. ▲ An earlier draft of this bullet asked for "an argument
  that durable transmission requires authority" — a confusion of *authority* with
  *authoritarianism*, and one the anarchist sources themselves do not make. Bakunin defers
  to the bootmaker and the engineer on their own ground while refusing to let either
  "impose his authority upon me" (see [[2.4-transmission-institutionalization]]), and most
  anarchist streams recognise legitimate authority — competence freely deferred to,
  delegation that stays recallable — while rejecting authority grounded in violence or
  capture. The genuinely missing counterweight is narrower and harder: a serious
  argument, by someone who believes it, that durable transmission requires authority the
  taught *cannot revoke* — office, orthodoxy and enforced non-voluntarism. No such source
  is in the corpus, and it would have to be imported [A].
- **Evidence-grade discipline for the specimens.** Ronan and Prouty are movement
  ephemera — a caucus pamphlet and a self-published essay. They are load-bearing here
  only as proof the fusion is being attempted and as data on *how* it assembles; no
  claim about whether such a fusion works, endures, or transmits may rest on them
  [S19][S20][A].
- **Ward is advocacy too, and says so.** He concedes his book is open to the charge of
  being "a selective gathering of anecdotal evidence to support the points that the
  author wants to make" [S18]; the discovery thesis is an anarchist's chosen reading of
  everyday life, not a neutral ethnographic result (the same normative-reading caveat
  [[0.2-politics-and-anarchism]] records against Graeber and Ward).
- **Free Cities is editorially mediated.** It is a posthumous anthology assembled by
  Eirik Eiglad from "letters, lectures, unfinished drafts, and manuscripts," with
  acknowledged consolidation and revision; late-Bookchin positions quoted from it
  carry the editor's hand [S22].
- **Unresolved.** Can ritual keep its binding power once everyone knows it is "just"
  bonding (the sincerity/transparency problem) [S7]? Can secular community ever match
  religious depth [S5]? Does fallibilism quietly undercut the wholehearted
  commitment that makes a dharma transformative — the efficacy-is-not-transformation
  tension of [[6.1-secular-translation]]? And can a dharma built on voluntarism and
  anti-authoritarianism institutionalise at all, or is the anarchist record — 150
  years of recurrence without an institution — the expected fate of the whole genre
  [S18][A]?
