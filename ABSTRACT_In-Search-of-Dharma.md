# ABSTRACT -- *In Search of Dharma*

Biksu Okusi · Preface + eight parts + coda + appendix · c. 56,000 words · Bali, 2026

## The book in one sentence

A dharma is not a revelation but a technology -- an evolved cultural technology worked out by a particular kind of social animal to solve a particular and permanent problem -- and since dharmas are made rather than found, the question that now falls due is whether we can build them consciously without crushing the plurality that makes them work.

## The argument

The series takes a word almost no one can define and almost everyone uses, and follows it down to a plain root: Sanskrit *√dhṛ*, to hold, support, sustain -- cognate, through Proto-Indo-European, with Latin *firmus*. *Dharma* is a load-bearing word: *that which holds*. Reframed as a function rather than a faith, "what is a dharma?" becomes "what holds a human life, or a people, together?" -- a question every human society has had to answer.

Two commitments, both established in **Part 1**, govern the whole book. First, there is no single *The* Dharma: the source traditions carry an irreducible plurality (*svadharma*, the momentary *dharmas* of Abhidharma, cosmic order), and the definite article is a late Western convenience. Secular philosophies, unwritten peasant codes, professional ethics and the great monotheisms are therefore treated as equally real candidates, none ranked in advance. Second, the definition offered -- a four-feature test of comprehensiveness, identity-constitution, orientation toward the good, and reflective self-awareness -- is presented as a *tool with the toolmaker's fingerprints on it*, not a recovered essence. Wittgenstein's family resemblance, Gallie's essential contestedness, and Talal Asad's genealogical objection are met head-on: authorship is claimed, innocence disclaimed.

**Part 2** explains why dharmas are universal. Morality is roughly a hundred thousand years older than the codes: foraging bands ran a demanding ethical order on reputation, story, and Boehm's "reverse dominance hierarchy", with no scripture and frequently no god. What broke was scale. Above Dunbar's ceiling, gossip cannot reach a stranger; the codes that answered bind by costly, hard-to-fake commitment -- with the sting, held for later, that secular communes never earned the same return on strictness. The Axial eruption of 800–200 BCE is read not as coincidence but as parallel response to converging pressures -- affluence, coinage, literacy, urban anonymity -- with the concept's Eurocentrism flagged rather than smoothed over.

**Part 3** takes the whole argument down to one case with the lid off: the Sedulur Sikep (Samin) of Java, a dharma assembled from about 1890 by an illiterate farmer, carried without scripture, and still being lived -- from deadpan refusal on a colonial forest road to nine women with their feet in wet cement at the Presidential Palace in 2017. It is a boundary case by design: neither world religion nor godless philosophy.

**Part 4** widens to five dharmas that disagree all the way down -- Ubuntu, Stoicism, the Dreaming, Sumarah, and the Abrahamic covenant -- and finds the differences real and the family resemblance equally real. The tour is bracketed by two disclosures of partiality: at the door, Buddhism -- the tradition through which the word reached most readers, and the author's own home water -- is put briefly in the witness chair rather than excused from it; and the Abrahamic traditions come last deliberately, as the test of whether the non-privileging eye can be turned on the (Western/Modern) reader's own water.

**Part 5** answers why a made thing stops feeling made. A dharma installs below the reasoning mind: in moral intuition (Haidt's elephant, with the WEIRD caveat), in habituated practice and ritual synchrony, and in the brain itself -- with the neuroscience carefully deflated where it has been oversold. The machinery is morally blank: it grooves cruelty as faithfully as kindness, and labels both "obvious truth".

**Part 6** turns on the wreckage, refusing the No True Scotsman exit. Every failure is the dark twin of a strength: the bond is the fence; the authority that shames a tyrant crowns one; the path becomes the cage; the fire hollows into form or flares into licensed righteousness; the blueprint becomes the bulldozer (the Jacobin cult, the New Soviet Man, Year Zero); the exchange becomes extraction. Outside the twins entirely stands the insincere predator, who does not over-extend a strength but wears it -- and for whom a trust-manufacturing dharma is very nearly the perfect hunting ground. The author's own Communist Party of Australia years are entered as disclosure and counter-case -- a constructed dharma that kept the power to unbuild itself.

**Part 7** asks what survives the loss of grounding, installation, and a cooperating world -- while conceding to Taylor and Asad that "the secular" is a construction site, not a void, and that every dharma now sits the same entrance exam. No ethic reaches bedrock; the honest question is whether it is grounded well enough to live by. Installation sometimes survives translation (Batchelor, Stoicism-to-CBT, Hadot's "recovery") and sometimes hollows out (McMindfulness). Sumarah, revisited, is the calibrated near-proof: practice without priesthood and conviction without creed were solved in Java decades ago, but no tradition has yet removed its own God from the inside. The new terrain -- an industrialised attention economy engineered against the gathering of a self -- is genuinely without precedent.

**Part 8** takes up conscious construction at two scales: a planetary ethic (thin overlapping floor over thick plurality, against China's effective and authoritarian singular version) and the ethics poured into machines (orthogonality, "whose values", value lock-in). Its central danger has a name -- **the flattening** -- and the book turns that charge on itself: the RAG corpus behind these essays is a majority-seeking instrument that already caused one documented error. The answer to relativism is a thin floor (Curry's cooperative rules; Nussbaum and Sen's capabilities) beneath thick diversity, and the criterion is the root word cashed out: a dharma is judged by whether it **holds without crushing**. The book's own hero case is then run through that test and does not pass cleanly -- the Sikep refusal of schooling, and the patriarchal household -- with the later self-correction from inside recorded as what the floor is actually for.

**Part 9** states in miniature, without argument, what the eight parts establish.

An **appendix**, *Dharmas: The Better Ones*, closes the book by promoting Part 8's verdict to a standard: two tests, one substantive -- a dharma holds without crushing -- and one procedural, the keystone -- dharmas are better to the extent they remain revisable by the people bound by them. The first test asks what a dharma does; the second asks who holds the pen.

## Method

Two deliberately separated stages: heavily sourced research notes first, essays written only from those notes afterwards -- the reverse of reaching for a supporting quotation once the argument is decided. Sourcing discipline is modelled explicitly on *isnad*, the chain of transmission. AI performed retrieval, note-assembly and citation-checking; judgement, structure and prose are the author's. Research notes are published openly.

## Stance

Secular, evolutionary, broadly Western and academically inflected -- declared as a partial lens rather than a neutral instrument. Written for an intelligent general reader: no lineage, no authority claimed, no practice being sold.
