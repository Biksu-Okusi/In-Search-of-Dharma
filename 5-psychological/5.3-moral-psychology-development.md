# 5.3 Moral Psychology & Development

**Question:** What does moral psychology (e.g. Haidt's moral foundations, Kohlberg's stages of moral
development) and the WEIRD-sample critique tell us about how dharmic intuitions form and vary across
populations?

## Synthesis

Moral psychology reframes a dharma as a **cultural recipe written over a small set of evolved moral
intuitions**. Haidt's Moral Foundations Theory holds that judgment is *intuition-first* — "the
emotional dog and its rational tail," with reasoning largely post-hoc — and rests on a handful of
innate-but-tunable "foundations" or "taste buds of the moral mind": care, fairness, liberty, loyalty,
authority, sanctity (and likely more) [S1][S2][S7]. Cultures *tune* these differently, so different
dharmas are different *settings* over a shared palette — and, tellingly, **WEIRD-liberal morality
fires mainly on care + fairness + liberty, while most of humanity's traditions weight the "binding"
foundations (loyalty, authority, sanctity) just as heavily** [S1][S3]. Kohlberg's developmental
ladder (pre-conventional → conventional → post-conventional) captures real sequencing but is dated
and skewed — Gilligan showed it was androcentric (justice over care), and cross-cultural work shows
the stages don't progress inevitably [S4][S6]. The **WEIRD critique** (Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan) is
the hammer: behavioural science built "human nature" from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich,
Democratic samples that are in fact global *outliers* on fairness, cooperation and moral reasoning
[S5] — so dharmic intuitions **vary systematically across populations**, and the project's own thin
floor (suffering-reduction = the care foundation) may be a WEIRD-parochial projection
([[3.4-relativism-universalism]], [[7.2-appropriation]]). On *formation*: intuitions come first, but
they are not fixed — they are "the end products of learning," our guts trained by past reasoning
([[5.4-ritual-habituation]]). Second pass (2026-08-27): read in the primaries, the foundations are
weaker as *architecture* than as *vocabulary* — Suhler and Churchland find that what constrains
moral development "appears neither to involve modules nor to amount to innateness in any
explanatorily useful sense" [S22], no theorised MFQ model fits [S14], and two rival structural
accounts now sit in the corpus: the Theory of Dyadic Morality, on which every moral judgment runs
through a template of perceived harm and "harmless wrongs" are an illusion [S19], and
morality-as-cooperation, on which even puritanical purity norms are cooperation devices [S13][S20].
The adjudication [[1.3-boundary-definition]] handed to this note is given below; its upshot is that
the *dharma* concept needs the plural vocabulary and can leave the reduction contest open [A].

## Findings

### Haidt: morality is intuition-first, built on tunable foundations
Haidt's two core claims. First, the **social intuitionist model**: most moral judgment is fast,
affective intuition, with conscious reasoning arriving afterward as justification — the "emotional
dog and its rational tail": "moral reasoning does not cause moral judgment; rather, moral reasoning
is usually a post hoc construction, generated after a judgment has been reached" [S7]. Haidt's own
2001 hedge is worth keeping: because much of the evidence came from outside the moral domain, the
model was "presented here only as a plausible alternative approach to moral psychology, not as an
established fact" [S7]. Second, **Moral Foundations Theory**: humans share a few evolved
"foundations" — care/harm, fairness/cheating, liberty/oppression, loyalty/betrayal,
authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation — which Haidt calls "the evolved taste buds of the moral
mind," explicitly open-ended ("how many foundations are there?... property, honesty, [dislike of]
wastefulness" are further candidates; "evolution didn't give a damn about parsimony") [S2]. The
theory's own statement of its nativism is careful: "Innate means organized in advance of experience,"
explicitly *not* "hardwired or insensitive to environmental influences," with Marcus's metaphor that
"Nature provides a first draft, which experience then revises" — and Liberty/oppression is offered
only as a candidate "sixth foundation" awaiting criteria of "foundationhood" [S18]. A dharma,
on this view, is a *culturally specific cuisine* built from universal tastebuds — which is exactly
why dharmas can be both recognisably moral and wildly variable [S2].

### The "binding" foundations and the WEIRD-liberal split
Both experimental and real-world data show **liberals weight the first three foundations (care,
fairness, liberty); conservatives weight all six**, especially the "binding" trio loyalty, authority,
sanctity [S1][S3]. These binding foundations have a real *function* — they "increase the benefits of
living in [an] in-group" (family, tribe, religion) — and a real *cost*: they license demonising
out-groups and dissenters [S1]. There is a live interpretive fight: conservatives read liberals as
"morally impoverished, with half their foundations atrophied"; Jost and Greene read liberals as
*refined*, having "jettisoned the less important, more historically damaging" foundations [S3]. That
this fight is unresolved is itself the point — *which* foundations a dharma elevates is a value
choice, not a fact (the is/ought wall, [[3.3-grounding-problem]]).

### Kohlberg's stages — and why they fracture
Building on Piaget's developmental sequences, Kohlberg proposed six stages of moral *reasoning*,
grouped as **pre-conventional** (self-interest: avoid punishment, seek reward), **conventional**
(conformity to group norms, law and order), and **post-conventional** (universal principles that can
*override* law — the realm of principled civil disobedience) [S4]. Three serious critiques: (1)
**gender bias** — Kohlberg's 1960s samples were largely male, and Gilligan's *In a Different Voice*
showed the scoring ranked women's more relational, care-based reasoning as "less mature," when it is a
*different, equally valid orientation* ("ethic of care" vs "ethic of justice") [S6]; (2)
**cultural bias** — Snarey's meta-analysis of 45 cross-cultural Kohlbergian studies across 27 cultures
(1969–1984) found a genuine basic-stage trend but that the *highest* (post-conventional) stages are
culturally specific to Western, urban, educated populations — so the stages do *not* progress in an
inevitable universal sequence, and "principled" reasoning skews WEIRD [S10] — Henrich, Heine and
Norenzayan's own example is Kuwait University, where faculty "scored lower on Kohlberg's schemes than
the typical norms for Western adults, and the elder faculty there scored no higher than the younger
ones, contrary to Western patterns" [S5]; (3) the **intuitionist
challenge** — Haidt argues Kohlberg over-rationalised morality, mistaking post-hoc justification for
the engine [S7], though the developmental field itself "remains dominated by rationalist perspectives"
[S11], so this dispute is live rather than settled in Haidt's favour. (Later work qualifies Gilligan:
both orientations appear across genders, with context and culture outweighing sex [S6].) These two
critiques share a single named methodological error — the **deficit assumption**, by which non-Western
or female difference is read as developmental *lack* rather than valid variation [S12].

### The WEIRD critique: the variation is systematic
Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan (2010) showed that behavioural scientists routinely generalise about
"human" psychology from **WEIRD** samples that are, across domain after domain — fairness and
cooperation (Ultimatum/Dictator games), analytic vs holistic cognition, self-concept, *moral
reasoning* — among the *least* representative humans on Earth, "frequent outliers"; a 2003–2007
survey of top psychology journals found 68% of subjects from the United States and "a full 96%"
from Western industrialised countries [S5]. The paper also names the dharma-relevant finding
directly: typical Western subjects "rely principally on justice- and harm/care-based principles,"
whereas "non-Western adults and Western religious conservatives rely on a wider range of moral
principles" — Shweder's ethics of community and divinity [S5]. The upshot for dharma: cross-population moral variation is not noise but
*structured*. The structuring variable proposed by Schulz, Bahrami-Rad, Beauchamp & Henrich (2019) is
**kin intensity**: "combining data on 24 psychological outcomes with historical measures of both
Church exposure and kinship," longer historical exposure to the Western Church's Marriage and
Family Program — the bans that began with alliance-sustaining practices such as levirate marriage
before the Church "became obsessed with incest" — predicts weaker kin-based institutions, and with them
greater individualism, lower conformity and more impersonal prosociality [S8] — the argument Henrich
later develops book-length [S9] ([[2.2-urban-complexity]]). It is an observational finding leaning on
instrumented historical exposure, not an experiment, and it is contested; but it means "how dharmic
intuitions vary across populations" has a candidate answer with a mechanism — variation along an
autonomy-vs-embeddedness axis that tracks kinship structure [A].

### How dharmic intuitions *form*
Three mechanisms, in tension and combination:
- **Intuition-first, reason-after** (Haidt) — the dharma's doctrines often *rationalise* pre-existing
  gut intuitions rather than generating them [S7].
- **Innate "first draft," revised by culture** — the foundations are "organised in advance of
  experience" but *tuned* by upbringing; the moral/conventional distinction children draw early
  ([[1.3-boundary-definition]]) shows some structure is precocious [A].
- **Intuitions are learned automaticities** — crucially, Sapolsky (with Woodward & Allman) insists
  moral intuitions "are neither primordial nor reflexively primitive... they are the end products of
  learning." Our gut conviction that slavery, child labour or cruelty is wrong is *recent*, built by
  "the fierce moral reasoning and activism of those who came before us" — "our guts learn their
  intuitions" [S3]. This dissolves the false either/or of innate-vs-cultural and grounds dharma's
  *training* function ([[5.4-ritual-habituation]]).

### How they *vary* — Shweder's three ethics and the Golden Rule's underdetermination
Shweder's cross-cultural framework — the ethics of **autonomy** (harm, fairness), **community**
(loyalty, authority), and **divinity** (purity, sanctity) — maps directly onto Haidt's foundations and
names the main axes of variation [S3]. Against this variety there *are* near-universals (condemnation
of some murder/theft; the Golden Rule), but the Golden Rule is famously underdetermined — it founders
on the masochist who says "beat me" — so the durable universal is a *generalised reciprocity* (give
others the concern you'd want in their place) rather than a fixed content [S3]. Shermer's
"provisional morality" captures the working stance: treat widespread norms as presumptively important,
"but watch your wallet" [S3].

### Harm as the moral essence? The adjudication handed over by 1.3
[[1.3-boundary-definition]] named the Theory of Dyadic Morality (TDM) as the monothetic rival to its
family-resemblance treatment of the moral and left the adjudication here. TDM's claim: moral
judgment runs on "a harm-based cognitive template" — an intentional agent damaging a vulnerable
patient — that "functions intuitively and is rooted in innate and evolved processes"; it "argues
against intuitive harmless wrongs and modular 'foundations,' but embraces moral pluralism through
varieties of values and the flexibility of perceived harm" [S19]. Political and cultural difference
is redescribed as difference in *what is seen as harmful*: "conservatives moralize patriotism and
chastity because they view their violation as harmful — unlike liberals," so loyalty, purity and
social order are "transformations" of harm rather than foundations beside it; "the essence of
morality is also unity in variety — cognitive unity in the variety of perceived harm" [S19]. The
purity review is the same programme's demolition work: purity's themes "are scaffolded on cultural
understandings of harm — the broad, pluralistic harm outlined by the Theory of Dyadic Morality"
[S15], and the meta-analysis of fifty disgust-induction studies finds a "small amplification effect
of disgust (d = .11)" that, once publication bias is accounted for, "disappears entirely (d = −.01)"
[S21] — the disgust-based sanctity foundation loses its distinctive mechanism.

A third claimant then complicates the two-way contest. Morality-as-cooperation had the same
"harmless wrongs" problem from the other side — if morality is cooperation, why is lust, gluttony or
dance condemned? — and Fitouchi, André and Baumard answer that "puritanical morality is no exception
to the cooperative function of moral cognition": it "consists of preemptive moralizations of
behaviors perceived as risk factors for uncooperative self-control failures," so that "explaining
puritanical norms does not require adding mechanisms unrelated to cooperation in our models of the
moral mind" [S20]. So the corpus now holds three structural accounts of where the many moral concerns
come from: innate modular foundations (MFT), a single harm template with culturally variable inputs
(TDM), and a family of cooperation problems (MAC). TDM and MAC *agree* against MFT's modules and
against genuinely harmless wrongs; they disagree on whether the reduction base is perceived harm or
cooperation. Verdict for this project [A]: the contest need not be settled to write about dharmas.
What 1.3 wanted from a boundary criterion was a way of seeing *svadharma* — role-indexed duty that a
harm template only reaches by stipulating the harm of dereliction — and MAC's family, deference and
group facets carry that better than a dyad does; but TDM supplies the corrective that the
*currency* in which any dharma argues its case, inside and outside, is perceived harm, which is why
dharmic disputes so rarely present as disputes about values at all. The working stance stands:
foundations as vocabulary, MAC as the better-supported structure, TDM as the reminder of the currency.

### Greene's two tragedies: why dharmas *clash*
Joshua Greene's *Moral Tribes* distinguishes the **tragedy of the commons** (Me vs Us — selfishness
within a group, which dharmas solve) from the **tragedy of commonsense morality** (Us vs Them — two
groups with *internally coherent but mutually incompatible* moral systems) [S3]. The second is the
dharma-clash problem of [[3.4-relativism-universalism]]: each side experiences its parochial intuition
as a self-evident "right," when "right" is often "enough post-hoc rationalisation slathered on a
shapeless, self-serving, parochial moral intuition" [S3].

## Tensions, gaps & corpus bias

- **The reflexive WEIRD bite (the headline).** The secular-dharma corpus is a WEIRD-liberal product,
  and it *shows*: a corpus essay proposes "increasing flourishing and reducing harm" as morality's
  *ultimate goal* [S1] — i.e. the **care/harm** foundation, the WEIRD-liberal 2–3-foundation palette —
  and treats the binding foundations (loyalty/authority/sanctity) chiefly as pathology (Pinker: the
  source of "most of the violence in human history") [S1]. MFT + WEIRD give empirical teeth to
  [[3.4-relativism-universalism]]'s worry: the project's thin floor may be a parochial projection, and
  "non-privileging" requires seeing the *function* of the binding foundations most dharmas rely on, not
  only their dangers ([[7.2-appropriation]]).
- **The theories themselves are contested.** Kohlberg's stage model is dated; **MFT's foundation count
  and even its innateness are unsettled** — Haidt himself asks "how many foundations are there?" [S2],
  Suhler and Churchland's critique, now in the corpus, holds that "innateness and modularity figure
  centrally in Haidt's account, but terminological and conceptual problems foster confusion and
  ambiguities," that the foundation count and taxonomy "appear contrived," and that whatever
  constrains moral development "appears neither to involve modules nor to amount to innateness in
  any explanatorily useful sense" [S22] — MFT's 2013 restatement answers by defining innate as
  "organized in advance of experience" and disowning the hardwired reading [S18]. The psychometric
  evidence is worse than the rhetorical question implies: in a latent-factor study of adolescents "only three of these foundations found psychometric support consistent with
  MFT" (autonomy, loyalty, animal physical harm), while adult work splits the care foundation three
  ways [S11]. The liberal/conservative result is partly WEIRD-bound. Claims here are "best current
  maps," not settled fact.

  ▲ **Sharpened 2026-07-28 — and it reaches across the series.** Four results now bear directly on how
  much weight MFT can carry. (a) **The head-to-head.** Curry, Jones Chesters & Van Lissa built a rival
  instrument, the MAC-Q, and tested it against the MFQ over four studies: "the results support the
  MAC-Q's seven-factor model of morality, but not the MFQ's five-factor model," so morality-as-cooperation
  "emerges as superior" on this comparison [S13]. (b) **The structural test.** Across five studies
  totalling N = 11,496, Zakharin & Bates "tested previously theorised models, confirming none provide
  adequate fit"; they that *none* of the previously theorised MFQ models
  then construct and replicate a well-fitting model in which care and
  fairness survive intact but the binding foundations are re-cut — arriving, independently, at **seven**
  factors [S14]. (c) **Purity fails as a construct.** Gray et al.'s systematic review finds ~9 distinct
  understandings of purity, held together only by not being obvious interpersonal harm; it "lacks
  convergent and divergent validity," is confounded with politics, religion and perceived harm, and
  "cannot be a distinct basis of moral judgment," being better read as "a loose set of themes in moral
  rhetoric" [S15]. (d) **MFT's own authors have re-specified it.** Atari, Haidt, Graham and colleagues
  split Fairness into Equality and Proportionality on data from 25 populations, yielding a
  six-foundation MFQ-2 [S16].

  **The consequence for this project is a live internal inconsistency, not just a caveat.**
  [[1.2-universal-patterns]] and Part 1 rest on Curry's morality-as-cooperation; this note and Part 5
  present Haidt's foundations as the working map of the moral mind. Curry's own head-to-head is a direct
  test between them, and it goes against MFT [S13]. Stage 2 cannot keep both framings at full strength.
  The defensible reconciliation: treat the foundations as a **vocabulary of moral concerns** — vivid,
  teachable, and useful for describing *which* concerns a dharma elevates — while treating MAC as the
  better-supported **structural** account of where those concerns come from. Note that MAC's seven and
  Zakharin & Bates's seven were reached by wholly different routes, which is the most interesting fact
  in this literature and belongs in Part 5 rather than a footnote.
- **Moral dumbfounding, the flagship demonstration, does not survive replication.** Haidt's "Julie and
  Mark" incest vignette is the standard evidence that judgment is intuition-first and reasoning merely
  post-hoc. Royzman, Kim & Leeman re-ran it with the confounds removed and report that their procedure
  "yielded a dumbfounding estimate of about 0" [S17]. The mechanism matters more than the number:
  subjects were not reasoning-free, they simply **failed to believe the stipulation** that the act was
  harm-free, and so held "excellent reasons to disapprove." Participants manoeuvred into looking
  dumbfounded subsequently recanted. Stanley, Yin and Sinnott-Armstrong's follow-up gives the
  reason-based reading its numbers: 80% of participants initially judged Julie and Mark's act not OK,
  and after failing to justify the judgment "only 32% of participants ended up claiming that the
  behavior was OK" — the residue is read as commitment to unstated harm-related reasons, not to
  reasonless intuition [S23]. Haidt's original paper already reports the Haidt–Hersh finding that
  "affective reactions were good predictors of judgment, whereas perceptions of harmfulness were
  not" [S7]; the two literatures disagree about what the subjects believed, not about what they said.
  ▲ This does not refute intuitionism, which has other support
  (the WEIRD and automaticity findings above stand), but the vignette should no longer be presented as
  proof that reasoning is post-hoc — in Part 5 or anywhere else.
- **Innate modules vs learned automaticity.** Haidt's "taste buds" imply innate modules; Sapolsky's
  "guts learn their intuitions" [S3] implies learned automaticity. The truth is probably gene–culture
  coevolutionary (Haidt's "tweak settings and timings" in the Holocene [S2]) — but the note should not
  reify the foundations as fixed hardware.
- **Describing ≠ justifying.** None of this tells us which intuitions are *right*; intuitionism can
  slide toward relativism, and adaptiveness grounds no *ought* ([[3.3-grounding-problem]]).
- **Second-pass corpus bias (2026-08-27).** The 2026-08 expansion built two shelves for this
  question — `1-moral-foundations/` and `2-mac-vs-mft/` — and the note is now anchored in the
  primaries for Haidt 2001 and 2013, Henrich et al 2010, Schulz et al 2019, Kline et al 2018, the
  four MFT stress-tests and both dumbfounding re-analyses. The shelf's lean is visible: it is
  *stress-test heavy* (five critiques of MFT, one restatement) and carries no defence of MFT beyond
  its authors' own; Haidt's *Righteous Mind* and Greene's *Moral Tribes* are still read through
  Sapolsky's *Behave*, and Shweder only through Sapolsky and Henrich. Kohlberg, Gilligan and Snarey
  remain wholly external — the corpus holds no developmental primary. Suhler & Churchland is a
  tesseract scan (author name garbled to "Subler" in the OCR); quotations were checked against the
  chunks.
- **Cited via popular syntheses — the Kohlberg spine now firmer.** Shweder and Greene
  are still read through Sapolsky's *Behave* and corpus essays. But the two claims that previously
  rested on an unnamed Sapolsky *lecture transcript* — the cross-cultural non-inevitability of the
  stages and the Gilligan gender critique — now carry primary anchors: Snarey's meta-analysis [S10],
  Gilligan (1982) direct [S6], and Kline et al.'s developmental-WEIRD account [S12]. The corpus itself
  holds no primary literature on Kohlberg, Gilligan or cross-cultural stage testing, so this repair was
  necessarily an external pass, not a corpus one.

## Open threads (non-registry)

- **Reader-raised seed (Rupert, correspondence, June 2026):** a reader described his late
  brother's rapid, whole-hearted swings between worldviews — atheist, then committed Christian,
  then another creed — each time declaring the previous "one true path" wholly mistaken and the
  new one the real thing. It is a vivid single case of *moral/worldview lability*: how plastic
  are an individual's dharmic commitments across a lifetime, and what distinguishes a lifelong
  "belief-hopper" from a steadfast adherent? Connects to conversion/deconversion research and to
  the mechanism-and-plasticity thread parked in [[5.1-neurological-basis]]. Not yet researched —
  parked here so it is not lost. [A]
